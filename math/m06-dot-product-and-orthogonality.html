01

Why This Lesson Exists

You already know how to store information in vectors.

Now we need a way to combine two vectors into one scalar and reason about how they point relative to one another.

The central operation is:

\[ \boxed{ x^Ty=\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_iy_i } \]

This is the dot product, also called the standard inner product on \(\mathbb R^d\).

It appears directly in:

\[ \boxed{ w^Tx+b } \]

which is the core weighted-sum operation behind linear regression, logistic regression and a basic neuron.

02

One-Minute Intuition

👨‍🏫 Teacher Intuition — Bilingual Mental Model
Dot product ka geometric matlab hai **Alignment**. Do vectors kitna ek hi direction mein ishara kar rahe hain? Agar angle $90^\circ$ hai, toh $\cos(90^\circ) = 0$, yaani dot product zero hai—dono vectors orthogonal hain. AI mein jab hum neuron ka output nikalte hain ($w^T x$), hum asal mein feature vector $x$ ka weight vector $w$ ke saath alignment hi calculate kar rahe hote hain!

Imagine two arrows.

The dot product asks:

How strongly are these arrows aligned?

Roughly:

same direction
→ positive dot product

perpendicular
→ zero dot product

opposite direction
→ negative dot product

The magnitude also depends on the lengths of the vectors.

So:

\[ x^Ty \]

contains information about:

length of x
+
length of y
+
angle between them
03

Real-World Analogy

Suppose a student has:

\[ x= \begin{bmatrix} hours\\ attendance \end{bmatrix} \]

and a model has:

\[ w= \begin{bmatrix} importance\ of\ hours\\ importance\ of\ attendance \end{bmatrix}. \]

Then:

\[ w^Tx \]

computes:

\[ w_1x_1+w_2x_2. \]

It is literally:

feature value × feature weight, added across features.

This is why the dot product is so central to ML.

04

Dot Product Definition

For:

\[ x= \begin{bmatrix} x_1\\ x_2\\ \vdots\\ x_d \end{bmatrix}, \qquad y= \begin{bmatrix} y_1\\ y_2\\ \vdots\\ y_d \end{bmatrix}, \]

the standard inner product is:

\[ \boxed{ \langle x,y\rangle = x^Ty = \sum_{i=1}^{d}x_iy_i } \]

Calculate by:

multiply matching components
↓
add all products
05

Worked Example — Basic Dot Product

Let:

\[ x= \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad y= \begin{bmatrix} 3\\ 4 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Then:

\[ x^Ty = 1(3)+2(4) = 3+8 = \boxed{11}. \]

Python & NumPy From-Scratch Verification

PYTHON
import numpy as np

a = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 3.0])
b = np.array([4.0, -1.0, 2.0])

dot_prod = np.dot(a, b)  # 1(4) + 2(-1) + 3(2) = 4 - 2 + 6 = 8

norm_a = np.linalg.norm(a)
norm_b = np.linalg.norm(b)
cos_sim = dot_prod / (norm_a * norm_b)

print("Dot product a · b:", dot_prod)
print("Cosine similarity:", np.round(cos_sim, 4))
print("Angle in degrees:", np.round(np.degrees(np.arccos(cos_sim)), 2))
06

Dot Product as Matrix Multiplication

A column vector:

\[ x\in\mathbb R^d \]

has shape:

\[ d\times1. \]

Its transpose:

\[ x^T \]

has shape:

\[ 1\times d. \]

Therefore:

\[ x^Ty \]

has shape:

\[ (1\times d)(d\times1) = 1\times1. \]

So the result is a scalar.

Dimension rule

\[ \boxed{ \text{inner dimensions must match} } \]

The outside dimensions become the result shape.

07

Dot Product in Linear Regression

A linear model is:

\[ \boxed{ \hat y=w^Tx+b } \]

Expanding:

\[ w^Tx = w_1x_1+w_2x_2+\cdots+w_dx_d. \]

So:

\[ \boxed{ \hat y= w_1x_1+w_2x_2+\cdots+w_dx_d+b } \]

This is the mathematical form of:

“Take a weighted combination of the input features, then add the intercept/bias.”

08

Dot Product as Weighted Sum

This is one of the most important interpretations.

If:

\[ w= \begin{bmatrix} 2\\ -1\\ 3 \end{bmatrix} \]

and:

\[ x= \begin{bmatrix} 4\\ 5\\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \]

then:

\[ w^Tx = 2(4)-1(5)+3(2) = 8-5+6 = 9. \]

Each feature contributes:

x₁ × w₁
x₂ × w₂
x₃ × w₃

and those contributions are added.

09

Dot Product and Angle

For nonzero vectors:

\[ \boxed{ x^Ty=\|x\|_2\|y\|_2\cos\theta } \]

where \(\theta\) is the angle between them.

Therefore:

\[ \boxed{ \cos\theta= \frac{x^Ty}{\|x\|_2\|y\|_2} } \]

This connects algebra directly to geometry.

10

Interpreting the Sign

For nonzero vectors:

Acute angle

If:

\[ 0^\circ\lt \theta\lt 90^\circ, \]

then:

\[ \cos\theta\gt 0 \]

and therefore:

\[ x^Ty\gt 0. \]

Right angle

If:

\[ \theta=90^\circ, \]

then:

\[ \cos\theta=0 \]

and:

\[ \boxed{x^Ty=0}. \]

Obtuse angle

If:

\[ 90^\circ\lt \theta\lt 180^\circ, \]

then:

\[ \cos\theta\lt 0 \]

and:

\[ x^Ty\lt 0. \]

11

Orthogonality

⚠️ University Exam Trap & Common Misconception
**Dot Product vs Matrix Outer Product Trap:** Dot product of two vectors $u, v \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is a SCALAR ($u^T v = \sum u_i v_i$). The outer product $u v^T$ is a $d \times d$ MATRIX! Mixing these two up in written derivations is guaranteed to lose full marks.

Two vectors are orthogonal if their inner product is zero:

\[ \boxed{ x\perp y \iff x^Ty=0 } \]

For nonzero vectors this corresponds to a \(90^\circ\) angle.

Example

\[ x= \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad y= \begin{bmatrix} 2\\ -1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Then:

\[ x^Ty=1(2)+2(-1)=0. \]

Therefore:

\[ \boxed{x\perp y}. \]
12

Why Orthogonality Matters

⚠️ University Exam Trap & Common Misconception
**Dot Product vs Matrix Outer Product Trap:** Dot product of two vectors $u, v \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is a SCALAR ($u^T v = \sum u_i v_i$). The outer product $u v^T$ is a $d \times d$ MATRIX! Mixing these two up in written derivations is guaranteed to lose full marks.

Orthogonality means the vectors have zero inner product.

This is useful because orthogonal components do not contribute to each other's dot-product alignment.

It also simplifies:

coordinates
projections
basis representations
geometry
optimization reasoning

and provides a clean structure for mathematical representations.

13

Linear Independence

A set:

\[ \{x_1,\ldots,x_k\} \]

is linearly independent if the only way to obtain the zero vector from a linear combination is to use all-zero coefficients:

\[ \boxed{ a_1x_1+\cdots+a_kx_k=0 \Rightarrow a_1=\cdots=a_k=0. } \]

If a non-zero coefficient combination gives zero, the vectors are dependent.

14

Intuition: Independence = No Redundancy

Suppose:

\[ x_1= \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad x_2= \begin{bmatrix} 2\\ 4 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Clearly:

\[ x_2=2x_1. \]

So \(x_2\) contributes no genuinely new direction.

The pair is linearly dependent.

Mental model

independent
→ genuinely new directions

dependent
→ at least one vector can be reconstructed from others

This is the same interpretation given in the supplied lecture: dependence corresponds to redundancy in representation.

15

Independence Test with Equations

Suppose:

\[ x_1= \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad x_2= \begin{bmatrix} 0\\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Assume:

\[ a_1x_1+a_2x_2=0. \]

Then:

\[ a_1 \begin{bmatrix} 1\\0 \end{bmatrix} + a_2 \begin{bmatrix} 0\\1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a_1\\a_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0\\0 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Therefore:

\[ a_1=0,\qquad a_2=0. \]

Hence the vectors are linearly independent.

16

Important Warning — Pairwise vs Whole-Set Independence

Consider:

\[ v_1= \begin{bmatrix} 1\\0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad v_2= \begin{bmatrix} 0\\1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad v_3= \begin{bmatrix} 1\\1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Every pair is linearly independent.

But:

\[ \boxed{ v_3=v_1+v_2 } \]

so the three together are dependent.

Therefore:

\[ \boxed{ \text{pairwise independence does not imply independence of the entire set.} } \]

This exact distinction is emphasized in the supplied Lecture04 example.

17

Span

Given:

\[ S=\{x_1,\ldots,x_k\}, \]

the span is the set of every possible linear combination:

\[ \boxed{ span(S) = \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^{k}a_ix_i: a_i\in\mathbb R \right\} } \]

In words:

Everything you can construct using the given vectors and arbitrary real coefficients.

18

Span as “Reachable Directions”

Think:

starting vectors
+
arbitrary scaling
+
arbitrary addition
=
everything reachable

Example:

\[ x= \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 0 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Its span is:

\[ \left\{ a \begin{bmatrix} 1\\0 \end{bmatrix} :a\in\mathbb R \right\}. \]

This is the entire \(x\)-axis.

19

Span of Two Standard Basis Vectors

Let:

\[ e_1= \begin{bmatrix} 1\\0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad e_2= \begin{bmatrix} 0\\1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Any vector:

\[ \begin{bmatrix} x\\y \end{bmatrix} \]

can be written:

\[ x e_1+y e_2. \]

Therefore:

\[ \boxed{ span\{e_1,e_2\}=\mathbb R^2 } \]

This example comes directly from the supplied lecture's treatment of span.

20

Testing Whether a Vector Lies in a Span

Suppose:

\[ x_1= \begin{bmatrix} 1\\0\\1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad x_2= \begin{bmatrix} 0\\1\\1 \end{bmatrix} \]

and:

\[ z= \begin{bmatrix} 1\\1\\2 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Ask whether:

\[ z\in span\{x_1,x_2\}. \]

We need:

\[ a x_1+b x_2=z. \]

Therefore:

\[ a \begin{bmatrix} 1\\0\\1 \end{bmatrix} + b \begin{bmatrix} 0\\1\\1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a\\b\\a+b \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1\\1\\2 \end{bmatrix}. \]

So:

\[ a=1,\quad b=1, \]

and:

\[ a+b=2. \]

Everything matches.

Therefore:

\[ \boxed{ z\in span\{x_1,x_2\}. } \]

This is the same structure as the supplied Lecture04 span example.

21

Basis

A basis is a set of vectors that satisfies two conditions:

\[ \boxed{ \text{basis} = \text{spans the space} + \text{is linearly independent} } \]

For a vector space \(U\):

\[ U=span\{b_1,\ldots,b_m\} \]

and the vectors are linearly independent.

The supplied lecture states exactly these two conditions and notes that every vector then has a unique coordinate representation in that basis.

22

Why Basis Matters

A basis is like a coordinate language.

For the standard basis of \(\mathbb R^2\):

\[ e_1= \begin{bmatrix} 1\\0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad e_2= \begin{bmatrix} 0\\1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

A vector:

\[ x= \begin{bmatrix} 3\\2 \end{bmatrix} \]

has coordinates:

\[ (3,2) \]

in that basis.

Change the basis and the coordinate numbers can change, even though the underlying vector does not.

23

Unique Representation

If:

\[ \{b_1,\ldots,b_m\} \]

is a basis, then every vector \(x\) in the space can be represented uniquely:

\[ \boxed{ x=\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_ib_i. } \]

Why unique?

Suppose there were two representations:

\[ x=\sum_i c_ib_i \]

and:

\[ x=\sum_i a_ib_i. \]

Subtract:

\[ 0=\sum_i(c_i-a_i)b_i. \]

Since the basis vectors are linearly independent:

\[ c_i-a_i=0 \]

for every \(i\).

Hence:

\[ \boxed{ c_i=a_i. } \]

This proof structure is given in Lecture04.

24

Dimension

For a finite-dimensional vector space:

\[ \boxed{ dim(U)=\text{number of vectors in any basis of }U. } \]

Therefore:

\[ dim(\mathbb R^d)=d. \]

In \(\mathbb R^2\), a basis has two vectors.

In \(\mathbb R^3\), a basis has three vectors.

25

Orthonormality

A set is orthonormal when:

orthogonal
+
every vector has unit norm

So for vectors \(b_i\):

\[ \boxed{ b_i^Tb_j= \begin{cases} 1,&i=j\\ 0,&i\ne j \end{cases} } \]

An orthonormal basis is a basis whose vectors are orthonormal.

26

Why Orthonormal Bases Are Special

If:

\[ \{b_1,\ldots,b_d\} \]

is an orthonormal basis, then the coordinate of \(x\) along basis vector \(b_i\) is:

\[ \boxed{ c_i=x^Tb_i. } \]

Therefore:

\[ \boxed{ x=\sum_i(x^Tb_i)b_i. } \]

This is a major simplification.

Instead of solving a general system every time, the inner products directly provide the coordinates.

This is explicitly covered in the supplied lecture.

27

Cosine Similarity

For nonzero vectors:

\[ \boxed{ \cos\theta= \frac{x^Ty}{\|x\|_2\|y\|_2} } \]

This value lies in:

\[ [-1,1]. \]

Interpretation:

close to +1
→ same direction

close to 0
→ near perpendicular

close to −1
→ opposite directions

The supplied Lecture04 specifically notes its use for word embeddings.

28

Example — Cosine Similarity

Let:

\[ x= \begin{bmatrix} 1\\2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad y= \begin{bmatrix} 2\\1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Dot product:

\[ x^Ty=1(2)+2(1)=4. \]

Norms:

\[ \|x\|_2=\sqrt5, \qquad \|y\|_2=\sqrt5. \]

Therefore:

\[ \cos\theta = \frac4{5} = \boxed{0.8}. \]

So the vectors point in reasonably similar directions.

This numerical example is also given in the supplied Lecture04 material.

29

Dot Product vs Cosine Similarity

These are related but different.

Dot product

\[ x^Ty \]

depends on:

direction
+
magnitudes

Cosine similarity

\[ \frac{x^Ty}{\|x\|\|y\|} \]

normalizes away the magnitudes and focuses on angular alignment.

Therefore two vectors can have high dot product because they are both large, while cosine similarity separately measures directional similarity.

30

A Projection Intuition

For a nonzero vector \(u\), the scalar component of \(x\) in the direction of \(u\) is:

\[ \boxed{ \frac{x^Tu}{\|u\|_2} } \]

and the vector projection is:

\[ \boxed{ proj_u(x) = \frac{x^Tu}{u^Tu}u } \]

This follows because \(u^Tu=\|u\|_2^2\).

Projection is useful for understanding:

coordinates
orthogonality
least squares
geometry

It also provides intuition for why dot products appear in linear models.

31

Why \(w^Tx\) Is More Than “Multiply and Add”

There are three useful interpretations:

Algebraic

\[ w^Tx=\sum_iw_ix_i. \]

ML

Weighted combination of features.

Geometric

Related to how \(x\) aligns with \(w\):

\[ w^Tx=\|w\|_2\|x\|_2\cos\theta. \]

Therefore:

large positive
→ strong alignment

near zero
→ weak / orthogonal alignment

negative
→ opposite alignment

This is a powerful bridge between linear algebra and ML.

32

Worked Example — Linear Model Geometry

Let:

\[ w= \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 0 \end{bmatrix}. \]

For:

\[ x= \begin{bmatrix} 5\\ 7 \end{bmatrix}, \]

we get:

\[ w^Tx=5. \]

The model only responds to the first coordinate.

Now take:

\[ x= \begin{bmatrix} 0\\ 7 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Then:

\[ w^Tx=0. \]

The second-direction component is orthogonal to \(w\), so it contributes nothing to \(w^Tx\).

Common Mistakes

⚠️ University Exam Trap & Common Misconception
**Dot Product vs Matrix Outer Product Trap:** Dot product of two vectors $u, v \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is a SCALAR ($u^T v = \sum u_i v_i$). The outer product $u v^T$ is a $d \times d$ MATRIX! Mixing these two up in written derivations is guaranteed to lose full marks.

Mistake 1 — Adding instead of multiplying

Dot product:

\[ x^Ty=\sum_ix_iy_i \]

not:

\[ \sum_i(x_i+y_i). \]

Mistake 2 — Dimension mismatch

Dot product requires matching vector dimensions.

Mistake 3 — Forgetting transpose

For column vectors:

\[ x^Ty \]

is a scalar.

But:

\[ xy \]

is not the same operation.

Mistake 4 — Confusing orthogonal and independent

For nonzero vectors:

\[ \text{orthogonal}\Rightarrow\text{linearly independent} \]

but:

\[ \text{linearly independent}\not\Rightarrow\text{orthogonal}. \]

This exact relationship is stated in Lecture04.

Mistake 5 — Pairwise independence

A whole set can be dependent even when every pair is independent.

Mistake 6 — Dot product equals cosine similarity

Only after normalization:

\[ \cos\theta=\frac{x^Ty}{\|x\|\|y\|}. \]
