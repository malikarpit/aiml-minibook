01

Why This Lesson Exists

Ordinary probability asks:

\[ P(A) \]

But real decisions often come with information.

Examples:

What is the probability a patient has a condition
given a positive test?

What is the probability of fraud
given certain transaction features?

What is the probability of rain
given that the sky is cloudy?

This is:

\[ \boxed{ P(A|B) } \]

Conditional probability is one of the central mathematical ideas behind probabilistic reasoning.

02

One-Minute Intuition

👨‍🏫 Teacher Intuition — Bilingual Mental Model
Bayes Theorem insaan ki soch ka mathematical model hai: "Jab naya saboot (evidence) milta hai, toh purana vishwas (prior) kitna badalna chahiye?" $\text{Posterior} = \frac{\text{Likelihood} \times \text{Prior}}{\text{Evidence}}$. Example: Agar kisi rare bimari ka test positive aa jaye, toh ghabraiye mat—kyunki bimari rare hai (low prior), isiliye zyadatar positive tests false alarms hote hain!

Conditional probability means:

Restrict attention to cases where B is known to have happened, then ask how often A occurs among those cases.

Think of the original sample space as a large room.

Knowing \(B\) happened shrinks your attention to a smaller room:

all possibilities Ω
        ↓
restrict to B
        ↓
ask about A inside B

That is the intuition behind:

\[ P(A|B). \]
03

Definition

If:

\[ P(B)\gt 0, \]

then:

\[ \boxed{ P(A|B) = \frac{P(A\cap B)} {P(B)}. } \]

Read:

probability of \(A\) given \(B\).

The supplied Lecture01 defines conditional probability in exactly this form.

04

Why the Denominator Is \(P(B)\)

Once we know \(B\) occurred, we are no longer considering all of \(\Omega\) equally.

Our new effective reference space is \(B\).

So:

favourable part
→ A ∩ B

total relevant part
→ B

Therefore:

\[ P(A|B) = \frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}. \]

This is the same ratio logic used in many ML metrics, but now the quantities are probabilities of events.

05

Worked Example — Die

graph LR
    A["Prior P(Disease)"] --> C["Bayes Theorem: P(Disease | Positive)"]
    B["Likelihood P(Positive | Disease)"] --> C
    D["Evidence P(Positive) via Total Probability"] --> C
    C --> E["Posterior Probability P(Disease | Positive)"]
    style C fill:#0f172a,stroke:#fbbf24,color:#fbbf24,stroke-width:2px

Roll a fair die.

Let:

\[ A=\{\text{even}\} = \{2,4,6\} \]

and:

\[ B=\{\text{number}\le3\} = \{1,2,3\}. \]

Then:

\[ A\cap B=\{2\}. \]

Therefore:

\[ P(A\cap B)=\frac16. \]

and:

\[ P(B)=\frac36=\frac12. \]

Thus:

\[ P(A|B) = \frac{1/6}{3/6} = \boxed{\frac13}. \]

This example is directly represented in the supplied Lecture01.

Python & NumPy From-Scratch Verification

PYTHON
import numpy as np

prior = 0.001
sensitivity = 0.99
false_positive_rate = 0.05

p_positive = (sensitivity * prior) + (false_positive_rate * (1 - prior))
posterior = (sensitivity * prior) / p_positive

print("Prior Probability of Disease:", prior)
print("Total Probability of Positive Test:", np.round(p_positive, 4))
print("Posterior Probability P(Disease | Positive):", np.round(posterior, 4))
06

Multiplication Rule

From:

\[ P(A|B) = \frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}, \]

multiply both sides by \(P(B)\):

\[ \boxed{ P(A\cap B) = P(B)P(A|B). } \]

By symmetry:

\[ \boxed{ P(A\cap B) = P(A)P(B|A). } \]

These identities are extremely important.

07

Chain Rule

For three events:

\[ \boxed{ P(A\cap B\cap C) = P(A) P(B|A) P(C|A\cap B). } \]

Read:

first A
→ then B given A
→ then C given both A and B

The supplied lecture gives this same chain-rule structure.

08

Why Conditional Probability Is Powerful

It lets us update our reasoning when information arrives.

Without evidence:

\[ P(A). \]

With evidence:

\[ P(A|B). \]

The probability can increase, decrease or remain unchanged depending on how informative \(B\) is.

This is the mathematical structure behind Bayesian updating.

09

Independence

Two events \(A\) and \(B\) are independent if knowing one occurred does not change the probability of the other.

Definition:

\[ \boxed{ P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B). } \]

If:

\[ P(B)\gt 0, \]

this is equivalent to:

\[ \boxed{ P(A|B)=P(A). } \]

The supplied Lecture01 states both forms and the intuition that \(B\) provides no information about \(A\).

10

Independence Does Not Mean “Unrelated in Every Sense”

Independence is a precise probability property.

It means the occurrence of one event does not change the relevant probability of the other under the model.

Do not replace the mathematical condition with vague language such as:

“They have nothing to do with each other.”

Use the formal definition.

print("Posterior Probability P(Disease | Positive):", np.round(posterior, 4))
11

Worked Example — Two Fair Coin Tosses

⚠️ University Exam Trap & Common Misconception
**The Prosecutor's Fallacy:** $P(A | B) \neq P(B | A)$! The probability that a suspect has DNA match given they are guilty $P(\text{Match} | \text{Guilty})$ is very high (~99.9%), but the probability they are guilty given a DNA match $P(\text{Guilty} | \text{Match})$ depends heavily on the population base rate!

Experiment:

Toss a fair coin twice.

Let:

\[ A=\{\text{head on toss 1}\} \]

and:

\[ B=\{\text{head on toss 2}\}. \]

Then:

\[ P(A)=\frac12, \quad P(B)=\frac12. \]

Both occur:

\[ P(A\cap B)=\frac14. \]

And:

\[ P(A)P(B) = \frac12\frac12 = \frac14. \]

Therefore:

\[ \boxed{A\text{ and }B\text{ are independent}.} \]

This is the course lecture's exercise structure.

print("Total Probability of Positive Test:", np.round(p_positive, 4))
print("Posterior Probability P(Disease | Positive):", np.round(posterior, 4))
12

Independence vs Disjointness

⚠️ University Exam Trap & Common Misconception
**The Prosecutor's Fallacy:** $P(A | B) \neq P(B | A)$! The probability that a suspect has DNA match given they are guilty $P(\text{Match} | \text{Guilty})$ is very high (~99.9%), but the probability they are guilty given a DNA match $P(\text{Guilty} | \text{Match})$ depends heavily on the population base rate!

Suppose \(A\) and \(B\) are disjoint:

\[ P(A\cap B)=0. \]

If both have positive probability, then:

\[ P(A)P(B)\gt 0. \]

So they cannot satisfy:

\[ P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B). \]

Therefore:

Two positive-probability mutually exclusive events are not independent.

This distinction is a classic probability trap.

13

Conditional Independence

Events \(A,B\) are conditionally independent given \(C\) when:

\[ \boxed{ P(A\cap B|C) = P(A|C)P(B|C) } \]

for:

\[ P(C)\gt 0. \]

The supplied Lecture01 introduces this exact condition.

Interpretation:

Once \(C\) is known, learning \(A\) gives no additional probability information about \(B\), under the conditional model.

Conditional independence is extremely important in probabilistic graphical models, even though those models are beyond the core DSC-14 syllabus.

14

Law of Total Probability

Suppose:

\[ B_1,B_2,\ldots,B_k \]

form a partition of the sample space:

disjoint
+
cover all possibilities.

Then:

\[ \boxed{ P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} P(A|B_i)P(B_i). } \]

The supplied Lecture01 presents the three-event version and derives it by decomposing \(A\) across the partition.

15

Why Total Probability Works

If the \(B_i\)'s partition the sample space:

\[ A=(A\cap B_1)\cup(A\cap B_2)\cup\cdots\cup(A\cap B_k). \]

These pieces are disjoint.

Therefore:

\[ P(A) = \sum_iP(A\cap B_i). \]

Using the multiplication rule:

\[ P(A\cap B_i) = P(A|B_i)P(B_i). \]

Therefore:

\[ \boxed{ P(A)=\sum_iP(A|B_i)P(B_i). } \]

This is a derivation worth knowing.

16

Partition Intuition

Think:

All possibilities
      ↓
split into cases
B₁, B₂, ..., Bₖ
      ↓
calculate A inside each case
      ↓
weight by probability of each case
      ↓
total P(A)

This is why total probability is sometimes called a weighted average of conditional probabilities.

17

Worked Example — Two Coins

Suppose:

  • Coin 1 produces Heads with probability \(0.9\);
  • Coin 2 produces Heads with probability \(0.1\);
  • choose one coin uniformly;
  • toss the chosen coin once.

Let:

\[ A=\{\text{Head}\}. \]

Cases:

\[ B_1=\{\text{Coin 1}\} \]
\[ B_2=\{\text{Coin 2}\}. \]

Then:

\[ P(B_1)=P(B_2)=\frac12. \]

And:

\[ P(A|B_1)=0.9, \qquad P(A|B_2)=0.1. \]

Total probability:

\[ P(A) = 0.9\left(\frac12\right) + 0.1\left(\frac12\right) \]
\[ =0.45+0.05 = \boxed{0.5}. \]

This is the exact two-coin total-probability example used in Lecture01.

18

Bayes' Rule

Bayes' rule reverses a conditional probability.

Starting from:

\[ P(A|B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)} \]

and:

\[ P(B|A)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(A)}, \]

we obtain:

\[ \boxed{ P(A|B) = \frac{P(B|A)P(A)} {P(B)}. } \]

This is Bayes' rule.

19

Bayes with a Partition

Using total probability:

\[ P(B) = \sum_iP(B|A_i)P(A_i), \]

so:

\[ \boxed{ P(A_k|B) = \frac{ P(B|A_k)P(A_k) }{ \sum_iP(B|A_i)P(A_i) }. } \]

This is one of the most useful forms for exam numericals.

20

Prior, Likelihood, Posterior

For:

\[ P(A|B) = \frac{P(B|A)P(A)} {P(B)}, \]

the standard Bayesian interpretation is:

Prior

\[ \boxed{P(A)} \]

Belief before observing the evidence.

Likelihood

\[ \boxed{P(B|A)} \]

How compatible the observed evidence \(B\) is with hypothesis \(A\).

Posterior

\[ \boxed{P(A|B)} \]

Updated belief after observing \(B\).

Evidence / marginal probability

\[ \boxed{P(B)} \]

Overall probability of observing the evidence.

The supplied Lecture01 labels prior, likelihood and posterior in its Bayes example.

21

Bayes as “Reverse the Direction”

This is the most useful intuition.

You may know:

\[ P(\text{positive test}|\text{disease}) \]

but the real question may be:

\[ P(\text{disease}|\text{positive test}). \]

These are not the same.

Bayes' rule connects them.

22

Bayes Numerical Example — Two Coins

Reuse the previous experiment.

We observed a Head.

Question:

What is the probability Coin 1 was selected?

We want:

\[ P(C_1|H). \]

Bayes:

\[ P(C_1|H) = \frac{ P(H|C_1)P(C_1) }{ P(H) }. \]

Values:

\[ P(H|C_1)=0.9 \]
\[ P(C_1)=0.5 \]

and:

\[ P(H)=0.5. \]

Therefore:

\[ P(C_1|H) = \frac{0.9(0.5)}{0.5} = \boxed{0.9}. \]

The supplied Lecture01 uses this same two-coin Bayes question.

23

Medical-Test Style Example

Suppose:

\[ P(D)=0.01 \]

where \(D\) means disease.

Suppose:

\[ P(+|D)=0.95 \]

and:

\[ P(+|D^c)=0.05. \]

We want:

\[ P(D|+). \]

First compute:

\[ P(+) = P(+|D)P(D) + P(+|D^c)P(D^c). \]

Since:

\[ P(D^c)=0.99, \]

we get:

\[ P(+) = 0.95(0.01)+0.05(0.99) \]
\[ =0.0095+0.0495 \]
\[ =0.059. \]

Then Bayes:

\[ P(D|+) = \frac{0.95(0.01)}{0.059} \approx \boxed{0.161}. \]

So despite a high \(P(+|D)\), the posterior is only about \(16.1\%\) because the condition is rare and false positives contribute substantially.

Lesson

A highly informative-looking test result does not automatically imply a high posterior probability.

26

Conditional Probability and Classification

For classification, one may reason about:

\[ P(y|x). \]

This reads:

probability of class \(y\) given feature vector \(x\).

This is conceptually different from:

\[ P(x|y), \]

which asks:

probability of observing features \(x\) given class \(y\).

Bayes connects them:

\[ \boxed{ P(y|x) = \frac{P(x|y)P(y)} {P(x)}. } \]
27

Likelihood vs Probability of Data

The same expression can be interpreted differently depending on what is considered variable.

For:

\[ P(D|\theta), \]

if \(D\) is fixed and \(\theta\) varies, the expression is viewed as a likelihood function of \(\theta\).

The numerical quantity is still a probability/density under the model, but the role of the variable changes.

This distinction becomes useful later when studying statistical estimation.

28

Conditional Probability Tree

For the two-coin example:

choose coin
├── C₁ (0.5)
│    ├── H (0.9)
│    └── T (0.1)
│
└── C₂ (0.5)
     ├── H (0.1)
     └── T (0.9)

Probability of a path:

\[ \boxed{ P(\text{path}) = P(\text{first branch}) \times P(\text{second branch}\mid\text{first branch}) } \]

For:

\[ C_1\rightarrow H: \]
\[ 0.5\times0.9=0.45. \]

For:

\[ C_2\rightarrow H: \]
\[ 0.5\times0.1=0.05. \]

Then:

\[ P(H)=0.45+0.05=0.5. \]

This tree is often the easiest way to visualize total probability and Bayes.

29

Conditional Probability as a New Probability System

For fixed \(B\) with \(P(B)\gt 0\):

\[ P(A|B) \]

behaves like a probability over the restricted condition \(B\).

For example:

\[ P(B|B)=1. \]

And:

\[ P(B^c|B)=0. \]

This “restricted universe” perspective is extremely useful.

30

Conditional Probability Chain

For events \(A,B,C,D\):

\[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} P(A\cap B\cap C\cap D) ={}&P(A) P(B|A)\\ &\times P(C|A\cap B)\\ &\times P(D|A\cap B\cap C). \end{aligned} } \]

The principle is:

probability of a complete sequence = product of successive conditional probabilities.

This idea later appears naturally in sequence models.

yeh:

hai.

Common Mistakes

⚠️ University Exam Trap & Common Misconception
**The Prosecutor's Fallacy:** $P(A | B) \neq P(B | A)$! The probability that a suspect has DNA match given they are guilty $P(\text{Match} | \text{Guilty})$ is very high (~99.9%), but the probability they are guilty given a DNA match $P(\text{Guilty} | \text{Match})$ depends heavily on the population base rate!

Mistake 1 — Reversing the condition

\[ P(A|B)\ne P(B|A) \]

in general.

Mistake 2 — Forgetting the denominator

\[ P(A|B) = \frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}. \]

Mistake 3 — Treating independence as automatic

You must establish:

\[ P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B). \]

Mistake 4 — Confusing disjointness with independence

Impossible together does not mean independent.

Mistake 5 — Bayes without total probability

When \(P(B)\) is not directly given, derive it using a partition.

Mistake 6 — Confusing prior and posterior

prior
→ before evidence

posterior
→ after evidence

Mistake 7 — Assuming high sensitivity means high posterior probability

The prior prevalence and false-positive behaviour matter.

Formula Cards

Trigger

F-M11-MULT-01 — Multiplication Rule

or:

\[ \boxed{ P(A\cap B)=P(B|A)P(A). } \]

F-M11-CHAIN-01 — Chain Rule

\[ \boxed{ P(A\cap B\cap C) = P(A)P(B|A)P(C|A\cap B) } \]

F-M11-INDEP-01 — Independence

\[ \boxed{ P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B) } \]

Equivalent, when \(P(B)\gt 0\):

\[ \boxed{ P(A|B)=P(A). } \]

F-M11-LOTP-01 — Total Probability

\[ \boxed{ P(A) = \sum_iP(A|B_i)P(B_i) } \]

for a partition \(\{B_i\}\).

F-M11-BAYES-01 — Bayes' Rule

\[ \boxed{ P(A|B) = \frac{P(B|A)P(A)} {P(B)} } \]

F-M11-BAYES-02 — Bayes with Partition

\[ \boxed{ P(A_k|B) = \frac{ P(B|A_k)P(A_k) }{ \sum_iP(B|A_i)P(A_i) } } \]

F-M11-CINDEP-01 — Conditional Independence

\[ \boxed{ P(A\cap B|C) = P(A|C)P(B|C) } \]

for:

\[ P(C)\gt 0. \]
35

Numerical-Solving Protocol

When you see:

“given that…”

immediately ask:

What is A?
What is B?
Do I need P(A|B)?
Do I know P(A∩B)?
Do I know P(B)?

If the question asks for a reversed conditional:

P(A|B)

but gives:

P(B|A)

then think:

\[ \boxed{\text{Bayes}} \]

If the denominator is missing and there is a natural case partition:

\[ \boxed{\text{Total Probability}} \]
