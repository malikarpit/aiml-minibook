01

Why This Lesson Exists

Variance describes one random variable:

How much does \(X\) vary around its mean?

Now consider two variables:

X = study hours
Y = exam score

We may ask:

When \(X\) is above its average, is \(Y\) also usually above its average?

Or:

Do they rise together?
Do they move in opposite directions?
Is there no linear relationship?

This requires a measure of joint variation.

That measure is covariance.

Correlation then rescales covariance into a standardized number between \(-1\) and \(1\).

02

One-Minute Intuition

👨‍🏫 Teacher Intuition — Bilingual Mental Model
Covariance batata hai do features ek sath move karte hain ya opposite: - Agar $X$ badhne par $Y$ bhi badhe, $\text{Cov}(X, Y) \gt 0$. - Agar $X$ badhne par $Y$ ghate, $\text{Cov}(X, Y) \lt 0$. Lekin Covariance units par depend karta hai (jaise rupees $\times$ kilograms). Isiliye hum use standard deviations se divide karke **Pearson Correlation $\rho$** bana dete hain jo hamesha $[-1, +1]$ ke beech lock ho jata hai!

Imagine plotting:

Y
        ↑
        |       •
        |     •
        |   •
        | •
        +------------→ X

As \(X\) rises, \(Y\) also rises.

That suggests positive linear association.

Now:

Y
        ↑
        | •
        |   •
        |     •
        |       •
        +------------→ X

As \(X\) rises, \(Y\) falls.

That suggests negative linear association.

Covariance captures the joint direction of deviation.

Correlation makes that measure scale-free.

03

Start with Deviations

Let:

\[ \mu_X=E[X], \]
\[ \mu_Y=E[Y]. \]

Define deviations:

\[ X-\mu_X \]

and:

\[ Y-\mu_Y. \]

Now multiply them:

\[ (X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y). \]

This product tells us whether their deviations point in the same or opposite directions.

04

Sign of the Deviation Product

Both above their means

\[ X-\mu_X\gt 0 \]

and:

\[ Y-\mu_Y\gt 0. \]

Product:

\[ (+)(+)=(+). \]

Both below their means

\[ (-)(-)=(+). \]

One above and one below

\[ (+)(-) = (-). \]

Therefore:

same side of means
→ positive product

opposite sides
→ negative product

Covariance averages these products.

05

Covariance Definition

The covariance of \(X,Y\) is:

\[ \boxed{ Cov(X,Y) = E[(X-E[X])(Y-E[Y])] } \]

The supplied Lecture02 gives this exact definition. fileciteturn67file3

Using:

\[ \mu_X=E[X],\quad \mu_Y=E[Y], \]

we can write:

\[ \boxed{ Cov(X,Y) = E[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)]. } \]
06

Covariance Computational Formula

Expand the product:

\[ (X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y) \]
\[ = XY-\mu_XY-\mu_YX+\mu_X\mu_Y. \]

Take expectation:

\[ Cov(X,Y) = E[XY] -\mu_XE[Y] -\mu_YE[X] +\mu_X\mu_Y. \]

Because:

\[ E[X]=\mu_X, \]
\[ E[Y]=\mu_Y, \]

we obtain:

\[ \boxed{ Cov(X,Y) = E[XY]-E[X]E[Y]. } \]

The supplied Lecture02 gives this identity directly. fileciteturn67file3

07

Interpretation of Covariance Sign

Positive covariance

\[ Cov(X,Y)\gt 0 \]

means the variables tend to move in the same direction relative to their means.

Negative covariance

\[ Cov(X,Y)\lt 0 \]

means they tend to move in opposite directions relative to their means.

Zero covariance

\[ Cov(X,Y)=0 \]

means there is no linear co-variation captured by covariance.

The supplied Lecture02 defines uncorrelated variables through zero covariance. fileciteturn67file3

08

Covariance Is Not Automatically “Strong” or “Weak”

A major limitation is scale.

Suppose:

X = height in metres

versus:

X = height in centimetres

Changing units changes covariance numerically.

So covariance is useful for direction and joint variation, but raw magnitude is not directly comparable across arbitrary scales.

That motivates correlation.

09

Correlation Coefficient

The Pearson correlation coefficient is:

\[ \boxed{ \rho_{X,Y} = \frac{Cov(X,Y)} {\sqrt{Var(X)Var(Y)}} } \]

provided both variances are positive.

The supplied Lecture02 gives this formula and states:

\[ \boxed{ \rho_{X,Y}\in[-1,1]. } \]

fileciteturn67file3

10

Why Divide by Standard Deviations?

Since:

\[ Std(X)=\sqrt{Var(X)}, \]

and:

\[ Std(Y)=\sqrt{Var(Y)}, \]

correlation can be written:

\[ \boxed{ \rho_{X,Y} = \frac{Cov(X,Y)} {Std(X)Std(Y)}. } \]

This normalizes covariance by the spreads of the two variables.

Conceptually:

covariance
↓
remove scale dependence
↓
correlation

Python & NumPy From-Scratch Verification

PYTHON
import numpy as np

hours = np.array([2.0, 3.0, 5.0, 7.0, 9.0])
marks = np.array([50.0, 58.0, 74.0, 85.0, 96.0])

cov_matrix = np.cov(hours, marks)
corr_matrix = np.corrcoef(hours, marks)

print("Covariance Matrix:\n", np.round(cov_matrix, 2))
print("Sample Covariance Cov(X, Y):", round(cov_matrix[0, 1], 2))
print("Pearson Correlation r:", round(corr_matrix[0, 1], 4))
11

Correlation Range

⚠️ University Exam Trap & Common Misconception
**Zero Correlation does NOT imply Independence:** $\text{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$ means there is no LINEAR relationship. A strong NON-LINEAR relationship can exist with exact zero covariance! Classic counterexample: Let $X \in \{-1, 0, 1\}$ with equal probability, and $Y = X^2$. Here $E[X] = 0$, $E[XY] = E[X^3] = 0 \implies \text{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$, but $Y$ is 100% deterministically dependent on $X$!
\[ \boxed{ -1\le\rho_{X,Y}\le1. } \]

Interpretation:

+1
→ perfect positive linear relation

0
→ no linear correlation

−1
→ perfect negative linear relation

Be careful:

\[ \rho=0 \]

does not mean:

absolutely no relationship of any kind.

It means no linear correlation under Pearson's measure.

The supplied Lecture02 explicitly warns that correlation does not capture nonlinear relationships. fileciteturn67file0

12

Perfect Positive Correlation

⚠️ University Exam Trap & Common Misconception
**Zero Correlation does NOT imply Independence:** $\text{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$ means there is no LINEAR relationship. A strong NON-LINEAR relationship can exist with exact zero covariance! Classic counterexample: Let $X \in \{-1, 0, 1\}$ with equal probability, and $Y = X^2$. Here $E[X] = 0$, $E[XY] = E[X^3] = 0 \implies \text{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$, but $Y$ is 100% deterministically dependent on $X$!

A perfect positive linear relationship has the form:

\[ Y=aX+b \]

with:

\[ a\gt 0 \]

under non-degenerate variation.

Then:

\[ \boxed{ \rho_{X,Y}=1. } \]

The points lie exactly along an increasing straight line.

13

Perfect Negative Correlation

If:

\[ Y=aX+b \]

with:

\[ a\lt 0, \]

then:

\[ \boxed{ \rho_{X,Y}=-1. } \]

The points lie exactly along a decreasing straight line.

14

Zero Correlation Does Not Mean Independence

This distinction is emphasized in the supplied Lecture02:

\[ \boxed{ Independent \Rightarrow Uncorrelated } \]

but:

\[ \boxed{ Uncorrelated \not\Rightarrow Independent. } \]

fileciteturn67file0

This is a high-value conceptual point.

15

Why Independence Implies Zero Covariance

If \(X,Y\) are independent:

\[ E[XY]=E[X]E[Y]. \]

Therefore:

\[ Cov(X,Y) = E[XY]-E[X]E[Y] = 0. \]

Hence:

\[ \boxed{ X\perp Y \Rightarrow Cov(X,Y)=0. } \]

And therefore:

\[ \boxed{ X\perp Y \Rightarrow \rho_{X,Y}=0 } \]

when the correlation is defined.

16

Why the Converse Fails

Suppose:

\[ Y=X^2 \]

and \(X\) is symmetric around zero, for example:

\[ X\sim Uniform(-1,1). \]

There is a clear nonlinear relationship:

\[ Y=X^2. \]

But:

\[ E[X]=0. \]

Also:

\[ E[XY] = E[X^3]. \]

For a symmetric distribution around zero:

\[ E[X^3]=0. \]

Therefore:

\[ Cov(X,Y) = E[X^3]-E[X]E[X^2] = 0-0\cdot E[X^2] = 0. \]

Yet \(Y\) is completely determined by \(X\), so the variables are certainly not independent.

This illustrates the limitation of correlation for nonlinear relationships, exactly the distinction highlighted by the supplied Lecture02. fileciteturn67file0

17

Worked Example — Positive Covariance

Suppose:

\[ X= \begin{cases} 0,&0.5\\ 2,&0.5 \end{cases} \]

and:

\[ Y= \begin{cases} 0,&0.5\\ 4,&0.5. \end{cases} \]

Assume the high values occur together and low values occur together.

Then:

\[ E[X]=1, \]
\[ E[Y]=2. \]

Also:

\[ E[XY] = 0(0)(0.5)+2(4)(0.5) = 4. \]

Thus:

\[ Cov(X,Y) = 4-(1)(2) = \boxed2. \]

Positive covariance reflects their tendency to move together.

20

Covariance with Itself

Set:

\[ Y=X. \]

Then:

\[ Cov(X,X) = E[(X-E[X])^2]. \]

But this is exactly variance:

\[ \boxed{ Cov(X,X)=Var(X). } \]

So covariance generalizes variance.

21

Correlation with Itself

If \(Var(X)\gt 0\):

\[ \rho_{X,X} = \frac{Var(X)} {\sqrt{Var(X)Var(X)}} \]
\[ = \frac{Var(X)}{Var(X)} = \boxed1. \]
22

Covariance Under Scaling

For constants \(a,b\):

\[ \boxed{ Cov(aX,bY)=ab\,Cov(X,Y). } \]

Why?

The deviations become:

\[ a(X-E[X]) \]

and:

\[ b(Y-E[Y]). \]

Their product gains a factor:

\[ ab. \]

This is useful when interpreting units and transformations.

23

Adding Constants to Covariance

For constants \(c,d\):

\[ \boxed{ Cov(X+c,Y+d)=Cov(X,Y). } \]

Shifting either variable does not change its deviations from its own mean.

Therefore covariance cares about joint variation, not absolute location.

24

Correlation Is Scale-Invariant

For positive scaling:

\[ Y=aX+b, \quad a\gt 0, \]

correlation remains:

\[ \rho_{X,Y}=1. \]

If scale is reversed:

\[ a\lt 0, \]

the sign reverses:

\[ \rho_{X,Y}=-1. \]

Thus correlation is unaffected by positive changes of units and sensitive to direction reversal.

25

Covariance and Expected Products

Recall:

\[ Cov(X,Y) = E[XY]-E[X]E[Y]. \]

Therefore:

\[ \boxed{ E[XY] = Cov(X,Y)+E[X]E[Y]. } \]

This rearrangement is useful in many derivations.

Special case:

If:

\[ Cov(X,Y)=0, \]

then:

\[ E[XY]=E[X]E[Y]. \]

But zero covariance alone does not establish independence.

26

Joint Distribution Connection

M14 introduced:

\[ p_{X,Y}(x,y). \]

That joint distribution lets us calculate:

\[ E[XY] = \sum_x\sum_y xy\,p_{X,Y}(x,y). \]

Therefore:

\[ \boxed{ Cov(X,Y) = \sum_x\sum_y xy\,p_{X,Y}(x,y) - E[X]E[Y] } \]

for discrete variables.

This shows exactly how joint information enters covariance.

27

Continuous Version

For continuous variables:

\[ E[XY] = \int\int xy\,f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,dx\,dy. \]

Then:

\[ \boxed{ Cov(X,Y) = \int\int xy\,f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,dx\,dy - E[X]E[Y]. } \]

The supplied lecture focuses on the definitions and examples of covariance/correlation; this is the corresponding continuous formulation.

28

Covariance Matrix Preview

For several random variables:

\[ X= \begin{bmatrix} X_1\\ X_2\\ \vdots\\ X_d \end{bmatrix}, \]

we can arrange variances and covariances into a matrix:

\[ \boxed{ \Sigma= \begin{bmatrix} Var(X_1)&Cov(X_1,X_2)&\cdots\\ Cov(X_2,X_1)&Var(X_2)&\cdots\\ \vdots&\vdots&\ddots \end{bmatrix}. } \]

This is called a covariance matrix.

It becomes important in multivariate statistics and ML.

29

AI/ML Connection — Feature Relationships

Suppose features are:

X₁ = house area
X₂ = number of rooms

If their covariance is positive, larger homes may tend to have more rooms.

If covariance is negative, one variable may tend to fall as the other rises.

But covariance magnitude depends on units.

Correlation gives a normalized measure:

\[ \rho_{X_1,X_2}. \]

This is why correlation is commonly used for comparing linear relationships between features.

30

AI/ML Connection — Regression

Correlation can give intuition about linear association between an input and target.

However:

\[ \boxed{ correlation \ne causation } \]

and:

\[ \boxed{ correlation \ne proof\ of\ predictive\ sufficiency. } \]

A feature can have low linear correlation but still contain useful nonlinear information.

This is consistent with Lecture02's statement that correlation does not capture nonlinear relationships. fileciteturn67file0

31

AI/ML Connection — Standardization

Recall:

\[ Z_X=\frac{X-\mu_X}{\sigma_X}. \]

For standardized variables:

\[ E[Z_X]=0, \]
\[ Var(Z_X)=1. \]

For two standardized variables:

\[ Cov(Z_X,Z_Y) = \rho_{X,Y}. \]

Thus correlation can be understood as the covariance of standardized variables.

This gives a strong conceptual link among:

mean
→ variance
→ standard deviation
→ covariance
→ correlation

Covariance vs Correlation

Property Covariance Correlation
Measures joint variation Yes Yes
Captures linear association Yes Yes
Scale-dependent Yes No
Fixed range No \([-1,1]\)
Unit-dependent Yes Dimensionless
Zero means no linear co-variation Yes Yes
Independence implies zero Yes Yes, when defined
35

Independent vs Uncorrelated

Independent

Knowledge of one does not change the distribution of the other.

This is a distribution-level statement.

Uncorrelated

\[ Cov(X,Y)=0. \]

This is a linear-association statement.

Therefore:

\[ \boxed{ Independent \Rightarrow Uncorrelated } \]

but:

\[ \boxed{ Uncorrelated \not\Rightarrow Independent. } \]

This exact logical distinction is highlighted in the supplied Lecture02. fileciteturn67file0

36

Common Mistakes

⚠️ University Exam Trap & Common Misconception
**Zero Correlation does NOT imply Independence:** $\text{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$ means there is no LINEAR relationship. A strong NON-LINEAR relationship can exist with exact zero covariance! Classic counterexample: Let $X \in \{-1, 0, 1\}$ with equal probability, and $Y = X^2$. Here $E[X] = 0$, $E[XY] = E[X^3] = 0 \implies \text{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$, but $Y$ is 100% deterministically dependent on $X$!

Mistake 1

Confusing:

\[ Cov(X,Y) \]

with:

\[ Var(X). \]

Variance is the special case:

\[ Cov(X,X)=Var(X). \]

Mistake 2

Using covariance as a scale-free measure.

It is not.

Mistake 3

Assuming:

\[ \rho=0 \]

means independence.

It does not.

Mistake 4

Assuming zero correlation means no relationship.

It only means no linear relationship captured by Pearson correlation.

Mistake 5

Forgetting standard deviations in the correlation denominator.

Correct:

\[ \sqrt{Var(X)Var(Y)}. \]

Mistake 6

Calling correlation evidence of causation.

It is not.

Mistake 7

Ignoring zero-variance variables.

If \(Var(X)=0\) or \(Var(Y)=0\), Pearson correlation is not defined by the standard formula.

Numerical-Solving Protocol

Covariance

1. Find E[X]
2. Find E[Y]
3. Find E[XY]
4. Compute E[XY]−E[X]E[Y]
5. Interpret sign

Correlation

1. Find covariance
2. Find Var(X)
3. Find Var(Y)
4. Take √(Var(X)Var(Y))
5. Divide covariance by denominator
6. Check answer ∈ [−1,1]

Independence test

Do not use correlation alone.

Use a distribution-level condition such as:

\[ p_{X,Y}(x,y)=p_X(x)p_Y(y) \]

for all relevant values in the discrete case.
