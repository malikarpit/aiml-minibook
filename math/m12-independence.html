01

Why This Lesson Exists

Probability often becomes manageable because we can sometimes decompose a complicated problem into simpler pieces.

The key question is:

Can knowing one variable/event change what we know about another?

If not, we may have independence.

This idea matters throughout AI/ML because many probabilistic calculations become simpler when variables can be factorized.

But there is an important warning:

\[ \boxed{ \text{independence is a precise mathematical condition, not a vague notion of “unrelated”.} } \]
02

One-Minute Intuition

👨‍🏫 Teacher Intuition — Bilingual Mental Model
Independence ka definition factorization se aata hai: $P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B)$. Lekin AI mein sabse powerful concept hai **Conditional Independence**: Fever aur Cough aapas mein strongly correlated hain. Lekin agar hume pata chal jaye ki patient ko Flu hai (condition $C$), toh fever ka aana cough ki extra information nahi deta: $P(\text{Fever}, \text{Cough} | \text{Flu}) = P(\text{Fever} | \text{Flu}) \times P(\text{Cough} | \text{Flu})$. Yahi 'Naive Bayes' ka magic foundation hai!

Suppose you toss two fair coins.

The result of the first toss does not change the probability of the second toss.

So:

Toss 1 information
       ↓
does not change
       ↓
Toss 2 probability

That is independence.

But now imagine:

choose one biased coin
↓
toss it twice

The two tosses can become statistically related because they share the hidden choice of which coin was selected.

This is where conditional independence becomes important.

03

Event Independence

Events \(A\) and \(B\) are independent if:

\[ \boxed{ P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B). } \]

When:

\[ P(B)\gt 0, \]

the equivalent form is:

\[ \boxed{ P(A|B)=P(A). } \]

Similarly, when \(P(A)\gt 0\):

\[ P(B|A)=P(B). \]

These formulations are given in the supplied Lecture01. fileciteturn64file5

04

Meaning of Independence

The equation:

\[ P(A|B)=P(A) \]

means:

once we know \(B\) occurred, our probability for \(A\) does not change.

So \(B\) provides no probabilistic information about \(A\), under the model.

05

Independence vs Conditional Probability

Conditional probability asks:

\[ P(A|B). \]

Independence asks whether the condition \(B\) matters:

\[ \boxed{ P(A|B)\stackrel{?}{=}P(A). } \]

So independence can be viewed as a special case of conditional probability where the condition makes no difference.

06

Worked Example — Two Coin Tosses

Let:

\[ A=\{\text{Head on first toss}\} \]

and:

\[ B=\{\text{Head on second toss}\}. \]

For fair independent tosses:

\[ P(A)=\frac12, \qquad P(B)=\frac12. \]

Also:

\[ P(A\cap B)=\frac14. \]

Since:

\[ P(A)P(B) = \frac12\cdot\frac12 = \frac14, \]

we get:

\[ \boxed{ A\text{ and }B\text{ are independent}. } \]

The supplied Lecture01 uses this exact experiment as an independence exercise. fileciteturn64file5

Python & NumPy From-Scratch Verification

PYTHON
import numpy as np

n = 100000
coin1 = np.random.randint(0, 2, size=n)
coin2 = np.random.randint(0, 2, size=n)

p_c1_heads = np.mean(coin1 == 1)
p_c2_heads = np.mean(coin2 == 1)
p_both_heads = np.mean((coin1 == 1) & (coin2 == 1))

print("P(Coin 1 = Heads):", p_c1_heads)
print("P(Coin 2 = Heads):", p_c2_heads)
print("P(Both Heads) Empirical:", p_both_heads)
print("P(C1) * P(C2) Theoretical:", p_c1_heads * p_c2_heads)
07

A Fast Independence Test

When a numerical question asks whether \(A\) and \(B\) are independent:

1. Compute P(A)P(B).
2. Compute P(A ∩ B).
3. Compare them.

If:

\[ P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B), \]

then independent.

Otherwise:

\[ \boxed{\text{not independent}.} \]
08

Worked Example — Not Independent

Suppose:

\[ P(A)=0.4 \]
\[ P(B)=0.5 \]
\[ P(A\cap B)=0.3. \]

Compute:

\[ P(A)P(B)=0.4(0.5)=0.2. \]

Since:

\[ 0.3\ne0.2, \]

the events are:

\[ \boxed{\text{not independent}.} \]

09

Independence with Three Events

For three events \(A,B,C\), mutual independence requires more than pairwise independence.

The supplied Lecture01 states that mutual independence requires:

\[ P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B), \]
\[ P(B\cap C)=P(B)P(C), \]
\[ P(A\cap C)=P(A)P(C), \]

and also:

\[ \boxed{ P(A\cap B\cap C) = P(A)P(B)P(C). } \]

Pairwise independence alone is not sufficient. fileciteturn64file6

10

Pairwise vs Mutual Independence

Pairwise independence

Every pair is independent.

For three events:

A ⟂ B
A ⟂ C
B ⟂ C

Mutual independence

The joint intersection must also factorize:

\[ P(A\cap B\cap C) = P(A)P(B)P(C), \]

along with the required lower-order conditions.

Therefore:

\[ \boxed{ \text{mutual independence} \Rightarrow \text{pairwise independence} } \]

but the reverse does not generally hold.

11

Independence of Random Variables

⚠️ University Exam Trap & Common Misconception
**Disjoint vs Independent Confusion:** Mutually Exclusive (disjoint, $A \cap B = \emptyset$) does NOT mean Independent! If $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive with positive probabilities, they are STRONGLY DEPENDENT because if $A$ occurs, $B$ is guaranteed NOT to occur ($P(B|A) = 0 \neq P(B)$)!

For discrete random variables \(X,Y\), independence means:

\[ \boxed{ p_{X,Y}(x,y) = p_X(x)p_Y(y) } \]

for all valid \(x,y\).

This is the random-variable analogue of event independence.

The supplied Lecture02 gives this definition explicitly and uses a joint-PMF example to test independence. fileciteturn64file7

12

Why Joint Distribution Matters

⚠️ University Exam Trap & Common Misconception
**Disjoint vs Independent Confusion:** Mutually Exclusive (disjoint, $A \cap B = \emptyset$) does NOT mean Independent! If $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive with positive probabilities, they are STRONGLY DEPENDENT because if $A$ occurs, $B$ is guaranteed NOT to occur ($P(B|A) = 0 \neq P(B)$)!

Knowing:

\[ p_X(x) \]

and:

\[ p_Y(y) \]

does not generally tell us the joint distribution:

\[ p_{X,Y}(x,y). \]

Different dependence structures can have the same marginals.

If \(X,Y\) are independent, however:

\[ \boxed{ p_{X,Y}(x,y) = p_X(x)p_Y(y). } \]

Therefore independence gives a powerful factorization.

13

Joint Table Independence Test

Suppose a joint PMF table is given.

To test independence:

1. Compute row marginal pX(x).
2. Compute column marginal pY(y).
3. Pick a cell (x,y).
4. Compare pXY(x,y) with pX(x)pY(y).
5. If even one valid cell disagrees → not independent.

To prove independence, verify the equality for all relevant cells.

14

Worked Example — Joint PMF Test

Suppose:

\[ p_{X,Y}(1,1)=0.25 \]

while:

\[ p_X(1)=0.55 \]

and:

\[ p_Y(1)=0.45. \]

If independent:

\[ p_X(1)p_Y(1) = 0.55(0.45) = 0.2475. \]

But:

\[ 0.2475\ne0.25. \]

Therefore:

\[ \boxed{ X,Y\text{ are not independent}. } \]

This is the same numerical comparison used in Lecture02. fileciteturn64file7

15

Conditional Independence

Events \(A,B\) are conditionally independent given \(C\) when:

\[ \boxed{ P(A\cap B|C) = P(A|C)P(B|C) } \]

for:

\[ P(C)\gt 0. \]

This was introduced in Lecture01. fileciteturn64file6

16

The Key Intuition

Conditional independence means:

A and B may be dependent generally, but once C is known, that dependence disappears under the conditional model.

Visual:

without C:
A ←→ B

given C:
A    B
 \   /
   C

This is an intuition only; a graph alone does not establish conditional independence unless the underlying probability model supports the claim.

17

Hidden-Variable Example

Suppose there are two coins:

Coin 1 → P(H)=0.9
Coin 2 → P(H)=0.1

Choose one coin at random, then toss the chosen coin twice.

Let:

\[ H_1=\{\text{Head on toss 1}\} \]

and:

\[ H_2=\{\text{Head on toss 2}\}. \]

Marginally, the two tosses can become dependent because both share the hidden coin-selection variable.

But given:

\[ C=\{\text{Coin 1 was chosen}\}, \]

the tosses are independent under the coin model:

\[ \boxed{ P(H_1\cap H_2|C) = P(H_1|C)P(H_2|C). } \]

The supplied Lecture01 uses this experiment to introduce conditional independence. fileciteturn64file6

18

Independent Given a Variable vs Independent

These are not the same statement.

Marginal independence

\[ X\perp Y. \]

Conditional independence

\[ X\perp Y\mid Z. \]

It is possible for:

\[ X\not\!\perp Y \]

but:

\[ X\perp Y\mid Z. \]

A hidden/common variable \(Z\) can create apparent dependence.

19

Conditional Distribution

For discrete random variables:

\[ \boxed{ p_{X|Y}(x|y) = \frac{p_{X,Y}(x,y)} {p_Y(y)} } \]

when:

\[ p_Y(y)\gt 0. \]

This lets us move between joint and conditional descriptions.

From the multiplication rule:

\[ \boxed{ p_{X,Y}(x,y) = p_{X|Y}(x|y)p_Y(y). } \]
20

Independence Simplifies Conditional Distribution

If \(X,Y\) are independent:

\[ p_{X,Y}(x,y)=p_X(x)p_Y(y). \]

Therefore:

\[ p_{X|Y}(x|y) = \frac{p_X(x)p_Y(y)}{p_Y(y)} = \boxed{p_X(x)}. \]

So knowing \(Y\) does not change the distribution of \(X\).

This is the random-variable equivalent of:

\[ P(A|B)=P(A). \]
21

Conditional Expectation Preview

If \(X,Y\) are discrete:

\[ \boxed{ E[Y|X=x] = \sum_y y\,p_{Y|X}(y|x). } \]

This is not yet the focus of M12, but it shows why conditional distributions matter later.

The supplied Lecture02 explicitly defines conditional expectation in this form. fileciteturn64file7

22

Independence and Expectation

If \(X,Y\) are independent, then:

\[ \boxed{ E[XY]=E[X]E[Y] } \]

when the expectations exist.

The supplied Lecture02 gives this as a useful identity while emphasizing that:

\[ E[X+Y]=E[X]+E[Y] \]

does not require independence. fileciteturn64file7

This distinction is important.

23

Linearity of Expectation Does Not Require Independence

Always remember:

\[ \boxed{ E[X+Y]=E[X]+E[Y] } \]

even if \(X,Y\) are dependent.

But:

\[ \boxed{ E[XY]=E[X]E[Y] } \]

generally requires independence.

Therefore:

sum
→ linearity is enough

product
→ independence may be needed
24

Independence and Covariance

For independent random variables, assuming the needed expectations exist:

\[ \boxed{ Cov(X,Y)=0. } \]

Why?

\[ Cov(X,Y) = E[XY]-E[X]E[Y]. \]

Independence gives:

\[ E[XY]=E[X]E[Y]. \]

Therefore:

\[ Cov(X,Y)=0. \]
25

But Zero Covariance Does Not Imply Independence

The reverse statement is false in general:

\[ \boxed{ Cov(X,Y)=0 \not\Rightarrow X\perp Y. } \]

The supplied Lecture02 gives a concrete counterexample:

\[ X\sim Uniform(-1,1), \]
\[ Y=X^2. \]

Then:

\[ Cov(X,Y)=0, \]

yet \(Y\) is completely determined by \(X\), so they are dependent. fileciteturn64file7

26

Why Correlation Can Miss Nonlinear Dependence

Pearson correlation measures linear association.

A relationship such as:

\[ Y=X^2 \]

can be strongly dependent but have zero linear correlation under a symmetric distribution.

Therefore:

correlation ≈ linear relationship
independence = much stronger probabilistic condition

This distinction is fundamental.

27

Worked Example — Independent Variables and Product Expectation

Suppose:

\[ E[X]=2, \quad E[Y]=3, \]

and \(X,Y\) are independent.

Then:

\[ E[XY] = E[X]E[Y] = 2(3) = \boxed6. \]

Without independence, the value cannot generally be determined from the two means alone.

They cannot be independent unless the distribution is degenerate in a special way.

Conditional Independence in AI/ML

Conditional independence is useful because complex probability distributions can sometimes be factorized.

Conceptually:

\[ P(A,B,C) \]

may be expressed through:

\[ P(A|C)P(B|C)P(C) \]

when:

\[ A\perp B|C. \]

This type of factorization is one of the mathematical ideas behind probabilistic graphical models.

It is not necessary to study full graphical-model machinery for the basic DSC-14 syllabus, but recognizing the factorization pattern is valuable.

30

Classification Connection

A probabilistic classifier estimates something like:

\[ P(y|x). \]

If features have conditional independence given the class, a model can factor:

\[ P(x_1,\ldots,x_d|y) = \prod_{j=1}^{d}P(x_j|y). \]

This is the core mathematical assumption behind naive Bayes.

Naive Bayes is not listed as a core DSC-14 topic in the supplied course map, so this equation is an extension example, not a required course topic.

Hinglish Teacher Explanation

Independence ka easiest idea:

“B ke baare mein pata chalne ke baad A ki probability change nahi hui.”

Mathematically:

\[ P(A|B)=P(A). \]

Ya:

\[ P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B). \]

Random variables ke case mein:

\[ p_{X,Y}(x,y)=p_X(x)p_Y(y). \]

Ab conditional independence:

“A aur B generally related ho sakte hain, lekin C pata chalne ke baad unka relationship disappear ho jata hai.”

\[ P(A\cap B|C) = P(A|C)P(B|C). \]

Coin example mein:

randomly coin choose karo
↓
same coin ko do baar toss karo

Dono tosses marginally related ho sakte hain because same hidden coin dono ko affect kar raha hai.

Lekin agar pata chal gaya:

“Coin 1 select hua tha”

toh dono tosses phir independent ho sakte hain.

Ek aur important point:

Independence se covariance zero hota hai, lekin covariance zero hone se independence guarantee nahi hoti.

Example:

\[ Y=X^2. \]

Dependence clear hai because \(X\) pata ho toh \(Y\) exact pata hai.

Phir bhi symmetric \(X\) ke case mein covariance zero ho sakta hai.

Common Mistakes

⚠️ University Exam Trap & Common Misconception
**Disjoint vs Independent Confusion:** Mutually Exclusive (disjoint, $A \cap B = \emptyset$) does NOT mean Independent! If $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive with positive probabilities, they are STRONGLY DEPENDENT because if $A$ occurs, $B$ is guaranteed NOT to occur ($P(B|A) = 0 \neq P(B)$)!

Mistake 1 — Independence means mutually exclusive

Wrong.

Independent events can occur together.

Mistake 2 — \(P(A|B)=P(B|A)\)

Wrong in general.

Mistake 3 — Pairwise independence means mutual independence

Not generally.

Mistake 4 — Zero covariance means independence

Wrong in general.

Mistake 5 — Linearity of expectation needs independence

It does not.

Mistake 6 — Conditional independence automatically implies marginal independence

Not generally.

Mistake 7 — Checking only intuition

Always use the mathematical definition for an exam proof/numerical.
