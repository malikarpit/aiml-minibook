01

Why This Lesson Exists

Once data is represented as vectors, we immediately want to ask:

How large is this vector?
How far apart are two vectors?
How similar are their magnitudes?
How strongly are parameters constrained?

A norm gives a mathematical way to measure vector magnitude.

Distance between vectors can then be constructed from the norm of their difference:

\[ \boxed{ d(x,y)=\|x-y\| } \]

This is fundamental to KNN and also appears in regularization.

02

One-Minute Intuition

👨‍🏫 Teacher Intuition — Bilingual Mental Model
Norm ka simple matlab hai **Vector ki lambai (length)**. $L_2$ norm straight-line Euclidean distance hai jaise panchi udta hai (as the crow flies). $L_1$ norm Manhattan taxi distance hai jahan aapko grid streets par turn le-lekar chalna padta hai. Kyunki $L_1$ norm ka unit ball sharp corners (diamonds) banata hai axes par, isiliye Lasso Regression feature weights ko EXACTLY ZERO par lock karta hai!

Imagine a point on a map.

A vector tells you:

how far
+
in which direction

A norm answers:

How long is the arrow?

Distance answers:

How far are two points from each other?

So:

vector → displacement
norm   → length
distance → separation
03

Real-World Analogy

Suppose two students are represented using:

study hours
attendance
previous score

as vectors:

\[ x= \begin{bmatrix} 5\\ 80\\ 75 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad y= \begin{bmatrix} 6\\ 78\\ 72 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The difference is:

\[ x-y= \begin{bmatrix} -1\\ 2\\ 3 \end{bmatrix}. \]

A distance measure can turn that difference into a single number.

If the number is small, the students are close according to that chosen geometry.

This is the core intuition behind K-nearest neighbours.

04

Norm Definition

A norm is a function that measures the size/magnitude of a vector.

A standard norm satisfies:

Non-negativity

\[ \boxed{ \|x\|\ge0 } \]

Definiteness

\[ \boxed{ \|x\|=0 \iff x=0 } \]

Absolute homogeneity

\[ \boxed{ \|cx\|=|c|\|x\| } \]

Triangle inequality

\[ \boxed{ \|x+y\|\le\|x\|+\|y\| } \]

These properties define the geometric behaviour expected from a norm.

The most familiar norm is Euclidean length:

\[ \boxed{ \|x\|_2= \sqrt{ \sum_{i=1}^{d}x_i^2 } } \]

For:

\[ x= \begin{bmatrix} 3\\ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \]

we get:

\[ \|x\|_2 = \sqrt{3^2+4^2} = 5. \]

This is simply the Pythagorean theorem generalized to more dimensions.

06

Why the Euclidean Formula Makes Sense

In 2D:

\[ x= \begin{bmatrix} x_1\\ x_2 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The horizontal displacement is \(x_1\).

The vertical displacement is \(x_2\).

Therefore:

\[ length^2=x_1^2+x_2^2. \]

Taking square root:

\[ \boxed{ length=\sqrt{x_1^2+x_2^2} } \]

For \(d\) dimensions, the same pattern continues:

\[ \sqrt{x_1^2+x_2^2+\cdots+x_d^2}. \]
07

The \(L_1\) Norm

The \(L_1\) norm is:

\[ \boxed{ \|x\|_1= \sum_{i=1}^{d}|x_i| } \]

For:

\[ x= \begin{bmatrix} 3\\ -4 \end{bmatrix}, \]

we get:

\[ \|x\|_1 = |3|+|-4| = 7. \]

It is associated with Manhattan distance when applied to vector differences.

08

Manhattan Geometry

Imagine walking through a city where you can only travel along perpendicular streets.

You cannot move diagonally.

The distance becomes:

\[ |x_1-y_1|+|x_2-y_2|. \]

That is Manhattan distance.

Geometrically:

Euclidean
→ straight-line distance

Manhattan
→ grid/street distance
09

General \(L_p\) Norm

For \(p\ge1\):

\[ \boxed{ \|x\|_p = \left( \sum_i|x_i|^p \right)^{1/p} } \]

Special cases:

\[ p=1 \Rightarrow L_1 \]
\[ p=2 \Rightarrow L_2. \]

As \(p\) changes, the geometry of “size” changes.

10

\(L_\infty\) Norm

Another useful special case:

\[ \boxed{ \|x\|_\infty=\max_i|x_i| } \]

It measures the largest absolute component.

Example:

\[ x= \begin{bmatrix} -2\\ 5\\ 3 \end{bmatrix} \]

then:

\[ \|x\|_\infty=5. \]

The full focus of DSC-14 regularization is \(L_1\) and \(L_2\), but knowing \(L_\infty\) clarifies that “norm” is a family of possible size measures.

Python & NumPy From-Scratch Verification

PYTHON
import numpy as np

x = np.array([3.0, -4.0])

l1_norm = np.linalg.norm(x, ord=1)        # |3| + |-4| = 7
l2_norm = np.linalg.norm(x, ord=2)        # sqrt(3^2 + (-4)^2) = 5
linf_norm = np.linalg.norm(x, ord=np.inf) # max(|3|, |-4|) = 4

print("Vector:", x)
print("L1 (Manhattan) Norm:", l1_norm)
print("L2 (Euclidean) Norm:", l2_norm)
print("L-infinity (Chebyshev) Norm:", linf_norm)
11

Norm Comparison Example

⚠️ University Exam Trap & Common Misconception
**Norm Ordering Misconception:** In any finite $d$-dimensional space, $\|x\|_2 \le \|x\|_1 \le \sqrt{d} \|x\|_2$. Many students incorrectly assume that $L_2$ is always larger than $L_1$ because of the square roots. Remember: the sum of absolute values is always greater than or equal to the square root of the sum of squares!

Let:

\[ x= \begin{bmatrix} 3\\ -4 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Then:

\[ \|x\|_1=7 \]
\[ \|x\|_2=5 \]
\[ \|x\|_\infty=4. \]

Same vector.

Different geometry.

This is important:

A norm does not merely calculate a number; it defines what “size” means.

Distance from a Vector to the Origin

⚠️ University Exam Trap & Common Misconception
**Norm Ordering Misconception:** In any finite $d$-dimensional space, $\|x\|_2 \le \|x\|_1 \le \sqrt{d} \|x\|_2$. Many students incorrectly assume that $L_2$ is always larger than $L_1$ because of the square roots. Remember: the sum of absolute values is always greater than or equal to the square root of the sum of squares!

Distance from \(x\) to the zero vector is:

\[ \boxed{ d(x,0)=\|x\} \]

for the chosen norm.

Under \(L_2\):

\[ d(x,0)=\|x\|_2. \]

Under \(L_1\):

\[ d(x,0)=\|x\|_1. \]
13

Distance Between Two Vectors

For a norm-induced distance:

\[ \boxed{ d(x,y)=\|x-y\} \]

Important correction to notation:

\[ \boxed{ d(x,y)=\|x-y\| } \]

The idea is:

subtract points
→ obtain displacement
→ measure its norm
→ get distance
14

Euclidean Distance

For vectors:

\[ x= \begin{bmatrix} x_1\\ \vdots\\ x_d \end{bmatrix}, \quad y= \begin{bmatrix} y_1\\ \vdots\\ y_d \end{bmatrix}, \]

Euclidean distance:

\[ \boxed{ d_2(x,y) = \sqrt{ \sum_{i=1}^{d}(x_i-y_i)^2 } } \]

This is central to KNN.

15

Manhattan Distance

\[ \boxed{ d_1(x,y) = \sum_{i=1}^{d}|x_i-y_i| } \]

Again:

difference first
→ absolute / square
→ aggregate
16

Why Scaling Matters for Distance

Suppose:

age ∈ [18,60]
income ∈ [20,000, 2,000,000]

A raw Euclidean distance may be dominated by income.

For example:

\[ d^2=(\Delta age)^2+(\Delta income)^2. \]

The income term can become enormous compared with the age term.

Therefore distance-based algorithms often require thoughtful feature scaling.

17

KNN Connection

KNN works by comparing a query point to training examples.

Basic sequence:

query point
↓
calculate distance to each training point
↓
select k nearest
↓
use neighbours for prediction

The exact distance metric matters.

Possible choices include:

Euclidean
Manhattan
other problem-specific metrics

So the mathematics of norms directly controls what “nearest” means.

18

Nearest Neighbour Example

Suppose query:

\[ q= \begin{bmatrix} 2\\ 3 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Points:

\[ p_1= \begin{bmatrix} 3\\ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad p_2= \begin{bmatrix} 5\\ 3 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Euclidean:

\[ d(q,p_1) = \sqrt{(2-3)^2+(3-4)^2} = \sqrt2. \]

For \(p_2\):

\[ d(q,p_2) = \sqrt{(2-5)^2+(3-3)^2} = 3. \]

Therefore \(p_1\) is closer under Euclidean distance.

Why Norms Appear in Regularization

Suppose a model uses parameter vector:

\[ w. \]

Large parameters can make a model more flexible or unstable in some settings.

Regularization adds a penalty involving parameter magnitude.

Ridge

\[ \boxed{ \lambda\|w\|_2^2 } \]

Lasso

\[ \boxed{ \lambda\|w\|_1 } \]

So:

norm
→ parameter size
→ penalty
→ optimization trade-off
20

\(L_2\) vs Squared \(L_2\)

Be precise.

\[ \|w\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_jw_j^2}. \]

But:

\[ \boxed{ \|w\|_2^2 = \sum_jw_j^2 } \]

The square removes the outer square root.

Ridge commonly uses:

\[ \lambda\|w\|_2^2. \]

Do not accidentally write:

\[ \lambda\|w\|_2. \]

They are different penalties.

24

Triangle Inequality Intuition

For any norm:

\[ \boxed{ \|x+y\|\le\|x\|+\|y\| } \]

Interpretation:

Going directly from one point to another cannot be longer than going through an intermediate route.

This mirrors the geometry of physical travel.

25

Homogeneity

For scalar \(c\):

\[ \boxed{ \|cx\|=|c|\|x\| } \]

Example:

\[ \|2x\|=2\|x\|. \]

If:

\[ c=-2, \]

then:

\[ \|-2x\|=2\|x\|. \]

The negative sign changes direction but not length.

l1_norm = np.linalg.norm(x, ord=1)        # |3| + |-4| = 7
l2_norm = np.linalg.norm(x, ord=2)        # sqrt(3^2 + (-4)^2) = 5
linf_norm = np.linalg.norm(x, ord=np.inf) # max(|3|, |-4|) = 4

print("Vector:", x)
print("L1 (Manhattan) Norm:", l1_norm)
print("L2 (Euclidean) Norm:", l2_norm)
print("L-infinity (Chebyshev) Norm:", linf_norm)

Common Mistakes

⚠️ University Exam Trap & Common Misconception
**Norm Ordering Misconception:** In any finite $d$-dimensional space, $\|x\|_2 \le \|x\|_1 \le \sqrt{d} \|x\|_2$. Many students incorrectly assume that $L_2$ is always larger than $L_1$ because of the square roots. Remember: the sum of absolute values is always greater than or equal to the square root of the sum of squares!

Mistake 1 — Forgetting absolute values in \(L_1\)

Wrong:

\[ \|x\|_1=\sum_i x_i. \]

Correct:

\[ \boxed{ \|x\|_1=\sum_i|x_i| } \]

Mistake 2 — Calling every distance Euclidean

Distance depends on the chosen metric.

Mistake 3 — Confusing norm with squared norm

\[ \|x\|_2 \ne \|x\|_2^2. \]

Mistake 4 — Forgetting to subtract first

Distance:

\[ d(x,y)=\|x-y\| \]

not simply \(\|x\|+\|y\|\).

Mistake 5 — Assuming normalization means standardization

They are different transformations.

Mistake 6 — Thinking L1 and L2 are just two formulas

They create different geometric notions of size and different optimization behaviour.
