01

Why This Lesson Exists

One random variable tells us about one uncertain quantity.

But machine-learning problems often involve many quantities at once:

temperature
humidity
rain

or:

input X
target Y
prediction Ŷ

We therefore need to describe:

X alone
Y alone
X and Y together
X given Y
Y given X

The mathematical tools are:

\[ \boxed{ \text{joint} \rightarrow \text{marginal} \rightarrow \text{conditional} } \]

This chapter builds that structure.

02

One-Minute Intuition

👨‍🏫 Teacher Intuition — Bilingual Mental Model
Marginalization ka practical matlab hai: "Jab aapko kisi table mein ek variable se koi matlab na ho, toh us variable ke sabhi cases ko add karke gayab kar do!" $P(X = x) = \sum_{y} P(X = x, Y = y)$. Generative classifiers (jaise Naive Bayes) pehle poora joint $P(X, Y)$ model karte hain, jabki Discriminative classifiers (jaise Logistic Regression) seedhe conditional boundary $P(Y | X)$ seekhte hain!

Imagine a table containing two random variables.

Y
          y₁   y₂   y₃
       ┌────────────────
X  x₁  │ •    •    •
   x₂  │ •    •    •
   x₃  │ •    •    •

The joint distribution describes every cell.

The marginal distribution collapses one dimension:

sum across Y
→ distribution of X

sum across X
→ distribution of Y

The conditional distribution focuses on one row/column after observing the other variable.

This is exactly the progression developed in the supplied Lecture02.

03

Joint Random Variables

Suppose \(X\) and \(Y\) are two random variables.

The pair:

\[ \boxed{ (X,Y) } \]

is itself a random object taking values in a product space.

For discrete variables, the joint PMF is:

\[ \boxed{ p_{X,Y}(x,y) = P(X=x,Y=y). } \]

The supplied Lecture02 gives this definition explicitly.

04

Why Individual PMFs Are Not Enough

Knowing:

\[ p_X(x) \]

and:

\[ p_Y(y) \]

does not generally determine:

\[ p_{X,Y}(x,y). \]

Two random variables can have the same marginal distributions but different dependence structures.

So:

marginal X
+
marginal Y
≠
necessarily joint(X,Y)

The supplied Lecture02 explicitly asks this question and answers no, except when independence provides the required factorization.

05

Joint PMF Table

    B --> D["Marginal Distribution P(X=x)"]
    C --> E["Marginal Distribution P(Y=y)"]

For discrete variables, a joint PMF can be represented as a table.

Example:

\(X\backslash Y\) 0 1 2
0 0.10 0.10 0.05
1 0.20 0.25 0.10
2 0.05 0.10 0.05

Each cell is:

\[ p_{X,Y}(x,y). \]

For example:

\[ p_{X,Y}(1,1)=0.25. \]
06

Joint PMF Must Sum to 1

Every possible pair belongs somewhere in the table.

Therefore:

\[ \boxed{ \sum_x\sum_y p_{X,Y}(x,y)=1. } \]

For the example:

\[ 0.10+0.10+0.05 +0.20+0.25+0.10 +0.05+0.10+0.05 =1. \]

The supplied Lecture02 explicitly notes that the joint PMF entries sum to 1.

07

Marginal Distribution

A marginal distribution describes one variable while ignoring the other.

For discrete \(X,Y\):

\[ \boxed{ p_X(x) = \sum_y p_{X,Y}(x,y) } \]

and:

\[ \boxed{ p_Y(y) = \sum_x p_{X,Y}(x,y). } \]

The supplied Lecture02 gives both formulas.

08

Why It Is Called “Marginal”

Imagine writing row sums and column sums at the edge of a probability table.

The values at the margins are the marginal probabilities.

Conceptually:

joint table
   ↓ collapse Y
distribution of X

joint table
   ↓ collapse X
distribution of Y
09

Worked Example — Marginal \(X\)

Using:

\(X\backslash Y\) 0 1 2
0 0.10 0.10 0.05
1 0.20 0.25 0.10
2 0.05 0.10 0.05

For:

\[ X=1, \]

sum row 1:

\[ p_X(1) = 0.20+0.25+0.10. \]

Therefore:

\[ \boxed{ p_X(1)=0.55. } \]

This exact marginal calculation is in Lecture02.

Python & NumPy From-Scratch Verification

PYTHON
import numpy as np

joint_pxy = np.array([[0.10, 0.20, 0.10],
                      [0.15, 0.30, 0.15]])

p_x = np.sum(joint_pxy, axis=1)
p_y = np.sum(joint_pxy, axis=0)
p_y_given_x1 = joint_pxy[1, :] / p_x[1]

print("Joint Table sum (must be 1.0):", np.sum(joint_pxy))
print("Marginal P(X):", p_x)
print("Marginal P(Y):", p_y)
print("Conditional P(Y | X=1):", np.round(p_y_given_x1, 4))
10

Worked Example — Marginal \(Y\)

For:

\[ Y=1, \]

sum the relevant column:

\[ p_Y(1) = 0.10+0.25+0.10. \]

Therefore:

\[ \boxed{ p_Y(1)=0.45. } \]

Again, this matches the supplied Lecture02 example.

11

Marginal Must Be Valid

⚠️ University Exam Trap & Common Misconception
**Conditional Distribution Renormalization:** When computing conditional distribution $P(Y | X = x)$, the probabilities across all $y$ MUST sum to 1.0! Dividing by the marginal $P(X = x)$ acts as the normalizing denominator: $\sum_y P(Y = y | X = x) = 1$.

After calculating:

\[ p_X(x), \]

we should have:

\[ \boxed{ \sum_xp_X(x)=1. } \]

Likewise:

\[ \boxed{ \sum_yp_Y(y)=1. } \]

This provides an excellent numerical sanity check.

12

Joint → Marginal as Summation

⚠️ University Exam Trap & Common Misconception
**Conditional Distribution Renormalization:** When computing conditional distribution $P(Y | X = x)$, the probabilities across all $y$ MUST sum to 1.0! Dividing by the marginal $P(X = x)$ acts as the normalizing denominator: $\sum_y P(Y = y | X = x) = 1$.

Think of a joint PMF as a 2D probability surface/table.

To get one dimension:

sum over the dimension you don't care about

Therefore:

\[ p_X(x) = \sum_y p_{X,Y}(x,y). \]

and:

\[ p_Y(y) = \sum_x p_{X,Y}(x,y). \]

Memory:

Marginalize by summing out the unwanted variable.

13

Conditional PMF

For:

\[ p_Y(y)\gt 0, \]

the conditional PMF is:

\[ \boxed{ p_{X|Y}(x|y) = \frac{p_{X,Y}(x,y)} {p_Y(y)}. } \]

The supplied Lecture02 gives this exact definition.

Similarly:

\[ \boxed{ p_{Y|X}(y|x) = \frac{p_{X,Y}(x,y)} {p_X(x)}. } \]
14

Conditional Distribution as “Normalize a Slice”

Suppose you know:

\[ Y=y. \]

Then only that row/column is relevant.

Take the joint probabilities for that condition and divide by the marginal probability of the condition.

Conceptually:

joint slice
↓
divide by total probability of condition
↓
conditional distribution

This ensures the resulting conditional probabilities add to 1.

15

Worked Example — Conditional PMF

Using the previous joint table, calculate:

\[ p_{X|Y}(1|1). \]

We know:

\[ p_{X,Y}(1,1)=0.25 \]

and:

\[ p_Y(1)=0.45. \]

Therefore:

\[ p_{X|Y}(1|1) = \frac{0.25}{0.45} = \frac59 \approx \boxed{0.556}. \]

This is the exact example in the supplied Lecture02.

16

Conditional PMF Must Sum to 1

For a fixed \(y\) with \(p_Y(y)\gt 0\):

\[ \boxed{ \sum_xp_{X|Y}(x|y)=1. } \]

Proof:

\[ \sum_x p_{X|Y}(x|y) = \sum_x \frac{p_{X,Y}(x,y)} {p_Y(y)} \]
\[ = \frac{\sum_xp_{X,Y}(x,y)} {p_Y(y)} = \frac{p_Y(y)}{p_Y(y)} = 1. \]

This is an important sanity check.

17

Recovering Joint from Conditional

Rearrange the conditional formula:

\[ \boxed{ p_{X,Y}(x,y) = p_{X|Y}(x|y)p_Y(y). } \]

Likewise:

\[ \boxed{ p_{X,Y}(x,y) = p_{Y|X}(y|x)p_X(x). } \]

Thus:

joint
↔
conditional × marginal

This is the random-variable version of the probability multiplication rule.

18

Independence Revisited

If \(X,Y\) are independent:

\[ \boxed{ p_{X,Y}(x,y) = p_X(x)p_Y(y). } \]

Then:

\[ p_{X|Y}(x|y)=p_X(x). \]

So:

knowing \(Y\) does not change the distribution of \(X\).

This connects M12 directly to M14.

19

Conditional vs Marginal — Key Difference

Marginal

\[ p_X(x) \]

asks:

What is the probability of \(X=x\), ignoring \(Y\)?

Conditional

\[ p_{X|Y}(x|y) \]

asks:

What is the probability of \(X=x\), after knowing \(Y=y\)?

Memory:

marginal
→ ignore other variable

conditional
→ condition on other variable
20

Joint Distribution and AI/ML

Suppose:

\[ X=\text{temperature}, \]
\[ Y=\text{rain}. \]

The joint distribution:

\[ p_{X,Y}(x,y) \]

captures how the two variables occur together.

The conditional:

\[ P(Y=y|X=x) \]

can answer:

How likely is rain for a given temperature?

This is the basic mathematical structure behind probabilistic prediction.

21

Classification Connection

For class \(Y\) and feature vector \(X\):

\[ \boxed{ P(Y=y|X=x) } \]

is a posterior-style class probability.

The model asks:

Given these observed features, what is the probability of the class?

Bayes gives:

\[ P(Y|X) \propto P(X|Y)P(Y). \]

The exact normalization is supplied by:

\[ P(X). \]

This is the bridge between M11's Bayes rule and M14's joint/conditional distributions.

22

Joint Distribution and Feature Relationships

If:

\[ p_{X,Y}(x,y) \]

does not factor into:

\[ p_X(x)p_Y(y), \]

then the variables are not independent.

The joint distribution therefore contains information about relationships that marginals alone cannot reveal.

23

Conditional Expectation Preview

Once we have a conditional distribution, we can compute an average conditioned on a known value.

For discrete variables:

\[ \boxed{ E[Y|X=x] = \sum_y y\,p_{Y|X}(y|x). } \]

The supplied Lecture02 demonstrates this using the same joint table.

The full expectation chapter comes next.

24

Worked Example — Conditional Expectation

For:

\[ X=1, \]

the conditional probabilities are:

\[ p_{Y|X}(0|1)=\frac{0.20}{0.55}, \]
\[ p_{Y|X}(1|1)=\frac{0.25}{0.55}, \]
\[ p_{Y|X}(2|1)=\frac{0.10}{0.55}. \]

Then:

\[ E[Y|X=1] = 0\frac{0.20}{0.55} + 1\frac{0.25}{0.55} + 2\frac{0.10}{0.55}. \]

Therefore:

\[ = \frac{0.25+0.20}{0.55} = \frac{0.45}{0.55} \approx \boxed{0.82}. \]

This matches the supplied Lecture02 result.

Marginalization with Continuous Variables

For continuous variables, the same conceptual operation becomes integration.

If a joint density is:

\[ f_{X,Y}(x,y), \]

then:

\[ \boxed{ f_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,dy. } \]

And:

\[ \boxed{ f_Y(y) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,dx. } \]

The supplied Lecture02 primarily works through the discrete joint-PMF example, so these continuous formulas are the natural mathematical extension of the same marginalization idea.

26

Continuous Conditional Density

When defined:

\[ \boxed{ f_{X|Y}(x|y) = \frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)} {f_Y(y)} } \]

for:

\[ f_Y(y)\gt 0. \]

Then:

\[ f_{X,Y}(x,y) = f_{X|Y}(x|y)f_Y(y). \]

This is the continuous analogue of the discrete conditional-PMF relationship.

27

Marginalization Pattern

This is worth memorizing as a universal pattern:

JOINT
  ↓
sum/integrate away unwanted variable
  ↓
MARGINAL

Then:

JOINT + marginal
  ↓
divide
  ↓
CONDITIONAL

And:

CONDITIONAL + marginal
  ↓
multiply
  ↓
JOINT

This triangle is one of the most useful probability structures in AI/ML.

28

The Joint–Marginal–Conditional Triangle

\[ \boxed{ p_{X,Y} } \]

can be transformed into:

\[ \boxed{ p_X,\quad p_Y } \]

by marginalization.

Then:

\[ \boxed{ p_{X|Y} = \frac{p_{X,Y}}{p_Y} } \]

and:

\[ \boxed{ p_{Y|X} = \frac{p_{X,Y}}{p_X}. } \]

Conversely:

\[ \boxed{ p_{X,Y}=p_{X|Y}p_Y. } \]

Memorize the triangle, not isolated formulas.

Hinglish Teacher Explanation

Joint distribution ko ek full probability table samjho.

Suppose:

X = temperature
Y = rain

Joint tells:

“Temperature x aur rain y dono saath mein kitni probability se occur karte hain?”

Marginal \(X\):

“Rain ko ignore kar do. Bas temperature ka distribution batao.”

Isliye:

\[ p_X(x)=\sum_y p_{X,Y}(x,y). \]

Conditional:

“Mujhe pata hai Y = y. Ab X ka distribution kya hai?”

Isliye:

\[ p_{X|Y}(x|y) = \frac{p_{X,Y}(x,y)} {p_Y(y)}. \]

Simple memory:

joint
→ complete picture

marginal
→ one variable alone

conditional
→ one variable after knowing another

Aur agar independent:

\[ p_{X,Y}=p_Xp_Y. \]

Then:

\[ p_{X|Y}=p_X. \]

Matlab:

Y ka pata chalne se X ke distribution mein koi change nahi hua.

Common Mistakes

⚠️ University Exam Trap & Common Misconception
**Conditional Distribution Renormalization:** When computing conditional distribution $P(Y | X = x)$, the probabilities across all $y$ MUST sum to 1.0! Dividing by the marginal $P(X = x)$ acts as the normalizing denominator: $\sum_y P(Y = y | X = x) = 1$.

Mistake 1 — Marginal means “divide”

No.

Marginalization means:

\[ \boxed{\text{sum or integrate}} \]

over the unwanted variable.

Mistake 2 — Conditional denominator wrong

For:

\[ p_{X|Y}(x|y), \]

denominator is:

\[ p_Y(y). \]

Mistake 3 — Confusing:

\[ p_{X|Y}(x|y) \]

with:

\[ p_{Y|X}(y|x). \]

Mistake 4 — Assuming marginals determine the joint

They do not generally.

Mistake 5 — Forgetting normalization

A conditional PMF for fixed condition must sum to 1.

Mistake 6 — Thinking independence is required to calculate a marginal

No.

Marginalization works whether or not variables are independent.

Mistake 7 — Confusing joint and product

\[ p_{X,Y}=p_Xp_Y \]

only under independence.
