MAIN-U1-C01 — What Is Artificial Intelligence?

Book: AI & Machine Learning — Complete Visual & Mathematical Textbook
Part: Unit I — Artificial Intelligence
Chapter ID: MAIN-U1-C01
Status: Foundation / Universal + University Course Lens
Difficulty: Beginner → Intermediate
Prerequisites: None beyond ordinary school-level mathematics and programming familiarity
Primary syllabus source: Faculty of Technology, University of Delhi — DSC-14 Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning, Unit I
Primary supplied lecture source: lecture-intelligent-agents.pdf
Primary external references: Russell & Norvig, Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach; Stanford HAI terminology explainer; Dartmouth AI history material

How to use this chapter

This is the first real chapter of the Main Book. Read it slowly. Do not try to memorize everything on the first pass. The purpose of this chapter is to give you the mental structure needed for everything that follows.

By the end, you should be able to answer five questions without looking at the page:

  1. What does Artificial Intelligence actually mean?
  2. What makes a system intelligent rather than merely automated?
  3. What is the relationship between AI, Machine Learning, Deep Learning, and Reinforcement Learning?
  4. What are the major areas/applications of AI?
  5. Why do search, agents, learning, vision, language, robotics, and games all appear inside one subject called AI?

1. The First Question: What Is Intelligence?

Before defining artificial intelligence, we need to be careful about the word intelligence.

At a simple level, intelligence is the ability to use information and capabilities to achieve goals, solve problems, learn from experience, adapt to changing situations, and make useful decisions.

This does not mean that intelligence has to look exactly like human thinking.

A bird can navigate without doing algebra.

A person can recognize a familiar face without consciously calculating pixel values.

A chess program can search millions of possible moves without experiencing the game emotionally.

A navigation system can select a route without "understanding" a city in exactly the same way a human does.

So, throughout this book, keep an important distinction in mind:

Intelligent behaviour is the behaviour we care about; human-like internal mechanisms are only one possible way of producing it.

👨‍🏫 Hinglish — teacher explanation
Socho intelligence ka matlab sirf **"insaan jaisa sochna"** nahi hai. Agar ek system ko situation samajh kar useful action lena aata hai, goal achieve karna aata hai, aur uncertainty ke andar decision lena aata hai, to hum us behaviour ko intelligent keh sakte hain — chahe andar se system human brain ki tarah kaam karta ho ya nahi.

2. What Is Artificial Intelligence?

2.1 The beginner definition

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is the field of computing concerned with building systems that can perform tasks requiring capabilities commonly associated with intelligence, such as perception, reasoning, learning, planning, language understanding, decision-making, and problem solving.

This is intentionally broad.

AI is a field, not one algorithm.

There is no single thing called "the AI algorithm."

AI is a large umbrella containing many methods and subfields.

2.2 A more formal way to think about AI

A useful way to organize AI is:

An AI system receives information about some problem or environment, represents or processes that information, and chooses or produces outputs that help accomplish a goal.

That output may be:

  • a decision,
  • an action,
  • a prediction,
  • a plan,
  • a generated response,
  • a classification,
  • a route,
  • a movement of a robot,
  • or a recommendation.

This is why the word goal appears so frequently in AI.

3. AI Through the Agent Lens

Your supplied course lecture introduces AI through intelligent agents. It defines an intelligent agent as a system that perceives its environment through sensors and acts rationally upon it with effectors. The same lecture introduces humans, robots, softbots, and thermostats as examples of agents and represents the agent function as a mapping from percept histories to actions. Course source: lecture-intelligent-agents.pdf, pp. 2–8.

The central picture is:

flowchart LR
    E[Environment] -->|percepts / observations| A[Agent]
    A -->|actions| E
    A --> G[Goal / Performance Measure]
    G --> A

How to read this diagram

Environment

The world in which the system operates.

Examples:

  • road traffic,
  • a chess board,
  • a factory floor,
  • the internet,
  • a game world,
  • a room,
  • a patient's medical data environment.

Percepts / observations

Information available to the agent.

For a self-driving vehicle this might include camera images, lidar measurements, speed, GPS and other sensor data. Your supplied lecture uses exactly this kind of self-driving-car example. Course source: lecture-intelligent-agents.pdf, self-driving-car example.

Agent

The decision-making system.

Actions

What the system can do.

For a car:

  • steer,
  • accelerate,
  • brake,
  • signal,
  • display/speak.

Goal / performance measure

The system needs some criterion for judging whether its behaviour is useful.

For a self-driving car, the course lecture lists goals such as safety, reaching the destination, obeying laws, and passenger comfort. Course source: lecture-intelligent-agents.pdf, self-driving-car example.

4. The Simplest Real-Life Analogy: A Person Crossing a Road

Imagine you want to cross a busy road.

You do not simply say:

"I have a road. Therefore I cross."

You look around.

You notice cars.

You estimate their motion.

You decide whether a gap is large enough.

You move.

While moving, you keep observing the environment.

If a vehicle suddenly approaches, you change your action.

That is an agent-like loop.

Observe → Understand/Estimate → Decide → Act
   ↑                              │
   └────────── New information ───┘
👨‍🏫 Hinglish
Road cross karte time tum ek fixed program follow nahi kar rahe: > "3 seconds rukna hai → exactly 2 metre chalna hai → road cross." Tum continuously situation dekh rahe ho. > Car aa rahi hai? Ruko. > > Gap bada hai? Chalo. > > Car speed badha rahi hai? Phir wait karo. Yeh **environment + perception + decision + action + feedback** ka loop hai.

5. Is a Calculator Intelligent?

Suppose you enter:

\[ 17\times 29 \]

and a calculator returns:

\[ 493. \]

Is that AI?

Not automatically.

The calculator is performing a computation according to a specified procedure. That alone does not make the system an AI system.

This gives us a useful distinction:

System Does it perform computation? Does that alone imply AI?
Calculator Yes No
Ordinary sorting program Yes No
Fixed thermostat Yes Not necessarily
Route planner Yes It can be an AI application
Chess-playing system Yes Common AI example
Image classifier Yes AI/ML example
Robot navigating uncertainty Yes AI example

The exact boundary is not always mathematically sharp. The important lesson is that computation itself is not the definition of intelligence.

6. Automation vs AI

This is one of the easiest concepts to confuse.

Automation

A machine follows a known procedure.

Example:

If temperature < 20°C → turn heater on.

AI-style decision making

A system may have to interpret observations, search among possibilities, estimate uncertainty, learn patterns, or choose an action using a model.

Example:

A robot receives noisy sensor information, determines where it is, predicts obstacles, plans a route, and changes its route when the environment changes.

Important nuance

A system can be highly sophisticated automation without using AI, and AI systems can also contain ordinary deterministic software components.

So do not use:

"It is automatic, therefore it is AI."

That reasoning is incorrect.

The word scope asks:

"What kinds of problems are included within the field?"

For your course, the most important areas are visible directly in Unit I:

  • Vision Systems
  • Search and problem solving

The later units add:

  • Machine Learning
  • Deep Learning
  • Reinforcement Learning
  • Generative Models

So your course is deliberately showing you that AI is much larger than neural networks.

8. The AI Family Tree

This is one of the most important diagrams in the entire book.

flowchart TB
    AI[ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE]
    AI --> SP[Search & Problem Solving]
    AI --> RS[Reasoning / Knowledge]
    AI --> AG[Agents & Robotics]
    AI --> ML[Machine Learning]
    AI --> NLP[Natural Language Processing]
    AI --> CV[Vision]

    ML --> SL[Supervised Learning]
    ML --> UL[Unsupervised Learning]
    ML --> RL[Reinforcement Learning]

    SL --> CL[Classical ML]
    SL --> DL[Deep Learning]
    DL --> NN[Neural Networks]
    NN --> CNN[CNN]
    NN --> RNN[RNN]
    RNN --> LSTM[LSTM]

    ML --> GM[Generative Models]
    GM --> AE[Autoencoders]
    GM --> GAN[GANs]

WARNING: this is a conceptual map, not a rigid taxonomy

Some areas overlap.

For example:

  • robotics can use ML,
  • NLP can use deep learning,
  • computer vision can use CNNs and transformers,
  • reinforcement learning is often treated as part of ML but also sits naturally inside the broader AI problem-solving/agent perspective.

The boxes are there to help you navigate the subject, not to imply that every research paper belongs to one and only one box.

9. AI vs Machine Learning vs Deep Learning

This distinction must become automatic for you.

Artificial Intelligence

The broad field.

Goal: build systems exhibiting useful intelligent behaviour.

Machine Learning

A major approach within AI in which systems learn patterns or decision rules from data or interaction rather than having every behaviour explicitly specified.

Deep Learning

A family of machine-learning methods based on neural networks with multiple layers that learn representations of data.

The mental model

AI
└── ML
    └── Deep Learning
        └── Neural-network architectures

This picture is useful but incomplete because not every AI method is ML and not every ML method is deep learning.

👨‍🏫 Hinglish
AI ko **bada umbrella** samjho. ML AI ke andar ek approach hai: > "Rules manually likhne ke badle data se pattern learn karao." Deep Learning ML ke andar neural-network based approach hai, jahan multiple layers representations learn karti hain.

10. Where Does Reinforcement Learning Fit?

Reinforcement Learning (RL) is another ML paradigm in your syllabus.

Instead of being given the correct answer for every training example, an agent interacts with an environment and receives rewards or penalties.

Example:

A game-playing agent makes a move → receives a reward/penalty → uses experience to improve future decisions.

A simplified picture is:

flowchart LR
    A[Agent] -->|action| E[Environment]
    E -->|state / observation| A
    E -->|reward| A

This explains why RL connects naturally to the agent idea introduced at the start of the course.

11. Four Ways People Historically Tried to Build Intelligent Systems

AI is not one philosophy.

Different periods emphasized different approaches.

Approach A — Symbolic / rule-based reasoning

Idea:

Represent knowledge explicitly and manipulate symbols/rules.

Example:

IF fever AND cough
THEN consider respiratory infection

This does not mean that real medical reasoning can safely be reduced to one rule. It is just a simplified example of rule-based representation.

This family includes expert-system ideas.

Approach B — Search and planning

Idea:

Represent possible states/actions and search for a useful sequence.

Example:

  • chess move selection,
  • route finding,
  • puzzle solving,
  • planning.

This is the part of Unit I you will study in detail through BFS, DFS, Greedy, A* and game playing.

Approach C — Learning from data

Idea:

Instead of writing every decision rule manually, learn a model from examples.

This takes us into Machine Learning.

Approach D — Neural representation learning

Idea:

Use multi-layer neural networks to learn increasingly useful representations from data.

This takes us into Deep Learning.

12. AI Is a Problem-Solving Field

A useful beginner mistake to avoid is thinking:

"AI = predicting things."

Prediction is only one activity.

AI can involve:

Perceive
   ↓
Represent
   ↓
Reason
   ↓
Search / Plan
   ↓
Decide
   ↓
Act
   ↓
Learn from feedback
   ↓
Repeat

Different AI techniques occupy different parts of this loop.

13. Application 1 — Natural Language Processing

What is NLP?

Natural Language Processing (NLP) is the area concerned with enabling computers to process, analyze, interpret, generate, or interact using human language.

Examples:

  • translation,
  • sentiment analysis,
  • spam detection,
  • question answering,
  • text classification,
  • speech/language interfaces,
  • information extraction.

Simple pipeline

flowchart LR
    T[Human Language] --> P[Process / Represent]
    P --> M[Model]
    M --> O[Prediction / Generation]

Real-life analogy

Suppose a friend sends:

"Bhai this phone is amazing! Battery is crazy good."

A human immediately understands a positive opinion.

An NLP system has to transform the language into representations it can process and then perform a task such as sentiment classification.

Later in your syllabus you will study:

  • tokenization,
  • stemming,
  • lemmatization,
  • text classification.

So NLP appears in Unit I as an AI application and later becomes a technical Unit IV topic.

14. Application 2 — Robotics

Robotics combines AI with a physical body.

A robot may need to:

  1. perceive its surroundings,
  2. estimate its state,
  3. understand its task,
  4. plan actions,
  5. move,
  6. detect changes,
  7. react.
flowchart LR
    S[Sensors] --> P[Perception]
    P --> D[Decision / Planning]
    D --> C[Control / Action]
    C --> W[World]
    W --> S
👨‍🏫 Hinglish
Robot ke paas sirf motor hona enough nahi hai. Usko yeh bhi decide karna hai: > "Main kahan hoon?" > > "Obstacle kahan hai?" > > "Mujhe jaana kahan hai?" > > "Kaunsa path safe hai?" > > "Abhi kya action lena hai?" Yahan AI ka agent + perception + planning + decision making wala part kaam karta hai.

15. Application 3 — Expert Systems

An expert system is a classical AI approach that attempts to capture domain knowledge explicitly and use it to reason about cases.

A simplified architecture is:

flowchart LR
    U[User / Case] --> I[Inference Engine]
    KB[Knowledge Base] --> I
    I --> A[Answer / Recommendation]

Knowledge base

Contains domain knowledge.

Inference engine

Uses rules/logic to derive conclusions.

Important distinction

An expert system is not simply:

"a database containing lots of information."

A database stores information.

An expert system also has a mechanism for applying represented knowledge to a problem.

Why this matters

It teaches an important historical AI idea:

Intelligence can be approached through explicit knowledge and reasoning, not only through statistical learning.

16. Application 4 — Vision Systems

Computer vision deals with extracting useful information from images or video.

Example tasks:

  • object recognition,
  • image classification,
  • object detection,
  • segmentation,
  • face-related recognition tasks,
  • medical-image analysis.

A human sees:

"There is a red car near a pedestrian."

A computer initially receives numerical data representing pixels.

Image
  ↓
Pixels / numerical representation
  ↓
Features / learned representations
  ↓
Model
  ↓
Prediction

Later in Unit III, CNNs will provide a central deep-learning approach for image processing.

17. AI as Search: Why Search Belongs Here

Suppose you are solving a maze.

At every intersection, several choices exist.

If you don't know which choice leads to the exit, you have to search.

That is an AI problem.

A state-space can be thought of as:

Start
               /     \
              A       B
             / \     / \
            C   D   E   F
               ...
                 \
                 Goal

AI search asks:

Which state should we explore next so that we find a solution efficiently?

This leads directly to:

  • BFS,
  • DFS,
  • Greedy search,
  • A*,
  • heuristics,
  • game search.

Your supplied Basic Search lecture begins with a problem-solving agent whose process is to formulate a goal, formulate a problem, and search for a solution; the solution is a sequence of actions. Course source: lecture-basic-search.pdf.

So search is not some unrelated algorithm chapter.

It is one of the original ways AI systems were designed to make decisions and solve problems.

18. AI and Games

Why does a chess-playing program belong to AI?

Because the program must choose actions in an environment where:

  • there are multiple possible actions,
  • future consequences depend on current choices,
  • the opponent responds,
  • the best action is not directly given.

That leads to game trees and eventually minimax and alpha-beta pruning.

Your supplied Game Playing lecture explicitly introduces games, perfect play, minimax decisions, alpha-beta pruning, resource limits, games of chance and games with imperfect information. Course source: lecture-game-playing.pdf.

The important mental connection is:

AI is often about choosing a good action when the correct action is not handed to you.

19. Narrow AI vs General AI

Another distinction you should know.

Narrow / task-oriented AI

A system designed for a bounded set of tasks.

Examples include systems specializing in:

  • image classification,
  • route planning,
  • recommendation,
  • speech recognition,
  • game playing.

General AI / AGI

A research concept referring to a much broader form of machine intelligence capable of flexible performance across many kinds of tasks.

Do not treat AGI as a normal synonym for today's AI.

A current deployed AI system can be broad and highly capable without that automatically establishing that it is an artificial general intelligence system.

Exam-safe statement

AI is a broad field; many practical systems are specialized, while AGI refers to a more general research goal/concept.

20. Can an AI System Be Intelligent Without Learning?

Yes.

This is crucial.

Consider a classical route-search system.

If the road map is already provided and the system searches that map using A*, it is doing an AI problem-solving task without learning a new model from examples.

Similarly, a rule-based expert system can reason using encoded rules without machine learning.

Therefore:

AI is not equal to Machine Learning.

Machine Learning is one major approach within AI.

21. Can Machine Learning Exist Without Deep Learning?

Absolutely.

Classical ML includes methods such as:

  • linear regression,
  • logistic regression,
  • decision trees,
  • k-NN,
  • SVMs,
  • clustering methods.

Your Unit II focuses heavily on this classical ML layer.

Deep learning comes later through neural networks.

So the chain is:

AI
│
├── Classical symbolic/search methods
│
└── Machine Learning
     │
     ├── Classical ML
     │
     └── Deep Learning

22. Where Mathematics Enters AI/ML

At this point you should not be frightened by the mathematics.

The math becomes useful because AI/ML needs a way to represent and optimize things.

Linear algebra

Useful for:

  • vectors,
  • data matrices,
  • model parameters,
  • neural networks,
  • image representations.

Your Lecture 4 explicitly explains why ML uses a data vector \(x\in\mathbb{R}^d\), a dataset matrix \(X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}\), and transformations such as \(y=\sigma(Wx+b)\). Course source: Lecture04.pdf, pp. 3–5.

Probability/statistics

Useful for:

  • uncertainty,
  • classification,
  • distributions,
  • expected performance,
  • evaluation,
  • probabilistic models.

Your Lecture 1 introduces sample spaces, events, conditional probability, independence and Bayes rule; Lecture 2 develops random variables, PMF/PDF/CDF and joint/marginal/conditional distributions. [Course sources: Lecture01.pdf, Lecture02.pdf.]

Calculus/optimization

Useful for:

  • minimizing loss,
  • learning parameters,
  • gradient descent,
  • backpropagation.

You will meet this much more heavily in Unit III.

23. The Complete AI Mental Model

At this stage, you should be able to see the subject as a stack rather than a list.

flowchart TB
    Q[REAL-WORLD PROBLEM]
    Q --> F[FORMULATE THE PROBLEM]
    F --> R{How can we solve it?}
    R --> S[SEARCH / PLANNING]
    R --> K[KNOWLEDGE + REASONING]
    R --> L[LEARN FROM DATA]
    R --> A[ACT IN ENVIRONMENT]

    L --> CML[CLASSICAL ML]
    L --> DL[DEEP LEARNING]
    L --> RL[REINFORCEMENT LEARNING]

    DL --> N[NEURAL NETWORKS]
    N --> CV[CNN / VISION]
    N --> SEQ[RNN / LSTM / SEQUENCE]

The key idea

AI is not a collection of unrelated chapters.

They are different answers to the same broad question:

How can a computational system use information to achieve useful goals in difficult environments?

24. Teacher's Hinglish Summary

AI ko ek bade toolbox ki tarah socho.

Problem alag ho to tool alag ho sakta hai.

Agar problem hai:

"Mujhe maze se bahar nikalna hai."

Use search.

Agar problem hai:

"Mere paas bahut saare examples hain; pattern seekhna hai."

Use machine learning.

Agar data image hai aur complex visual patterns hain:

Use deep learning / CNNs jaisi techniques.

Agar agent ko baar-baar environment mein action lena hai aur reward se improve karna hai:

Use reinforcement learning.

Agar problem language ki hai:

Use NLP.

Agar physical world mein action lena hai:

Use robotics + AI.

Agar expert knowledge ko rules ke form mein represent karna hai:

Use expert-system style reasoning.

Agar opponent bhi moves kar raha hai:

Use game playing / adversarial search.

So AI ko ek sentence mein yaad rakho:

AI is the broad field; search, reasoning, learning, perception, language, planning and action are different tools for creating useful intelligent behaviour.

25. What AI Is NOT — Common Misconceptions

⚠️ Misconception 1
**"AI means robots."** False. Robots are physical systems; AI can exist entirely in software.
⚠️ Misconception 2
**"AI = machine learning."** False. ML is one major approach within AI.
⚠️ Misconception 3
**"Deep learning = all machine learning."** False. Linear regression, decision trees, k-NN and many other ML methods are not deep learning.
⚠️ Misconception 4
**"Anything automatic is AI."** False. Automation may follow fixed rules without the kind of adaptive problem-solving associated with AI.
⚠️ Misconception 5
**"An AI must think exactly like a human."** False. AI can produce intelligent behaviour through mechanisms very different from human cognition.
⚠️ Misconception 6
**"AI always learns from data."** False. Classical search, rule-based reasoning and planning can operate without statistical learning from datasets.

26. A Small Mathematical Example: AI as a Function

Your course's agent lecture represents an agent function as a mapping from percept histories to actions:

\[ f:P^*\rightarrow A \]

where:

  • \(P\) represents percepts,
  • \(P^*\) represents a history/sequence of percepts,
  • \(A\) represents possible actions.

Do not worry if \(P^*\) looks unfamiliar.

It simply means:

The agent's action can depend on everything it has observed so far, not necessarily only the latest observation.

👨‍🏫 Hinglish
Agar robot ko sirf current information hi dekh kar decision lena hota, to woh limited ho sakta hai. Lekin agar usko pichhle observations bhi yaad hain, to woh history ko use kar sakta hai. Example: 
Observation 1 → Observation 2 → Observation 3
                                      ↓
                              Current decision
This idea becomes important later when we talk about memory and sequential models.

37. History of AI — First-Pass Timeline

Your syllabus explicitly requires History and Evolution of AI, so we need a timeline. For now, learn the milestones conceptually; detailed historical depth can be expanded later if your professor expects it.

1940s–50s
Foundations of computing, cybernetics, logic and early machine intelligence research
        ↓
1950
Turing asks the question: can machines imitate intelligent behaviour?
        ↓
1955
John McCarthy uses the term "artificial intelligence" in the proposal for the Dartmouth project
        ↓
1956
Dartmouth Summer Research Project — AI becomes established as a named research field
        ↓
1950s–60s
Early symbolic AI, game playing, theorem proving, search and neural models
        ↓
1970s–80s
Expert systems and knowledge-based approaches become prominent
        ↓
1980s–90s
Machine learning and neural-network research develop further; statistical methods become increasingly important
        ↓
1997
IBM Deep Blue defeats Garry Kasparov in a match under standard match conditions
        ↓
2010s
Large datasets + GPUs + deep learning produce major advances in vision, speech and other tasks
        ↓
2016
AlphaGo's success demonstrates the power of combining learning and search in a complex game
        ↓
2017 onward
Transformer architecture becomes an important foundation for modern language and multimodal systems
        ↓
2020s
Large-scale foundation models and generative AI become central areas of AI development

IBM's maintained history overview gives a broader historical timeline into modern AI and generative AI. 🔗 Source Reference
