MAIN-U1-C10 — Game Playing

Main Book chapter: Unit I — Artificial Intelligence
Topic ID: AI-U1-C10
Primary source: lecture-game-playing.pdf — Philipp Koehn, Artificial Intelligence: Game Playing, 17 February 2026
Prerequisites: MAIN-U1-C02 Intelligent Agents, MAIN-U1-C03 Problem Formulation, MAIN-U1-C04 Uninformed Search

1. Concept Identity

What are we learning?

Until now, search looked like a problem in which the world was waiting for us:

“Here is the state. Here are the possible actions. Find a path to the goal.”

Game playing changes that situation.

Now there is another decision-maker who is actively choosing actions against us.

That changes the question from:

“Which path should I take?”

to:

“Which action should I take when another intelligent player will respond to my action?”

That single change is the foundation of adversarial search.

2. Why Does Game Playing Need a Special Kind of Search?

Imagine you are playing chess.

You have several legal moves. You choose one. Then your opponent gets to choose one. Their choice changes the position you will face next.

So your plan cannot simply be:

My move → My move → My move → Win

because the opponent gets a turn in between:

My move
   ↓
Opponent move
   ↓
My move
   ↓
Opponent move
   ↓
...

The supplied game-playing lecture makes the key point that when the opponent is unpredictable from your perspective, a solution is a strategy specifying a move for every possible opponent reply. fileciteturn18file0L1-L9

That is why game-playing search is not just ordinary path search.

3. One-Minute Intuition

In ordinary search, you choose the path.

In adversarial search, you choose a strategy while an opponent chooses responses.

Think about playing a competitive game with a friend.

If your friend always makes exactly the move you want them to make, the game is easy to plan.

But a rational opponent will deliberately choose the response that hurts your plan.

So you must ask:

“What is the strongest response my opponent could make?”

and then choose your action accordingly.

That is the mental bridge to MINIMAX.

4. Teacher Explanation — English

A game is a sequential decision problem in which multiple agents take actions and the resulting state depends on those actions.

In a classical deterministic two-player game, players alternate turns. Each player wants a favourable final outcome, but their objectives conflict.

Therefore, when choosing an action, a player must consider not only the consequences of that action under their own future choices but also the consequences of the opponent's best responses.

The search tree therefore contains alternating decision layers:

  • one layer where we choose the action that helps us most;
  • another layer where the opponent chooses the action that helps them most, which is usually worst for us.

This produces MAX and MIN nodes and eventually the minimax decision rule.

5. Teacher Explanation — Hinglish

Normal search mein tum soch rahe the:

“Mujhe A se B jaana hai. Kaunsa path best hai?”

Game mein problem badal gayi:

“Main ek move karunga, saamne wala mujhe counter karega, phir mujhe us counter ka bhi answer dena padega.”

Matlab tumhe sirf apna move nahi, opponent ka strongest move bhi imagine karna hai.

Isliye game tree mein levels alternate hote hain:

Tumhara decision
      ↓
Opponent ka decision
      ↓
Tumhara decision
      ↓
Opponent ka decision

Simple mental rule:

“Main apne liye best move choose karta hoon, aur opponent mere liye worst response choose karega.”

Yehi MINIMAX ka core idea hai.

6. Real-World Analogy — Negotiation

Suppose you are negotiating the price of a bike.

You think:

“Main ₹80,000 offer karunga.”

But the other person also thinks:

“Main maximum price lena chahta hoon.”

You cannot evaluate your offer by assuming they will cooperate.

You must mentally simulate:

My offer
   ↓
Their strongest counter
   ↓
My response
   ↓
Their strongest counter

The analogy is not identical to formal game theory, but it helps you develop the correct intuition:

An intelligent opponent must be included in your planning.

7. Games vs Ordinary Search Problems

The supplied lecture contrasts ordinary search and games in an important way.

Ordinary search

The solution can often be represented as a single sequence of actions:

A → B → C → D → Goal

Game search

A single sequence is not enough because the opponent can respond differently.

You need something closer to a conditional strategy:

IF opponent does X → I do A
IF opponent does Y → I do B
IF opponent does Z → I do C

The exact solution therefore becomes a strategy tree rather than one predetermined path.

The lecture also notes that time limits make exact search impractical, so practical game-playing systems often use finite horizons and approximate evaluation. fileciteturn18file0L4-L10

8. Game Classification

A useful way to understand games is to classify them using two major questions:

  1. Is there chance?
  2. Do players have perfect information?

The supplied lecture gives this classification: fileciteturn18file0L4-L10

Deterministic Chance / stochastic
Perfect information Chess, Checkers, Go, Othello Backgammon, Monopoly
Imperfect information Battleships, Bridge, Poker, Scrabble Can combine hidden information and chance in many real games

What does this mean?

Deterministic: once the current state and action are known, the next state is determined.

Chance: random events such as dice or card shuffling affect the next state.

Perfect information: the relevant game state is visible to the players.

Imperfect information: some important information is hidden.

9. Why Chess Is a Useful AI Example

Chess is useful because it has:

  • many possible moves,
  • alternating players,
  • a huge game tree,
  • a clear objective,
  • and a strong requirement to reason about future responses.

The lecture gives an approximate chess branching factor of about 35 and game depths around 100 for reasonable games, making exhaustive exact search infeasible. fileciteturn18file0L9-L10

This motivates the tools we study next:

Game tree
   ↓
Minimax
   ↓
Alpha-beta pruning
   ↓
Depth limits
   ↓
Evaluation functions

10. Formal Game Representation

For a simplified two-player deterministic game, we can describe a state using:

  • current game position;
  • whose turn it is;
  • legal actions;
  • transition rule;
  • terminal test;
  • utility/payoff.

A useful conceptual representation is:

GAME STATE
                    │
          ┌─────────┴─────────┐
          │                   │
      Legal actions       Player to move
          │
          ▼
     Successor states
          │
          ▼
      Next player's turn

Each successor becomes a branch in the game tree.

11. Game Tree

A game tree is a tree representing possible sequences of moves.

For a simple two-player game:

START
                        │
              ┌─────────┴─────────┐
              │                   │
            Move A              Move B
              │                   │
        ┌─────┴─────┐       ┌─────┴─────┐
        │           │       │           │
      Opp-1       Opp-2   Opp-3       Opp-4
        │           │       │           │
       ...         ...     ...         ...

The levels alternate between players.

A game tree is therefore a search tree with adversarial decision layers.

12. MAX and MIN

To reason about two competing players, we often use two conceptual roles:

MAX

The player whose utility we are evaluating.

MAX wants the value to be as large as possible.

MIN

The opponent.

MIN wants the value to be as small as possible from MAX's perspective.

This does not mean that one player is literally always called “MAX” in every game. It is a mathematical viewpoint used to evaluate the tree.

13. Utility / Payoff

At the end of a game, we need some notion of outcome.

For a simple game we might define:

Win  → +1
Draw →  0
Loss → -1

Then a terminal state gets a numerical utility.

Example:

Terminal state
     │
     ├── Win  → +1
     ├── Draw →  0
     └── Loss → -1

The numerical values are just a convenient representation of preference ordering.

Your supplied lecture later emphasizes that, in deterministic games, only the ordering of evaluation values matters under monotonic transformations. fileciteturn18file0L21-L30

14. Why the Opponent Changes Search

Consider this ordinary search-style thought:

“Move A eventually reaches +10, so A is good.”

A game player must ask:

“Will the opponent allow me to reach +10?”

If the opponent can choose a response that changes the outcome to -8, then evaluating only our preferred continuation is meaningless.

The opponent's best response must be incorporated into the value of our move.

That is why game search alternates maximization and minimization.

15. Perfect Play

A perfect-play strategy is one that assumes both players act optimally.

The supplied lecture describes minimax as the method for perfect play in deterministic, perfect-information games. fileciteturn18file0L8-L10

The central assumption is:

If I choose an action, my opponent will respond in the way that is best for them.

This leads directly to C11.

16. When Exact Search Becomes Impossible

The game tree can grow exponentially because each move may have many legal actions.

A rough tree-size intuition is:

\[ \text{number of nodes} \approx b^m \]

where:

  • \(b\) = branching factor;
  • \(m\) = search depth.

Even a moderately large branching factor makes deep exact search huge.

The lecture illustrates this using chess and concludes that exact exhaustive solution is completely infeasible for such search depths. fileciteturn18file0L9-L10

17. Practical Solution — Search Only as Deep as You Can

When time or computation is limited, we cannot always reach a terminal game state.

The supplied lecture's standard practical approach is:

TERMINAL-TEST
      ↓
   replace with
      ↓
 CUTOFF-TEST

UTILITY
   ↓
replace with
   ↓
 EVAL

So instead of asking:

“Who eventually wins if we continue all the way to the end?”

the system asks:

“What is the estimated quality of this position at the depth we can afford?”

fileciteturn18file0L21-L25

18. Evaluation Function

An evaluation function estimates the desirability of a non-terminal game state.

Conceptually:

\[ \boxed{Eval(s) \approx \text{desirability of state }s} \]

For chess, the lecture gives an example of a weighted feature sum:

\[ \boxed{Eval(s)=w_1f_1(s)+w_2f_2(s)+\cdots+w_nf_n(s)} \]

where the features may represent things such as differences in piece counts or positional characteristics. fileciteturn18file0L22-L25

👨‍🏫 Hinglish
> Terminal utility bolega **“end mein kya result hua?”** > > Evaluation function bolega **“abhi jo position hai, woh roughly kitni achhi/buri lag rahi hai?”**

19. Evaluation Function Analogy — Sports Scoreboard

Imagine a cricket match that is not finished yet.

You might estimate the current position using:

  • runs scored;
  • wickets remaining;
  • overs remaining;
  • required run rate;
  • batting resources.

You are not observing the final result yet.

You are estimating who is currently in the stronger position.

That is the role of an evaluation function in a game-search system.

Again, the analogy is only intuition; the formal function depends on the game.

20. Learning an Evaluation Function

The supplied lecture explains that evaluation functions can also be learned from game data.

A simplified workflow is:

Large collection of games
          ↓
Record positions + game outcomes
          ↓
Extract features
          ↓
Learn weights
          ↓
Evaluation function

The lecture also mentions learning evaluation functions through self-play. fileciteturn18file0L24-L25

This is an important bridge between classical AI and machine learning:

Search can provide the decision structure, while ML can help learn how to evaluate states.

21. Resource Limits and the Horizon Effect

Depth-limited game search introduces a new danger.

Suppose the search stops at depth 4 and the position at depth 4 appears excellent.

But immediately after that cutoff, a major loss occurs.

The algorithm may fail to see the danger because the dangerous event is beyond the horizon it searched.

This is called the horizon effect.

The lecture defines it as a situation in which an adverse outcome can be delayed but not avoided, yet limited search may prefer the delay. fileciteturn18file0L25-L27

👨‍🏫 Hinglish
> Tum future ko sirf ek fixed distance tak dekh pa rahe ho. Problem us distance ke just bahar chhupi hui hai.

22. Quiescence

Another concern is evaluating an unstable position too early.

For example, a chess position might look good numerically, but a queen could be lost on the next move.

The lecture calls for deeper search of game-changing moves in such unstable positions. fileciteturn18file0L25-L27

The idea is:

Do not evaluate a position too early when important tactical changes are still unfolding.

23. Forward Pruning

The lecture also discusses forward pruning:

stop exploring clearly bad-looking moves before reaching the maximum depth.

This can save computation, but it is risky because a move that initially looks inferior may lead to a much better later position. fileciteturn18file0L27-L28

This gives a useful general lesson:

Any shortcut that discards possibilities before proving they are irrelevant can introduce errors.

Alpha-beta is different: under the standard minimax assumptions, its pruning is safe and does not change the final minimax result.

24. Lookup Instead of Search

Not every game decision needs to be recomputed from scratch.

The lecture describes:

  • opening-move libraries;
  • endgame databases.

These can replace search/evaluation for situations that have already been analysed or solved. fileciteturn18file0L27-L30

This is a useful systems idea:

If a difficult subproblem can be precomputed, store the answer and avoid recomputing it.

25. Games of Chance

So far we assumed deterministic transitions.

But many games include randomness:

  • dice;
  • card shuffling;
  • random draws.

The lecture uses backgammon and coin-flipping as examples of nondeterministic games. fileciteturn18file0L31-L34

Now the tree has a third type of node:

MAX node
   ↓
MIN node
   ↓
CHANCE node

At a chance node, we cannot simply choose the best outcome because the random event chooses the outcome according to its probability.

This leads to EXPECTIMINIMAX.

26. Expectiminimax

The supplied lecture gives the rule:

MAX node

Take the maximum successor value.

MIN node

Take the minimum successor value.

Chance node

Take the probability-weighted average successor value.

For equally likely outcomes:

\[ V(s)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}V(s_i) \]

For unequal probabilities:

\[ V(s)=\sum_i P(s_i\mid s) V(s_i) \]

The lecture's algorithm describes the chance-node value as the average of successor expectiminimax values in the simplified equally likely case. fileciteturn18file0L31-L35

👨‍🏫 Hinglish
> Chance node par tum “best” ya “worst” directly choose nahi karte. Tum probability ke hisaab se **expected value** nikalte ho.

27. Imperfect Information

Some games hide important information.

Examples in the lecture include card games where an opponent's cards are unknown. fileciteturn2file7L1-L8

In these settings, the player does not know the exact world state.

Instead, decision quality may depend on an information state or belief state.

That changes the nature of the search problem again.

You may need to reason about:

What I know
+ What I do not know
+ What I believe may be true
+ How my actions affect future information

The lecture notes that rational behaviour can include acting to obtain information, signalling, or deliberately randomizing to reduce information disclosure. fileciteturn2file7L1-L8

28. Why Poker Is Hard

The supplied lecture uses computer poker as an example because it combines several difficulties:

  • imperfect information;
  • stochastic outcomes;
  • partial observability;
  • multiple non-cooperative players;
  • bluffing and deception.

It therefore cannot be treated like a simple deterministic perfect-information game. fileciteturn2file7L1-L8

29. Big Picture — Evolution of Game AI

The supplied lecture gives a short historical progression:

Consider possible lines of play
        ↓
Perfect-play algorithms
        ↓
Finite-horizon approximate evaluation
        ↓
Early chess programs
        ↓
Machine learning for better evaluation
        ↓
Pruning for deeper search

The lecture attributes milestones to Babbage, Zermelo, von Neumann, Zuse, Wiener, Shannon, Turing, Samuel and McCarthy across the historical sequence. fileciteturn18file0L4-L10

The important conceptual evolution is more useful than memorising every date:

Search → approximation → pruning → learned evaluation.

30. Deep Connection to Machine Learning

This chapter connects directly to later ML topics.

The supplied lecture explicitly describes learning evaluation weights from game databases and self-play. fileciteturn18file0L24-L25

So you should recognize this pattern:

\[ \text{Game state} \rightarrow \text{features} \rightarrow \text{evaluation} \rightarrow \text{decision} \]

Later in ML, we will study models that learn functions mapping inputs/features to outputs.

A game-playing evaluation function is an important early example of that idea.

31. Comparison — Ordinary Search vs Game Search

Property Ordinary Search Game Search
Decision-makers Usually one agent Multiple competing agents
Solution Path/action sequence Strategy/contingent plan
Opponent No Yes
Typical tree Search tree Game tree
Evaluation Goal/path cost Utility / evaluation function
Main challenge Search-space size Search-space size + opponent responses
Core methods BFS, DFS, A* Minimax, alpha-beta

32. Comparison — Deterministic vs Chance vs Hidden Information

Situation Main difficulty Typical method/idea
Deterministic + perfect information Opponent choice Minimax
Deterministic + perfect information + limited compute Tree too large Depth cutoff + evaluation + alpha-beta
Chance Random transitions Expectiminimax
Imperfect information Unknown state Information/belief-state reasoning

33. Common Misconceptions

Misconception 1

“Minimax means I try to minimise my own score.”

Wrong.

MIN is a viewpoint representing the opponent's action choice. From MAX's perspective, MIN tries to reduce MAX's eventual value.

Misconception 2

“A game solution is one sequence of moves.”

Not generally.

A robust strategy must account for possible opponent replies.

Misconception 3

“Evaluation function gives the guaranteed final result.”

No.

It estimates the current state's desirability when the full game cannot be searched to the end.

Misconception 4

“Alpha-beta changes minimax's answer.”

Under standard minimax assumptions, alpha-beta prunes computations while preserving the final minimax value. fileciteturn18file0L18-L20

Misconception 5

“A game with chance is just minimax with random guessing.”

No.

Chance nodes use probabilities and expected values.

Q2

Q3

B. Small calculation

Suppose three possible random outcomes have utilities:

\[ 10, \quad 4, \quad -2 \]

and probabilities:

\[ 0.5, \quad 0.3, \quad 0.2 \]

Calculate the expected value.

\[ E[V]=0.5(10)+0.3(4)+0.2(-2) \]
\[ =5+1.2-0.4=5.8 \]

So the expected value is:

\[ \boxed{5.8} \]
