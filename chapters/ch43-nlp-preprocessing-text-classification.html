1. Why This Chapter Exists

A computer does not naturally receive a sentence as “meaningful language.”

A human sees:

“The students are learning artificial intelligence.”

A machine-learning system eventually needs a numerical representation it can process.

NLP—Natural Language Processing—is the broad area concerned with computational processing of human language.

For this chapter, the syllabus path is narrower:

raw text
  ↓
preprocessing
  ↓
tokens / normalized text
  ↓
numerical representation
  ↓
classifier
  ↓
predicted class
  ↓
evaluation

The chapter focuses on the preprocessing steps explicitly named in the course guide and then builds a complete text-classification pipeline.

3. What Is NLP?

Natural Language Processing is the field of AI and machine learning that deals with computational representation, analysis and generation of human language.

Examples:

  • spam detection;
  • sentiment classification;
  • topic classification;
  • language modelling;
  • information extraction;
  • question answering;
  • machine translation;
  • speech/language applications.

For this chapter, think of NLP as a transformation problem:

\[ \boxed{ \text{text} \rightarrow \text{representation} \rightarrow \text{decision} } \]

4. The One-Minute Intuition

Imagine giving a teacher 10,000 handwritten answers and asking:

“Which answers are positive, negative, neutral, or abusive?”

The teacher can read words.

A classical ML algorithm cannot directly manipulate a raw sentence as if it understood language.

So we build a pipeline:

sentence
   ↓
split into useful pieces
   ↓
clean / normalize where appropriate
   ↓
represent pieces numerically
   ↓
learn patterns associated with labels
   ↓
predict label for new text

The biggest idea is:

Text classification is supervised learning on a numerical representation of language.

5. Teacher Explanation — English

Before a machine-learning model can classify text, we must decide what counts as the model's input.

Words, subwords or characters may become tokens.

Tokens may be normalized.

The collection of documents can then be converted into numerical features such as word counts or TF-IDF values, or into learned representations such as embeddings.

A classifier learns a mapping:

\[ \text{representation} \rightarrow \text{class label}. \]

Good NLP therefore depends not only on the classifier but also on the representation and preprocessing decisions.

6. Teacher Explanation — Hinglish

Computer ke liye sentence directly waise useful nahi hota jaise human ke liye hota hai.

Hume sentence ko pieces mein todna, zarurat ke hisaab se normalize karna, aur phir numbers mein represent karna padta hai.

Phir model seekhta hai:

yeh numerical pattern
        ↓
spam

ya:

yeh pattern
        ↓
positive sentiment

Important point:

Preprocessing khud classification nahi hai.

Preprocessing input ko usable form deta hai; classifier prediction seekhta hai.

8. Tokenization — The First Essential Step

8.1 Definition

Tokenization is the process of dividing text into units called tokens.

A token might be:

  • a word;
  • a subword;
  • a character;
  • or another task-specific unit.

For a simple word tokenizer:

"Machine learning is useful"

may become:

["Machine", "learning", "is", "useful"]

The tokenizer determines what the model can treat as a separate unit.

9. Why Tokenization Is Not Trivial

Consider:

"I can't attend."

Possible tokenization choices include:

["I", "can't", "attend", "."]

or a more decomposed representation:

["I", "ca", "n't", "attend", "."]

A social-media sentence introduces more cases:

"Great!!! 😭 #examday"

Questions appear immediately:

  • Should punctuation be separate?
  • Should emojis be preserved?
  • Should hashtags be split?
  • Should URLs become special tokens?
  • Should contractions remain whole?

There is therefore no universal tokenization rule that is best for every task.

10. Tokenization Analogy

Imagine a recipe written as one giant string:

2cupsflour1cuppani1spoonsalt...

A human cook would first separate useful units:

2 cups | flour | 1 cup | water | 1 spoon | salt

Tokenization does something analogous for language.

The exact “cut points” matter because later stages operate on the resulting pieces.

11. Types of Tokenization

Word-level

"deep learning"
→ [deep, learning]

Easy to understand, but rare/unseen words can be a problem.

Character-level

"cat"
→ [c, a, t]

Vocabulary can be small, but sequences become much longer.

Subword-level

A word may be decomposed into smaller meaningful or frequent pieces.

Subword approaches reduce the out-of-vocabulary problem while keeping sequences shorter than pure character-level representations in many settings.

Modern NLP systems frequently use subword-style tokenization, but the syllabus focus here is the underlying preprocessing idea rather than one specific tokenizer implementation.

12. Normalization — Making Representation Consistent

Normalization means applying transformations that reduce unnecessary variation.

Possible examples include:

  • converting case;
  • standardizing whitespace;
  • normalizing certain punctuation;
  • handling repeated symbols;
  • mapping known variants to a common form.

Example:

"Machine Learning"
"machine learning"
"MACHINE LEARNING"

A lowercasing step could map all three to:

"machine learning"

But normalization is task-dependent.

Case can carry information.

For example:

US
us

can mean different things.

Therefore:

Do not normalize information away merely because it looks inconvenient.

13. Stemming

⚠️ Misconception — Common Exam Trap
**Mistake:** Stemming aur Lemmatization ek hi cheez hain. **Reality:** Dono mein zameen-aasmaan ka farak hai: - **Stemming (Rule-based chopping):** Porter/Snowball stemmer bas end ke letters kat deta hai bina dictionary check kiye. Example: `"better"` $\to$ `"better"`, `"universe"` $\to$ `"univers"`. Yeh grammatically galat words bana sakta hai. - **Lemmatization (Vocabulary & Morphological analysis):** WordNet dictionary aur Part-of-Speech (POS) tag use karta hai. Example: `"better"` $\to$ `"good"`, `"running"` (verb) $\to$ `"run"`. Yeh hamesha meaningful valid root word (lemma) deta hai.

14. Stemming Analogy

Think of stemming as quickly chopping different forms toward a shared root-like piece.

walking
walked
walker
walks
  ↓
common reduced form

The method is often:

fast
mechanical
rule-based

but can create forms that are linguistically awkward.

15. Lemmatization

15.1 Definition

Lemmatization maps a word to a linguistically meaningful base form called its lemma.

For example, depending on the linguistic analysis:

am
is
are
was
were

may be mapped to:

be

Similarly:

better

may be mapped to a base form such as:

good

when the lemmatizer uses the appropriate linguistic information.

16. Stemming vs Lemmatization

This comparison is directly exam-relevant.

Property Stemming Lemmatization
Main idea reduce word mechanically map to linguistic base form
Typical mechanism rules / suffix reduction morphology + linguistic information
Output may not be a valid word intended to be a valid lemma
Computational cost usually lower usually higher
Language awareness relatively limited greater
Best described as aggressive normalization linguistically informed normalization

Memory anchor:

Stem = chopped-down form. Lemma = dictionary-style base form.

17. Worked Example — Stemming vs Lemmatization

Suppose the input is:

"The children were running quickly."

Possible lemmatized representation might include:

child
be
run
quickly

A stemmer may produce mechanically reduced forms that are less natural.

The exact output depends on the algorithm and language.

Therefore, in an exam, explain the principle, not a claimed universal stemmer output.

18. Stop Words — Useful Idea, Not a Universal Rule

Stop words are very frequent words that are sometimes removed from a bag-of-words style representation.

Examples in English may include:

is
am
the
of
and

A classical pipeline may remove them to reduce vocabulary size.

But automatic removal can destroy information.

Consider:

"not good"

If “not” is removed, we might retain:

"good"

which changes the meaning.

Therefore:

Stop-word removal is a preprocessing choice, not a law of NLP.

19. Punctuation, Numbers, URLs and Emojis

Preprocessing must consider the task.

For sentiment analysis:

"good!!!"

may contain useful intensity information.

For financial text:

₹10,000
+3.5%

numbers may be essential.

For social-media classification:

😂
🔥
#winning

may carry strong signal.

For spam detection:

http://...

may be informative.

So:

Cleaning text means deciding which information is noise and which information is signal.

20. From Tokens to Numbers

A classifier expects numerical features.

Suppose we have documents:

D1: "machine learning is useful"
D2: "deep learning is useful"
D3: "machine vision is useful"

A vocabulary might be:

[deep, is, learning, machine, useful, vision]

Now each document can be represented as a vector.

For example:

D1 → [0,1,1,1,1,0]
D2 → [1,1,1,0,1,0]
D3 → [0,1,0,1,1,1]

This is the basic bag-of-words (BoW) idea.

21. Bag of Words

Definition

Bag of Words represents a document using counts or binary indicators of vocabulary items, while largely ignoring word order.

For vocabulary:

[cat, dog, eats]

sentence:

"dog eats dog"

count vector:

[0, 2, 1]

The representation knows:

  • dog appeared twice;
  • eats appeared once.

But it does not directly preserve the original sequence.

22. Why “Bag”?

Imagine putting all words into a bag and forgetting the order in which they entered.

"dog bites man"

and

"man bites dog"

contain the same individual words.

A pure unigram BoW representation can therefore be identical for the two sentences.

This demonstrates a major limitation:

Bag-of-words captures occurrence information but loses much of the word-order structure.

23. Binary Presence vs Count Representation

For vocabulary:

[apple, banana, orange]

Document:

apple banana banana

Binary representation

[1,1,0]

Count representation

[1,2,0]

The correct representation depends on the model and task.

24. N-Grams — Reintroducing Some Order

An n-gram is a contiguous sequence of \(n\) tokens.

For:

"machine learning is useful"

Unigrams

machine
learning
is
useful

Bigrams

machine learning
learning is
is useful

Trigrams

machine learning is
learning is useful

N-grams preserve some local word-order information.

But increasing \(n\) can dramatically increase the feature space.

25. TF-IDF — Giving More Weight to Informative Words

Simple frequency can overvalue words that occur in nearly every document.

Suppose every document contains:

"the"

Then “the” does not distinguish documents well.

TF-IDF addresses this by combining:

  • term frequency (TF);
  • inverse document frequency (IDF).

A common IDF form is:

\[ \boxed{ IDF(t)=\log\frac{N}{df(t)} } \]

where:

  • \(N\) = number of documents;
  • \(df(t)\) = number of documents containing term \(t\).

A common TF-IDF form is:

\[ \boxed{ TFIDF(t,d)=TF(t,d)\times IDF(t) } \]

Definitions vary slightly between implementations because of smoothing and TF normalization choices.

26. TF-IDF Intuition

Think of a classroom vocabulary.

If everyone uses the word:

"student"

it tells you little about which assignment a student is discussing.

If only a few documents use:

"backpropagation"

that word may be much more informative for distinguishing technical documents.

Hence:

frequent in one document
        +
rare across the document collection
        ↓
high discriminative value

This is the intuition behind TF-IDF.

27. TF-IDF Worked Example

PYTHON
import numpy as np

def compute_tf_idf(corpus):
    """Pure NumPy calculation of TF-IDF matrix for a collection of documents."""
    tokenized = [doc.lower().split() for doc in corpus]
    vocab = sorted(list(set(word for doc in tokenized for word in doc)))
    word_to_idx = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(vocab)}
    N = len(corpus)
    
    tf = np.zeros((N, len(vocab)))
    df = np.zeros(len(vocab))
    
    for i, doc in enumerate(tokenized):
        for word in doc:
            tf[i, word_to_idx[word]] += 1
        tf[i] = tf[i] / len(doc)  # Normalized term frequency
        for word in set(doc):
            df[word_to_idx[word]] += 1
            
    # Smooth IDF formulation: log(N / (1 + df))
    idf = np.log((N + 1) / (1 + df)) + 1.0
    tfidf = tf * idf
    
    # L2 normalize rows
    norms = np.linalg.norm(tfidf, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    norms[norms == 0] = 1.0
    return tfidf / norms, vocab

docs = ["machine learning is intelligent", "deep learning is powerful learning"]
mat, vocab = compute_tf_idf(docs)
print("Vocabulary:", vocab)
print("TF-IDF Matrix Shape:", mat.shape)
print("Doc 0 TF-IDF:
", np.round(mat[0], 3))

Suppose there are:

\[ N=100 \]

documents.

A term appears in:

\[ df(t)=10 \]

documents.

Ignoring smoothing:

\[ IDF(t)=\log\frac{100}{10}=\log(10). \]

If the term frequency in a particular document is:

\[ TF(t,d)=3, \]

then:

\[ TFIDF(t,d)=3\log(10). \]

The exact numerical value depends on the logarithm base and TF convention; what matters conceptually is that the term gets a weight larger than a term occurring in almost every document.

28. Vector Similarity — Cosine Similarity

Once text is represented as vectors, we can measure similarity.

The cosine similarity between vectors \(x\) and \(y\) is:

\[ \boxed{ \cos\theta = \frac{x^Ty}{\|x\|_2\|y\|_2} } \]

Interpretation:

  • near 1 → similar direction;
  • near 0 → little directional similarity;
  • near -1 → opposite direction in settings where negative coordinates occur.

For ordinary nonnegative bag-of-words/TF-IDF vectors, values are typically in \([0,1]\).

Cosine similarity is especially useful because document length can differ while the directional pattern remains informative.

Definition

Text classification is the supervised-learning task of assigning one or more labels to a text item.

Examples:

Let:

\[ D=\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n} \]

We learn a function:

\[ \hat f:x\rightarrow y. \]

This connects directly to the supervised-learning framework from Unit II.

Suppose training examples are:

32. Which Classifier Can Be Used?

Text classification does not require one universal classifier.

Possible choices include:

  • logistic regression;
  • Naive Bayes;
  • support vector machines;
  • decision trees / ensembles;
  • neural networks;
  • sequence models;
  • other learned architectures.

For a first classical baseline, logistic regression with TF-IDF features is an especially useful conceptual example because it directly connects Unit IV NLP to the Unit II material on logistic regression.

This does not mean logistic regression is the only suitable text classifier.

33. Connection to Logistic Regression

Suppose the text has been transformed into a vector:

\[ x\in\mathbb R^d. \]

For binary classification, logistic regression computes:

\[ z=w^Tx+b \]

followed by:

\[ \boxed{ \hat p=\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}} } \]

Then a threshold may be used to convert the probability into a class prediction.

So the NLP pipeline becomes:

text
 ↓
TF-IDF vector x
 ↓
wᵀx + b
 ↓
sigmoid
 ↓
probability of class

This is a powerful cross-unit connection.

34. Multi-Class Text Classification

If there are more than two classes, a common approach is to produce one score per class.

For classes:

sports
technology
finance

we may use a score vector:

\[ z\in\mathbb R^K. \]

Softmax converts scores into a probability distribution:

\[ \boxed{ P(y=k\mid x)= \frac{e^{z_k}} {\sum_{j=1}^{K}e^{z_j}} } \]

Then:

\[ \sum_kP(y=k\mid x)=1. \]

The class with the largest predicted probability can be selected as the predicted class in a standard multiclass setup.

35. Why Text Classification Is More Than Word Matching

Suppose a classifier sees:

"not good"

A naive unigram representation produces:

not

 good

But the meaning comes from their relationship.

Now compare:

"good"
"not good"

A purely frequency-based system can struggle when the representation fails to capture composition or context.

This motivates:

  • n-grams;
  • richer features;
  • word embeddings;
  • sequence models;
  • modern contextual language models.

The important progression is:

word counts
  ↓
local word combinations
  ↓
learned vector representations
  ↓
context-sensitive models

Only the earlier stages are required for the core preprocessing path here; later stages are included as conceptual bridges.

36. Word Embeddings — Bridge to Neural NLP

An embedding maps a token to a dense vector.

Conceptually:

\[ \text{word} \rightarrow \mathbb R^d \]

For example:

king   → vector
queen  → vector
apple  → vector

The vectors are learned so that useful relationships in language can be represented geometrically.

This is a major difference from one-hot representations.

One-hot

Vocabulary size = \(V\).

A token is represented by a sparse vector of length \(V\).

Embedding

A token is represented by a learned dense vector of length \(d\), usually with:

\[ d \ll V \]

in the conceptual setup.

Embeddings provide the bridge from classical text features to neural NLP.

38. Preprocessing Choices Can Change the Model

Suppose the original sentence is:

"NOT amazing!!!"

A careless pipeline might:

  1. lowercase;
  2. remove punctuation;
  3. remove stop words;
  4. stem;

and produce something that loses emphasis or negation.

A task-aware pipeline might preserve:

amazing
!!!

or encode these signals through a richer representation.

Therefore:

Preprocessing is part of model design.

It can alter the information available to the classifier.

39. Train-Test Leakage in NLP

A common mistake is to build vocabulary statistics using the entire dataset before the split.

For example, with TF-IDF:

wrong:
all documents
   ↓
fit vocabulary / IDF
   ↓
split into train and test

This can leak information from the test set into the representation process.

A cleaner workflow is:

split data
  ↓
fit representation on training data
  ↓
transform training data
  ↓
transform validation/test using the fitted representation

The test set should remain unseen during model/representation selection.

This connects directly to the generalization and validation ideas from Unit II.

40. Evaluation

After classification, use appropriate evaluation metrics.

For binary classification:

  • confusion matrix;
  • precision;
  • recall;
  • F1;
  • ROC/AUC where appropriate.

For example:

\[ Precision=\frac{TP}{TP+FP} \]
\[ Recall=\frac{TP}{TP+FN} \]
\[ F1=\frac{2PR}{P+R} \]

The correct metric depends on the cost of errors.

For spam detection, for example, false positives can matter because a legitimate message may be incorrectly blocked.

This links Unit IV NLP directly back to Unit II evaluation.

41. Multilabel vs Multi-Class

These terms are commonly confused.

Multi-class

Each document belongs to one of several classes.

Example:

article → {sports, finance, technology}

One label is selected.

Multi-label

A document can receive several labels simultaneously.

Example:

article → {technology, AI, healthcare}

Several labels can be true at once.

The output design and evaluation can therefore differ.

44. Common NLP Preprocessing Misconceptions

“More cleaning is always better.”

False.

Cleaning can remove useful information.

“Stemming and lemmatization are the same.”

False.

Stemming is generally more mechanical; lemmatization is linguistically informed.

“Every NLP system should remove stop words.”

False.

It depends on the task and representation.

“BoW understands sentence meaning.”

Not in the full semantic/contextual sense.

It mainly records features such as token occurrence/counts.

“Tokenization is classification.”

No.

Tokenization is representation/preprocessing.

“TF-IDF knows grammar.”

Not by itself.

TF-IDF reweights terms according to their document frequency patterns.

“Text must be converted to a vector before ML.”

For conventional numerical ML models, yes, some numerical representation is required. The specific representation may be sparse, dense, learned, or structured.

45. Failure Cases and Trade-offs

Choice Possible benefit Possible problem
lowercasing smaller vocabulary loses case information
stemming fewer features unnatural stems / over-merging
lemmatization more linguistic structure more processing / language dependence
stop-word removal smaller feature space can remove meaning
BoW simple and interpretable weak order/context
n-grams captures local order feature-space growth
TF-IDF emphasizes discriminative terms still sparse and largely lexical
embeddings dense semantic-style representation can depend strongly on training data/context

This is not a list of universally correct choices. It is a decision map.

46. Formula Card — TF-IDF

Core formula

\[ \boxed{TFIDF(t,d)=TF(t,d)\times IDF(t)} \]

with one common unsmoothed IDF definition:

\[ \boxed{IDF(t)=\log\frac{N}{df(t)}} \]

Meaning

A word gets higher weight when:

  • it is important/frequent in the current document;
  • but relatively uncommon across the entire collection.

Exam trigger

Question:

“Why does TF-IDF downweight common words?”

Answer:

Because terms appearing in many documents have lower inverse document frequency and therefore contribute less discriminative weight.

47. Formula Card — Cosine Similarity

\[ \boxed{ \cos\theta = \frac{x^Ty}{\|x\|_2\|y\|_2} } \]

Symbols

  • \(x,y\): document vectors;
  • \(x^Ty\): dot product;
  • \(\|x\|_2\): Euclidean norm;
  • \(\theta\): angle between vectors.

Intuition

Compare the direction of document vectors rather than only their raw length.

48. Formula Card — Binary Logistic Text Classifier

After converting text to vector \(x\):

\[ z=w^Tx+b \]
\[ \boxed{ \hat p=\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}} } \]

Interpretation:

\[ \hat p\approx P(y=1\mid x) \]

under the chosen logistic-model interpretation.

The key cross-unit message:

Once text becomes a feature vector, ordinary supervised ML methods from Unit II can operate on it.
