MAIN-U2-C21 — Logistic Regression: Mathematics & Decision Boundaries

Main Book: AI & Machine Learning — Visual + Mathematical Self-Learning Textbook
Unit: II — Machine Learning Fundamentals
Topic ID: ML-U2-C21
Prerequisites: ML-U2-C19, ML-U2-C20
Purpose: Deep mathematical companion to logistic regression: sigmoid, log-odds, likelihood, cross-entropy, gradients, parameter updates, decision boundaries, threshold changes, geometry and numerical reasoning.
Course-note status: The supplied Lecture07–Lecture10 material establishes the supervised-learning/loss/linear-model/gradient foundations but does not provide a complete logistic-regression derivation. This chapter is therefore explicitly marked as a universal mathematical expansion.

1. Why Have a Separate Mathematics Chapter?

In the previous chapter you learned what logistic regression does.

Here we ask a deeper question:

Why do these equations fit together, and how does the model actually learn its parameters?

The complete conceptual chain is:

Features x
   ↓
Linear score z = wᵀx+b
   ↓
Probability p = σ(z)
   ↓
Compare p with true y
   ↓
Cross-entropy loss
   ↓
Gradient
   ↓
Update w,b
   ↓
Repeat

This is one of the first places where your earlier mathematics becomes a real ML training mechanism.

2. Mathematical Prerequisites

Before continuing, you should recognize:

Vector

\[ x=[x_1,\ldots,x_d]^T \]

Inner product

\[ w^Tx=\sum_{j=1}^{d}w_jx_j \]

Linear model

\[ z=w^Tx+b \]

Derivative

A derivative tells us how a quantity changes when its input changes.

Gradient

For many parameters:

\[ \nabla_w L = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1}\\ \vdots\\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_d} \end{bmatrix} \]

Your Lecture09 explicitly defines the gradient as stacked partial derivatives and demonstrates the gradient of an inner product. Course source: Lecture09.pdf, slides 14–16.

3. The Sigmoid as a Mathematical Function

\[ \sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}} \]

The sigmoid has several useful properties.

Range

\[ 0\lt \sigma(z)\lt 1 \]

Center

\[ \sigma(0)=\frac12 \]

Monotonicity

If z increases, σ(z) increases.

Symmetry

\[ \sigma(-z)=1-\sigma(z) \]

This is useful when reasoning about positive vs negative evidence.

4. Deriving the Sigmoid Derivative

This derivative appears everywhere in neural networks and logistic regression.

Start with:

\[ \sigma(z)=(1+e^{-z})^{-1} \]

Differentiate:

\[ \frac{d\sigma}{dz} = -(1+e^{-z})^{-2}(-e^{-z}) \]

so:

\[ \frac{d\sigma}{dz} = \frac{e^{-z}}{(1+e^{-z})^2} \]

This can be rearranged into the elegant form:

\[ \boxed{ \sigma'(z)=\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z)) } \]

Why this form is useful

Instead of carrying exponentials everywhere, we can express the derivative directly using the output of the sigmoid.

👨‍🏫 Hinglish
Sigmoid ka output `p` mil gaya toh derivative ko directly:
\[ p(1-p) \]
likh sakte ho.

5. From Probability to Odds

Let:

\[ p=P(Y=1\mid x) \]

The odds of class 1 are:

\[ \boxed{ \frac{p}{1-p} } \]

Interpretation:

How much more likely is class 1 relative to class 0?

For example, if:

\[ p=0.8 \]

then:

\[ 1-p=0.2 \]

and:

\[ \text{odds}=\frac{0.8}{0.2}=4 \]

So the odds are 4 to 1.

6. From Odds to Log-Odds

Take the logarithm:

\[ \log\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right) \]

Using the sigmoid relationship, we get:

\[ \boxed{ \log\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)=z } \]

and because:

\[ z=w^Tx+b \]

we obtain:

\[ \boxed{ \log\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)=w^Tx+b } \]

This is the formal statistical statement behind the name logistic regression.

The model is linear in log-odds, not directly in probability.

7. Deriving the Sigmoid from the Log-Odds Model

Suppose:

\[ \log\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)=z \]

Exponentiate:

\[ \frac{p}{1-p}=e^z \]

Multiply:

\[ p=e^z(1-p) \]

Expand:

\[ p=e^z-e^zp \]

Move terms:

\[ p+e^zp=e^z \]

Factor:

\[ p(1+e^z)=e^z \]

Therefore:

\[ p=\frac{e^z}{1+e^z} \]

Divide numerator and denominator by e^z:

\[ \boxed{ p=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}} } \]

which is exactly the sigmoid.

Why this derivation matters

Now sigmoid is not just a memorized trick.

You understand that it appears naturally when the log-odds are assumed to be linear.

8. The Bernoulli Probability Model

Binary classification uses a Bernoulli random variable.

For one observation:

\[ y\in\{0,1\} \]

and the model predicts:

\[ p=P(Y=1\mid x) \]

The probability of observing y can be written compactly as:

\[ \boxed{ P(Y=y\mid x)=p^y(1-p)^{1-y} } \]

Check both cases.

If y=1

\[ p^1(1-p)^0=p \]

If y=0

\[ p^0(1-p)^1=1-p \]

This compact expression is the foundation for the likelihood.

9. Likelihood

Suppose the observations are:

\[ (x_1,y_1),\ldots,(x_n,y_n) \]

and assume the observations are conditionally independent given the model parameters.

The likelihood is:

\[ L(w,b) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p_i^{y_i}(1-p_i)^{1-y_i} \]

where:

\[ p_i=\sigma(w^Tx_i+b) \]

The idea is:

How compatible are the observed labels with the probabilities produced by the model?

10. Why Do We Use the Log-Likelihood?

Products are awkward to differentiate.

Take logarithms:

\[ \log L(w,b) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left[ y_i\log p_i+(1-y_i)\log(1-p_i) \right] \]

Maximizing likelihood is equivalent to maximizing log-likelihood because logarithm is monotonic.

Instead, ML commonly minimizes the negative log-likelihood:

\[ \boxed{ J(w,b)= -\sum_{i=1}^{n} \left[ y_i\log p_i+(1-y_i)\log(1-p_i) \right] } \]

The average version divides by n.

This is the binary cross-entropy / log-loss objective.

11. Why Cross-Entropy and Maximum Likelihood Are Connected

This is a high-value conceptual connection:

Bernoulli model
      ↓
Likelihood
      ↓
Log-likelihood
      ↓
Negative log-likelihood
      ↓
Binary cross-entropy

So the loss is not an arbitrary penalty invented solely for convenience.

It arises naturally from the probabilistic model.

12. Deriving the Gradient — The Important Part

For one observation, define:

\[ z=w^Tx+b \]
\[ p=\sigma(z) \]

and loss:

\[ \ell=-[y\log p+(1-y)\log(1-p)] \]

We want:

\[ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial z} \]

Differentiate with respect to p first:

\[ \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial p} = -\left(\frac{y}{p}-\frac{1-y}{1-p}\right) \]

Simplifying:

\[ \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial p} = \frac{p-y}{p(1-p)} \]

From the sigmoid derivative:

\[ \frac{dp}{dz}=p(1-p) \]

Apply the chain rule:

\[ \frac{d\ell}{dz} = \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial p} \frac{dp}{dz} \]

Therefore:

\[ \boxed{ \frac{d\ell}{dz}=p-y } \]

This is one of the most useful results in introductory ML.

13. Gradient with Respect to the Weights

Remember:

\[ z=w^Tx+b \]

Therefore:

\[ \frac{\partial z}{\partial w}=x \]

Using chain rule:

\[ \nabla_w\ell = \frac{d\ell}{dz} \frac{\partial z}{\partial w} \]

so:

\[ \boxed{ \nabla_w\ell=(p-y)x } \]

Similarly:

\[ \boxed{ \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial b}=p-y } \]

For n training examples, the gradient becomes the sum/average of the individual contributions.

14. What Does `(p-y)x` Mean?

This formula looks compact, but it has a simple interpretation.

\[ \nabla_w\ell=(p-y)x \]

There are two important pieces.

Prediction error

\[ p-y \]

How far the predicted probability is from the true label.

Input vector

\[ x \]

Which features were present and by how much.

Together they tell the optimizer:

How wrong was the prediction, and which parameter directions contributed to that error?

👨‍🏫 Hinglish
`p-y` tells you **kitni galti hui**. `x` tells you **kaunse features us prediction mein involved the**. Their product gives the direction in which the weights should be corrected.

15. Gradient Descent Update

A standard gradient-descent update is:

\[ \boxed{ w\leftarrow w-\eta\nabla_wJ } \]

and:

\[ \boxed{ b\leftarrow b-\eta\frac{\partial J}{\partial b} } \]

where:

  • η = learning rate;
  • J = objective/loss;
  • gradient = direction of increasing loss.

Subtracting the gradient moves toward lower loss.

Your Lecture09 explicitly introduces gradients, chain rule and optimization in the context of least squares; the same mathematical machinery reappears here. Course source: Lecture09.pdf, slides 14–18.

16. One-Step Numerical Gradient Example

Take one observation:

\[ x=2, \qquad y=1 \]

and parameters:

\[ w=0.5, \qquad b=-1 \]

Step 1 — score

\[ z=wx+b \]
\[ z=(0.5)(2)-1=0 \]

Step 2 — probability

\[ p=\sigma(0)=0.5 \]

Step 3 — error signal

\[ p-y=0.5-1=-0.5 \]

Step 4 — weight gradient

\[ \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial w}=(p-y)x \]
\[ =(-0.5)(2)=-1 \]

Step 5 — bias gradient

\[ \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial b}=-0.5 \]

Step 6 — update

Suppose:

\[ \eta=0.1 \]

Then:

\[ w_{new}=0.5-0.1(-1)=0.6 \]
\[ b_{new}=-1-0.1(-0.5)=-0.95 \]

The parameters move in a direction intended to increase the probability for the positive example.

17. Decision Boundary Derivation

With threshold 0.5:

\[ p\ge0.5 \]

Since sigmoid is monotonic and:

\[ \sigma(0)=0.5 \]

we have:

\[ w^Tx+b\ge0 \]

Therefore:

\[ \boxed{ w^Tx+b=0} \]

is the boundary.

18. Two-Dimensional Geometry

Suppose:

\[ w_1x_1+w_2x_2+b=0 \]

Then:

\[ \boxed{ x_2=-\frac{w_1}{w_2}x_1-\frac{b}{w_2} } \]

This has the form:

\[ y=mx+c \]

so the decision boundary is a straight line.

Geometric intuition

The weight vector:

\[ w \]

is normal/perpendicular to the decision boundary.

That means w points in the direction in which the linear score changes most directly.

This is the same vector-geometry language you learned in Lecture04.

19. What Does the Bias Term Do Geometrically?

Without b:

\[ w^Tx=0 \]

The boundary must pass through the origin.

With b:

\[ w^Tx+b=0 \]

we can shift the boundary.

Analogy

Think of w as deciding the orientation of a sheet and b as allowing the sheet to slide without changing its orientation.

👨‍🏫 Hinglish
`w` boundary ko ghumata/orient karta hai. `b` usko idhar-udhar shift karta hai.

20. Changing the Classification Threshold

0.5 is common, but not mathematically sacred.

Suppose threshold is:

\[ \tau \]

Then predict class 1 if:

\[ p\ge \tau \]

Since:

\[ p=\sigma(z) \]

and sigmoid is invertible:

\[ z\ge\log\left(\frac{\tau}{1-\tau}\right) \]

Therefore the threshold-dependent boundary is:

\[ \boxed{ w^Tx+b= \log\left(\frac{\tau}{1-\tau}\right) } \]

Example

For:

\[ \tau=0.5 \]

we obtain:

\[ \log\frac{0.5}{0.5}=0 \]

so:

\[ w^Tx+b=0 \]

For a higher threshold, the classifier becomes more conservative about assigning class 1.

This matters later when you study precision, recall and ROC curves.

21. Connection to Confusion-Matrix Metrics

Changing the threshold changes predictions.

Therefore it can change:

  • TP;
  • FP;
  • TN;
  • FN;
  • precision;
  • recall;
  • F1;
  • ROC coordinates.

So the topics are connected:

Logistic probability
        ↓
Choose threshold
        ↓
Predicted classes
        ↓
Confusion matrix
        ↓
Precision / Recall / F1 / ROC-AUC

This is an important bridge to the evaluation material in your Lecture07. Course source: Lecture07.pdf, slides 20–23.

22. Why Feature Scaling Can Matter

Your Lecture09 gives a concrete example where two features have very different units and shows that scaling changes the meaning of distances and can improve optimization. Course source: Lecture09.pdf, slides 7–9.

For logistic regression, scaling is also useful for optimization because parameter magnitudes and loss geometry can become poorly conditioned when feature scales differ greatly.

Example

Suppose one feature is:

Age: 18–80

and another is:

Income: 100,000–5,000,000

The model can still be expressed mathematically, but optimization can become less convenient than with sensibly scaled features.

23. Convexity Connection

For standard binary logistic regression with log loss, the optimization objective is convex in the linear parameters under the standard formulation.

That has an important consequence:

A local minimum is also a global minimum for a differentiable convex objective.

Your Lecture09 explicitly introduces convexity and explains why ∇g(x*)=0 identifies a global minimum when the function is convex and differentiable. Course source: Lecture09.pdf, slide 13.

Do not confuse this with all neural-network objectives, which can be non-convex.

24. Logistic Regression Training — Full Mathematical Picture

Put everything together.

\[ \boxed{ p_i=\sigma(w^Tx_i+b)} \]

then:

\[ \boxed{ J(w,b)= -\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n} [y_i\log p_i+(1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)] } \]

and:

\[ \boxed{ \nabla_wJ = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}(p_i-y_i)x_i } \]
\[ \boxed{ \frac{\partial J}{\partial b} = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}(p_i-y_i) } \]

Then update:

\[ w\leftarrow w-\eta\nabla_wJ \]
\[ b\leftarrow b-\eta\frac{\partial J}{\partial b} \]

This is the mathematical learning loop.

25. The Entire Algorithm in Human Language

English

  1. Look at each training example.
  2. Compute the linear score.
  3. Convert the score into a probability.
  4. Compare that probability with the true label.
  5. Compute the loss.
  6. Determine how each parameter contributed to the loss.
  7. Move parameters in the direction that reduces the loss.
  8. Repeat over the data until the chosen stopping criterion is satisfied.
👨‍🏫 Hinglish
1. Features lo. 2. Score nikalo. 3. Score ko sigmoid se probability banao. 4. Actual answer se compare karo. 5. Loss nikalo. 6. Gradient bataega parameters ko kis direction mein change karna hai. 7. Chhota update karo. 8. Repeat.

26. Full Numerical Exercise — Two Features

Suppose:

\[ x=[1,2]^T, \qquad y=1 \]
\[ w=[0.2,-0.1]^T, \qquad b=0.3 \]

Calculate:

Step 1

\[ z=w^Tx+b \]
\[ =(0.2)(1)+(-0.1)(2)+0.3 \]
\[ =0.3 \]

Step 2

\[ p=\sigma(0.3) \]

approximately:

\[ p\approx0.574 \]

Step 3

\[ p-y\approx-0.426 \]

Step 4 — Weight gradient

\[ \nabla_w\ell=(p-y)x \]
\[ \approx-0.426 \begin{bmatrix} 1\\2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -0.426\\-0.852 \end{bmatrix} \]

Step 5 — Bias gradient

\[ \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial b}\approx-0.426 \]

Now the optimizer can update the parameters.

The exact arithmetic is less important than being able to reconstruct the chain without memorization.

28. Common Mathematical Mistakes

Mistake 1

Writing:

\[ P(Y=1\mid x)=w^Tx+b \]

Wrong.

Correct:

\[ P(Y=1\mid x)=\sigma(w^Tx+b) \]

Mistake 2

Forgetting the bias term.

Mistake 3

Confusing the probability p with the raw score z.

Mistake 4

Using squared error automatically without checking the logistic model formulation.

Mistake 5

Forgetting the chain rule in the gradient derivation.

Mistake 6

Treating a threshold such as 0.5 as part of the trained probability model rather than as a decision rule.
