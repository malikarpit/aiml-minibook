1. Why This Chapter Exists

Financial systems generate large amounts of structured and unstructured data.

Examples include:

  • transaction records;
  • account activity;
  • market prices;
  • company reports;
  • news;
  • customer interactions;
  • payment histories;
  • loan applications.

AI/ML can be used to identify patterns, estimate risk, classify events, detect unusual behaviour and support decisions.

But finance is also a domain where:

  • false positives have costs;
  • false negatives have costs;
  • data distributions change;
  • decisions can affect people;
  • privacy matters;
  • explanations and accountability matter.

Therefore, this chapter is not “AI predicts the stock market.”

It is a case study of how the AI/ML toolbox is applied to financial problems.

2. One-Minute Intuition

Imagine a bank receiving thousands of transactions every minute.

A simple rule might say:

transaction > ₹1,00,000 → suspicious

But that rule can be poor.

A ₹2 lakh transaction might be completely normal for one customer and extremely unusual for another.

A useful ML system can consider several signals together:

amount
+ time
+ location
+ device
+ merchant
+ recent behaviour
+ historical pattern
      ↓
model score
      ↓
review / approve / block / escalate
👨‍🏫 Hinglish teacher explanation
Socho bank ke paas lakhon transactions aa rahi hain. Sirf amount dekhke fraud decide karna weak hoga. Tum puchoge: > “Amount kitna hai?” > “Usually ye customer kya karta hai?” > “Ye transaction kahan se aayi?” > “Device same hai?” > “Ekdam unusual pattern hai kya?” Yaani AI ek **pattern-based risk signal** de sakta hai. Lekin model ka score final truth nahi hota.

5. Fraud Detection — The Most Useful Example

Suppose each transaction is represented as:

\[ x= [ \text{amount}, \text{hour}, \text{location}, \text{merchant type}, \text{device features}, \text{recent activity}, \ldots ]. \]

The model estimates:

\[ \hat p=P(Y=1\mid x) \]

where:

  • \(Y=1\) means the transaction belongs to the chosen fraud class;
  • \(\hat p\) is the model's estimated score/probability under the model.

Then a threshold may be applied:

\[ \hat y= \begin{cases} 1,&\hat p\ge t\\ 0,&\hat p\lt t. \end{cases} \]

This connects directly to logistic regression, classification metrics and threshold selection.

6. Why Threshold Choice Matters

Suppose:

\[ t=0.5. \]

Any score above 0.5 is flagged.

Now lower the threshold to:

\[ t=0.2. \]

More transactions are likely to be flagged.

That may increase the number of suspicious cases caught, but it may also increase false positives.

Conceptually:

lower threshold
      ↓
more alerts
      ↓
potentially more TP
      +
potentially more FP

This is why precision, recall and the confusion matrix matter.

7. Fraud Detection Confusion Matrix

Predicted fraud Predicted normal
Actual fraud TP FN
Actual normal FP TN

Why the distinction matters

  • FN: real fraud missed.
  • FP: legitimate customer incorrectly flagged.

The operational costs can differ significantly.

A financial institution may therefore optimize for a business objective rather than blindly maximizing accuracy.

8. Worked Numerical Example — Precision and Recall

Suppose a validation sample contains:

  • TP = 40
  • FP = 10
  • FN = 20
  • TN = 930

Then:

\[ \text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP+FP} = \frac{40}{50} = 0.80 \]
\[ \text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP+FN} = \frac{40}{60} \approx0.667 \]

So:

  • precision = 80%;
  • recall ≈ 66.7%.

Interpretation

Of flagged transactions, 80% are actually in the positive class.

The model caught approximately 66.7% of actual positive cases in this sample.

This is much more informative than simply saying:

“The model has 97% accuracy.”

9. Class Imbalance

Fraud is often rare relative to normal transactions.

Imagine:

1,000,000 transactions
999,000 normal
  1,000 fraud

A useless classifier that predicts “normal” every time gets:

\[ \frac{999000}{1000000}=99.9\% \]

accuracy.

Yet it catches:

\[ 0\% \]

of fraud.

This is a classic demonstration of why imbalanced classification must be evaluated with appropriate metrics.

Your Unit II Lecture07 explicitly introduces this idea through confusion-matrix metrics and class imbalance.

10. Anomaly Detection

Not every financial system has perfect fraud labels.

An alternative framing is:

“Find activity that is unusual relative to normal behaviour.”

Conceptually:

normal cluster
████████████████

             •
         unusual point

Possible anomaly signals include:

  • extreme transaction amount;
  • unusual timing;
  • unexpected geographic change;
  • unusual sequence of transactions;
  • device/account mismatch.

But:

Unusual ≠ fraudulent.

Anomaly detection identifies behaviour worthy of attention; it does not automatically establish wrongdoing.

11. Credit Risk

Suppose a lender wants to estimate whether an applicant belongs to a risk class.

Features might include:

  • income-related variables;
  • repayment history;
  • debt-related variables;
  • employment information;
  • account history.

A classifier could estimate:

\[ P(Y=1\mid X=x) \]

where \(Y\) might represent a defined risk outcome.

A regression model could instead estimate a continuous quantity.

The model choice depends on the target variable and business formulation.

12. Credit Decision vs Credit Prediction

This distinction is important.

Model output

“Estimated probability of outcome = 0.18”

Business decision

“Approve / reject / request review.”

These are not necessarily the same layer.

A robust system can separate:

prediction
   ↓
policy constraints
   ↓
decision
   ↓
human review when required

This separation improves auditability.

13. Bias and Fairness in Finance

Financial decisions can affect:

  • access to credit;
  • pricing;
  • account monitoring;
  • fraud review;
  • insurance or risk assessment.

Bias can enter through:

historical data
      ↓
feature choices
      ↓
labels
      ↓
sampling
      ↓
model
      ↓
threshold
      ↓
deployment process

Therefore:

A mathematically accurate model can still create problematic outcomes for particular groups or contexts.

This connects directly to C46.

14. Proxy Variables

A variable may not explicitly represent a sensitive attribute but may correlate with it.

For example, some location-based or behavioural variables can indirectly encode population differences.

The lesson is not:

“Never use correlated variables.”

The real lesson is:

Check what information a feature captures and what consequences its use can create.

Technical fairness analysis needs the actual population, features, labels and decision process.

15. Fairness Is Not One Formula

Different fairness ideas can examine different quantities.

Examples include:

Group-wise positive rates

\[ P(\hat Y=1\mid A=a) \]

Group-wise true-positive rates

\[ TPR_a=P(\hat Y=1\mid Y=1,A=a) \]

Group-wise false-positive rates

\[ FPR_a=P(\hat Y=1\mid Y=0,A=a) \]

Different fairness criteria can conflict.

Therefore a statement such as:

“The model is fair.”

is incomplete without specifying:

  • fair by what criterion?
  • on which population?
  • for which decision?
  • at what threshold?
  • using which data period?

16. Explainability

A financial system may need to answer:

“Why did this application receive this risk assessment?”

Different levels of explanation exist.

Feature-level explanation

Which features influenced the model output?

Case-level explanation

Why did this particular case receive this score?

Model-level explanation

How does the model behave overall?

These are distinct questions.

17. Privacy

Financial data can be highly sensitive.

A useful mental model is:

collect
  ↓
store
  ↓
process
  ↓
model
  ↓
decision

Privacy considerations can arise at every stage.

Examples:

  • data minimization;
  • access control;
  • secure storage;
  • retention policies;
  • appropriate anonymization/pseudonymization;
  • monitoring access and use.

AI capability does not remove data-governance requirements.

18. NLP in Finance

Financial institutions process enormous amounts of text.

Examples:

  • annual reports;
  • earnings statements;
  • customer messages;
  • regulatory documents;
  • support conversations;
  • news.

An NLP pipeline can look like:

raw document
     ↓
tokenization / normalization
     ↓
representation
     ↓
classification / extraction / retrieval
     ↓
decision-support output

This connects directly to C43.

19. Example — Text Classification

Suppose a bank wants to classify customer messages:

“This transaction is not mine.”

Possible classes:

fraud complaint
billing issue
technical issue
general query

This is a multi-class text-classification problem.

A simple classical pipeline could be:

message
 ↓
tokens
 ↓
TF-IDF
 ↓
classifier
 ↓
class label

A neural NLP pipeline may instead use learned embeddings and a neural classifier.

20. Time-Series Data

Financial systems contain time-dependent observations:

\[ x_1,x_2,x_3,\ldots,x_T. \]

Examples:

  • daily transaction volume;
  • account balance over time;
  • market price series;
  • payment behaviour.

The ordering matters.

This makes sequence modelling concepts relevant.

A simplified model might use:

\[ h_t=f(x_t,h_{t-1}) \]

from the RNN framework.

The important point is:

Time ordering creates information that a bag-of-features representation can lose.

21. Avoiding Temporal Data Leakage

A serious financial modelling trap is using future information to predict the past.

Suppose you are predicting:

“Will a customer default next month?”

You must not accidentally include a feature generated after that future event.

Bad:

prediction date
      ↓
future information accidentally included
      ↓
model
      ↓
artificially strong performance

Correct:

information available at prediction time
      ↓
model
      ↓
future outcome

Memory anchor

At prediction time, only use information that would actually have been available then.

22. Train/Test Splitting for Time

Randomly mixing time periods can create unrealistic evaluation.

Example:

2019 ── 2020 ── 2021 ── 2022
          ↑ training
          ↑ test accidentally mixed

For time-dependent tasks, chronological evaluation is often more meaningful:

past data ─────────► training
later data ────────► validation
future data ───────► test

The exact split depends on the problem.

23. Dataset Shift in Finance

Financial behaviour changes.

Examples:

  • new regulations;
  • economic shocks;
  • seasonal changes;
  • new fraud patterns;
  • new payment technologies;
  • new customer behaviour.

Therefore:

\[ P_{\text{train}}(X,Y)\neq P_{\text{deployment}}(X,Y) \]

can occur.

A model can degrade even when the underlying software has not changed.

This is why monitoring is essential.

24. Model Drift Monitoring

A production system can monitor:

input distribution
      ↓
prediction distribution
      ↓
observed outcomes
      ↓
metric trend
      ↓
drift / degradation detection

Possible signals include:

  • rising false positives;
  • declining recall;
  • population changes;
  • feature-distribution shifts;
  • calibration deterioration.

The monitoring rule depends on the business problem.

25. Calibration

Suppose a set of transactions receives predicted probability:

\[ \hat p=0.8. \]

Calibration asks whether cases assigned probability around 0.8 actually show the event at roughly that frequency over an appropriate population.

A well-calibrated probability estimate is useful for downstream risk decisions.

Important distinction

A model can rank cases well but still be poorly calibrated.

So:

discrimination / ranking
≠
calibration

26. Worked Case Study — Suspicious Transaction Review

Suppose a system receives:

amount = ₹85,000
time = 02:14
new device = yes
location = unusual
recent failed logins = 3

The model outputs:

\[ \hat p=0.87. \]

A policy threshold is:

\[ t=0.80. \]

Therefore the system flags the transaction.

But the correct system interpretation is:

“This case crossed the configured model/policy threshold and should receive the defined downstream action.”

Not:

“The customer is definitely committing fraud.”

This distinction is central to responsible deployment.

27. Decision Pipeline

A mature system may look like:

transaction
    ↓
feature generation
    ↓
model score
    ↓
policy / threshold
    ↓
 ┌───────────────┬─────────────────┐
 ↓               ↓                 ↓
approve       additional review    block/hold
                  │
                  ▼
             human analysis
                  │
                  ▼
            final disposition
                  │
                  ▼
            feedback / labels
                  │
                  └────► future model improvement

This is much closer to real system thinking than:

transaction → neural network → yes/no

28. Reinforcement Learning in Finance — Conceptual Link

Sequential decision problems can sometimes be expressed using RL ideas.

For example:

  • state = portfolio or operational state;
  • action = a decision;
  • reward = defined objective;
  • transition = resulting state.

A simplified MDP framing is:

\[ (S,A,P,R,\gamma). \]

However, this does not mean:

“Use Q-learning to trade stocks and it will work.”

Financial environments are uncertain, non-stationary, affected by many interacting agents and constrained by risk and policy.

The RL framework is a modelling language; it is not a guarantee of profitable or safe decisions.

29. Why Backtesting Can Mislead

Imagine a strategy has excellent historical performance.

That does not automatically prove future performance.

Potential issues include:

  • data leakage;
  • look-ahead bias;
  • overfitting;
  • unrealistic transaction costs;
  • unrealistic execution assumptions;
  • changing market conditions;
  • selection bias.

This is another manifestation of the generalization problem you learned in Unit II.

30. Overfitting in Financial Models

Suppose a model is extremely flexible and trained on historical market data.

It may learn:

real pattern
+
noise
+
historical quirks

Training performance can look excellent.

Future performance can then be much weaker.

This is the same bias-variance/generalization idea from C17.

Memory anchor

A historical pattern is not automatically a durable pattern.

31. A Numerical Overfitting Illustration

Suppose:

Model Training score Future/test score
Model A 90% 84%
Model B 99% 61%

Model B fits the historical data more closely but generalizes much worse in this hypothetical example.

The lesson is about generalization, not about declaring one algorithm universally better.

32. Risk-Aware Evaluation

Different financial systems need different evaluation measures.

Fraud

  • precision;
  • recall;
  • F1;
  • ROC-AUC;
  • false-positive burden.

Credit risk

  • discrimination;
  • calibration;
  • class-specific error analysis;
  • fairness analysis.

Forecasting

  • suitable time-series error metrics;
  • out-of-sample testing;
  • robustness across periods.

NLP support systems

  • classification quality;
  • escalation quality;
  • human review burden.

There is no universal “finance AI metric.”

33. Common Traps

Trap 1 — “99.9% accuracy = excellent fraud model”

Class imbalance can make this meaningless.

Trap 2 — “High model score = fraud confirmed”

A score is not the same as a verified event.

Trap 3 — “A protected variable was removed, so bias is gone”

Proxy information can remain elsewhere.

Trap 4 — “Random train/test splitting is always fine”

Time-dependent problems can require chronological evaluation.

Trap 5 — “Historical backtest = future guarantee”

No.

A backtest is an evaluation on historical data under assumptions.

34. Exam Connection

📝 2-mark question
**Q:** Name three applications of AI in financial systems. Possible answers: > Fraud detection, credit-risk assessment, document/text classification.
📝 5-mark question
**Q:** Explain how ML can be used for fraud detection. Structure: 1. represent transactions with features; 2. train a classifier/anomaly detector; 3. generate a fraud-risk score; 4. apply a threshold or ranking policy; 5. evaluate using confusion-matrix metrics; 6. handle class imbalance; 7. monitor drift and false-positive burden.

Application question

Q: Why is accuracy often insufficient for fraud detection?

Because the positive class may be rare, so a classifier can have high accuracy while detecting very few actual fraud cases. Precision, recall and confusion-matrix analysis provide additional information.

36. Formula Sheet

Binary decision threshold

\[ \hat y= \begin{cases} 1,&\hat p\ge t\\ 0,&\hat p\lt t \end{cases} \]

Precision

\[ \text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP+FP} \]

Recall

\[ \text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP+FN} \]

F1

\[ F_1= 2\frac{\text{Precision}\cdot\text{Recall}} {\text{Precision}+\text{Recall}} \]

Probability-style model output

\[ \hat p=P(Y=1\mid X=x) \]

This denotes a model's estimated conditional probability under the chosen modelling framework.
