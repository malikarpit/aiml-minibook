0. Why this chapter exists

In the previous chapter, we learned how uninformed search works. Algorithms such as BFS and DFS know what problem we are solving, but they do not know which direction looks promising.

That creates a natural question:

Can we give a search algorithm a useful hint about where the goal probably is?

That hint is the central idea of a heuristic.

Your supplied Informed Search lecture introduces heuristics before Greedy search and A*, and later uses the heuristic value h(n) as an estimate of the cost from a node to a goal. It uses straight-line distance to Bucharest as the canonical Romania-map example. lecture-informed-search.pdf, slides 2, 3, 6, 8–9.

3. Why Do We Need a Heuristic?

Imagine you are standing in a huge city and someone tells you:

“Reach the airport.”

You could try roads systematically. But if there are thousands of possible roads, blindly exploring them is expensive.

Now suppose you have a rough idea of where the airport is.

You might think:

“This road appears to take me in the direction of the airport, so let's investigate that one first.”

That directional information is similar to a heuristic.

Important distinction

The heuristic does not necessarily tell you the correct route.

It tells you something more modest:

“This state looks promising according to the information I have.”

That distinction is essential.

4. Real-World Analogy — GPS

Suppose you are travelling from Delhi to Jaipur.

At some intermediate city, you have two possible roads.

You know:

  • Road A has already cost you 80 km.
  • Road B has already cost you 40 km.

But if you only compare the distance already travelled, Road B automatically looks better.

However, Road B may take a huge detour.

A smarter decision would also consider:

“How far am I likely to be from Jaipur after taking this route?”

That estimated remaining distance is the intuition behind h(n).

Analogy mapping

Real world Search terminology
Current location Current node/state
Destination Goal
Distance already travelled g(n)
Estimated remaining distance h(n)
Choosing a promising route Node expansion strategy

Where the analogy breaks

A heuristic is not automatically a physical distance.

It can be any useful estimate appropriate to the problem.

For the 8-puzzle, for example, the lecture uses:

  • number of misplaced tiles
  • total Manhattan distance

So the heuristic depends on the problem representation.

5. Formal Definition

A heuristic function is a function that assigns an estimate of the remaining cost of reaching a goal from a node/state.

We write it as:

\[ h(n) \]

where:

  • n = current node/state
  • h(n) = estimated cost from n to a goal

Your supplied lecture gives exactly this interpretation in the Greedy Search section and uses straight-line distance to Bucharest as an example.

Course framing: h(n) is an estimate of the cost from n to the closest goal.
Source: lecture-informed-search.pdf, slide 9.

6. Teacher Explanation — English

Think of a search algorithm as a person exploring an unfamiliar building.

Without a heuristic, the person has a map only of where they have been and what they can do next. They have no clue which unexplored room is more promising.

With a heuristic, the person receives an additional clue:

“Based on what you know, this room appears to be closer to the destination.”

The person can now spend more of their effort on promising possibilities.

That is the central purpose of a heuristic:

Use problem-specific knowledge to guide search toward promising states.

The word estimate is extremely important. A heuristic is not necessarily the true remaining cost.

7. Teacher Explanation — Hinglish

Socho tum ek giant mall mein ho aur tumhe ek specific shop dhoondhni hai.

Agar tumhe kuch bhi idea nahi hai, toh tum systematically har floor aur har corridor explore karoge.

Lekin agar security guard bol de:

“Shop roughly east side mein hai.”

Toh tum us information ka use karke pehle east side explore karoge.

Ye east-side wali information heuristic jaisi hai.

Tumhe exact answer nahi mila.

Tumhe bas ek useful estimate mila.

Aur search algorithm isi estimate ki help se decide karta hai:

“Pehle kaunsi direction try karni hai?”

8. The Most Important Notation

You will repeatedly see three symbols while studying informed search:

\[ g(n),\quad h(n),\quad f(n) \]

For this chapter, focus primarily on h(n).

Symbol Meaning Question it answers
g(n) Cost already spent to reach n “Main yahan tak kitna aa chuka hoon?”
h(n) Estimated remaining cost “Goal tak aur kitna lag sakta hai?”
f(n) Search evaluation value “Overall, which node looks promising?”

g(n) and f(n) become central in the next A* chapter. Here they are introduced only so the role of a heuristic is not isolated from the rest of informed search.

The supplied lecture defines A* as f(n)=g(n)+h(n), with g(n) as cost so far and h(n) as estimated cost to the goal. lecture-informed-search.pdf, slide 17.

9. Heuristic vs. True Cost

This is one of the easiest places to make a mistake.

Suppose the actual cheapest remaining path from node n to a goal costs:

\[ 17 \]

but our heuristic says:

\[ h(n)=12 \]

Then:

  • True remaining cost = 17
  • Estimated remaining cost = 12

The heuristic is allowed to be different from the truth.

The question is how it differs.

That leads to the most important property of heuristics in A*.

10. True Remaining Cost: \(h^*(n)\)

To reason mathematically about a heuristic, define:

\[ h^*(n) \]

as the actual cost of the cheapest path from n to a goal.

So:

  • h(n) → our estimate
  • h*(n) → the true optimal remaining cost

Example:

Current node n
      |
      |  estimated by us
      ↓
   h(n) = 10

But the actual cheapest remaining route is
      ↓
   h*(n) = 13

Here the heuristic underestimated the true cost.

That is acceptable for an admissible heuristic.

11. Admissible Heuristic

Your supplied lecture gives the A* requirement:

\[ h(n)\le h^*(n) \]

and also requires:

\[ h(n)\ge 0 \]

Therefore, for an admissible heuristic:

\[ \boxed{0\le h(n)\le h^*(n)} \]

Plain English

An admissible heuristic never overestimates the true cheapest remaining cost.

It can:

  • underestimate
  • exactly estimate

but it must not estimate something more expensive than reality.

👨‍🏫 Hinglish
> Admissible heuristic ka simple rule: **“Guess zyada nahi karna.”** Agar actual remaining cost `13` hai, heuristic `10` ya `13` bol sakta hai. But `15` nahi bolna chahiye. Your lecture uses straight-line distance to Bucharest as an example that never overestimates actual road distance. `lecture-informed-search.pdf`, slide 17.

12. Why Does “Never Overestimate” Matter?

This idea becomes extremely important in A*.

Suppose the heuristic says a path is still cheap when it might actually be expensive.

That is a form of uncertainty that A* can safely reason about.

But if the heuristic is allowed to say:

“This node will definitely cost 100 more,”

when the real remaining cost is only 20,

the search may reject a genuinely good path too aggressively.

So admissibility gives a useful guarantee:

The heuristic is optimistic about remaining cost.

It can be pleasantly wrong by being too small.

It cannot be pessimistically wrong by being too large.

Memory anchor

Admissible = optimistic estimate.

13. Goal Nodes and the Heuristic

At a goal node G, there is no remaining cost to reach a goal.

Therefore:

\[ h^*(G)=0 \]

For an admissible heuristic with non-negative estimates:

\[ 0\le h(G)\le 0 \]

so:

\[ \boxed{h(G)=0} \]

This is why the supplied A* lecture explicitly notes that non-negative admissibility implies h(G)=0 for a goal.

👨‍🏫 Hinglish
> Goal par pahunch gaye ho. Ab destination tak aur kitna cost? **Zero.**

14. A Concrete Heuristic Example — Road Navigation

Consider a simplified map:

B = Goal
                / \\
               /   \\
              C     D
              |      |
              |      |
              A------E
             Start

Suppose A is the current state and B is the goal.

Imagine we can estimate the straight-line distance from each location to the goal.

We might have:

Node h(n)
A 100
C 70
D 40
E 30
B 0

The smaller h(n) is, the closer the node appears to the goal according to this heuristic.

Important

This does not mean the actual road distance follows the same ordering.

A road can be physically close but require a huge detour.

That is why a heuristic is an estimate, not the complete answer.

15. Heuristics from the Romania Map

The supplied lecture uses the classic Romania map.

The heuristic example is:

\[ h_{SLD}(n)=\text{straight-line distance from }n\text{ to Bucharest} \]

where SLD means Straight-Line Distance.

The idea is simple:

Actual roads
A ────────╲
 \         ╲
  \         ╲
   ╲         B = Bucharest
    ╲_______/

Straight line is a direct geometric estimate.

The straight-line distance is never longer than the actual drivable road distance for the road-network example, so it provides a lower-bound style estimate appropriate to the lecture's admissibility discussion.

Source: lecture-informed-search.pdf, slides 8–9 and 17.

16. Heuristics for the 8-Puzzle

The supplied lecture gives two important heuristic examples for the 8-puzzle:

Heuristic 1 — Misplaced Tiles

\[ \boxed{h_1(n)=\text{number of misplaced tiles}} \]

Count how many tiles are not in their desired positions.

Heuristic 2 — Manhattan Distance

\[ \boxed{h_2(n)=\text{total Manhattan distance}} \]

For each tile, count how many horizontal + vertical squares it is away from its target position, then add the values.

The lecture gives a worked state where:

\[ h_1(S)=6 \]

and:

\[ h_2(S)=4+0+3+3+1+0+2+1=14 \]

This is a very useful example because the two heuristics look at different notions of “distance from the goal.” lecture-informed-search.pdf, slides 31–32.

17. Visualising the Two 8-Puzzle Heuristics

Suppose the target is:

+---+---+---+
| 1 | 2 | 3 |
+---+---+---+
| 4 | 5 | 6 |
+---+---+---+
| 7 | 8 | _ |
+---+---+---+

Now suppose a current state has several misplaced tiles.

Misplaced-tile heuristic asks:

“How many tiles are simply in the wrong place?”

Manhattan heuristic asks:

“How far, in total, do the tiles have to move?”

So Manhattan distance captures how wrong a tile is, while misplaced-tile count captures mainly whether it is wrong.

That distinction helps explain why a heuristic can contain more useful information than another even when both are valid estimates.

18. Heuristic Quality

A heuristic is useful because it can reduce unnecessary search.

Your supplied lecture explicitly states:

Good heuristics can dramatically reduce search cost.

This is the practical reason informed search exists.

A heuristic that gives almost no useful information may behave much like uninformed search.

A heuristic that gives a much more informative estimate can guide search toward promising states much faster.

The key trade-off

Weak heuristic
     ↓
Cheap/simple estimate
     ↓
Less guidance
     ↓
More nodes may be explored

Strong / informative heuristic
     ↓
Better guidance
     ↓
Fewer nodes may need exploration

This does not mean “the strongest-looking heuristic is always safe.” Its properties matter.

19. Dominance: Comparing Two Admissible Heuristics

Your lecture introduces a useful comparison rule.

Suppose h1 and h2 are both admissible and:

\[ h_2(n)\ge h_1(n) \]

for every node n.

Then h2 dominates h1.

The lecture calls the dominating heuristic better for search in this sense.

Why?

Because both are still valid lower-bound estimates, but h2 gives a tighter estimate whenever it is larger.

Think of it as:

True remaining cost
|-----------------------------------------|

h1        |---------|

h2        |---------------|

Both stay below the truth,
but h2 is closer to the truth.

Important: this is a relation under the condition that both heuristics are admissible and the inequality holds everywhere.

20. Example of Dominance — 8-Puzzle

Your lecture gives:

\[ h_1(n)=\text{misplaced tiles} \]

and:

\[ h_2(n)=\text{Manhattan distance} \]

and uses the fact that the second can provide substantially stronger guidance.

For the lecture's example state:

\[ h_1(S)=6 \]

while:

\[ h_2(S)=14 \]

The larger value is more informative because the heuristic remains a valid lower bound under the assumptions used for the 8-puzzle.

Source: lecture-informed-search.pdf, slides 31–33.

21. Where Do Good Heuristics Come From?

A natural question is:

“Who invents the heuristic?”

Often, the heuristic is designed by looking at a simplified version of the original problem.

Your lecture calls this a relaxed problem.

Main idea

Remove some restrictions from the original problem.

If the relaxed problem becomes easier to solve, its exact solution cost can become a useful estimate for the original problem.

The lecture states that the optimal solution cost of a relaxed problem is no greater than the optimal solution cost of the real problem.

Therefore, it can provide a lower-bound style estimate.

22. Relaxed Problem Analogy

Imagine a real-life journey where:

  • some roads are closed
  • you must obey traffic rules
  • you may have to stop
  • you may have tolls

Now imagine a fantasy version where some of those restrictions are removed.

The fantasy version can usually be completed at no greater cost than the real version.

That cheap idealised answer can tell you:

“At the absolute minimum, you need to spend at least about this much.”

That is precisely the kind of information a useful heuristic can provide.

👨‍🏫 Hinglish
> Real problem mein restrictions bahut hain. Relaxed problem mein kuch rules hata do. Agar relaxed version ka exact cost bhi itna hi low hai, toh original problem ke liye woh ek useful lower bound ban sakta hai.

23. What Makes a Heuristic Good?

For this course, remember three dimensions:

1. Relevance

Does it actually tell us something about distance to the goal?

2. Safety

For algorithms requiring admissibility, does it obey the required bound?

3. Informativeness

Does it substantially distinguish promising states from poor ones?

A heuristic can be easy to compute but provide little useful information.

A different heuristic may require more work to compute but save far more search later.

This produces a classic engineering trade-off:

Spend some computation on a good estimate now to save much more computation later.

24. Heuristic vs Evaluation Function

These words are related but not identical.

Heuristic function

A function that estimates the remaining cost or quality relevant to search.

Evaluation function

A function used by a search strategy to rank candidate nodes.

Your supplied lecture introduces Best-First Search through an evaluation function and then identifies Greedy and A* as special cases. lecture-informed-search.pdf, slides 5–6.

For the upcoming chapters:

Best-First Search
       │
       ├── Greedy → evaluation based on h(n)
       │
       └── A*     → evaluation based on g(n)+h(n)

This distinction will become much clearer in C06 and C07.

25. The Three Questions You Must Ask About Any Heuristic

Whenever you see a new heuristic, ask:

Question 1 — What is it estimating?

Usually:

Remaining cost to a goal.

Question 2 — How is the estimate calculated?

Examples:

  • straight-line distance
  • misplaced tiles
  • Manhattan distance

Question 3 — What guarantees does it have?

Especially:

Is it admissible?

Do not memorise the formula while ignoring the meaning.

26. Worked Example A — Simple Navigation

Suppose the goal is node G.

S
├── A
│   └── G
└── B
    └── G

Suppose the heuristic estimates are:

Node h(n)
A 6
B 3
G 0

A heuristic-guided strategy that prefers smaller estimated remaining cost would consider B more promising than A.

Why?

Because:

\[ 3\lt 6 \]
👨‍🏫 Hinglish
> Algorithm ko B goal ke zyada paas **lag raha hai**. Notice the wording: **“lag raha hai.”** Not: **“definitely hai.”** That's the whole point of a heuristic.

27. Worked Example B — Is a Heuristic Admissible?

Suppose the actual optimal remaining costs are:

Node h*(n) Proposed h(n) Admissible?
A 12 9 ✅ Yes
B 10 10 ✅ Yes
C 7 3 ✅ Yes
D 15 18 ❌ No

Check the rule:

\[ 0\le h(n)\le h^*(n) \]

For D:

\[ 18\gt 15 \]

So D violates admissibility.

Exam rule

One overestimate is enough to make the heuristic non-admissible.

28. Worked Example C — Build a Heuristic from a Relaxed Problem

Suppose the real puzzle has three rules:

  1. Only adjacent moves are allowed.
  2. A move costs 1.
  3. A tile can only move into the blank position.

Now create a relaxed version where a tile is allowed to move directly toward its destination without obeying all original restrictions.

If solving the relaxed problem takes 5 steps, then:

\[ h(n)=5 \]

can act as a lower-bound style estimate for the real problem because the real problem has more restrictions.

Important

This is a teacher-built example of the relaxed-problem idea. The underlying method is the one described in your supplied informed-search lecture.

29. A Heuristic Is Problem-Specific Knowledge

There is no universal heuristic that automatically works for every search problem.

For example:

Road navigation

A geometric distance can be meaningful.

8-puzzle

Tile displacement is meaningful.

Timetabling

The number of unresolved conflicts may be meaningful.

Robot motion

Estimated remaining travel distance/time may be meaningful.

Therefore:

A heuristic is tied to the structure of the problem.

👨‍🏫 Hinglish
> Har problem ke liye same shortcut nahi chalega. Problem ko samjho, phir us problem ke structure se useful estimate banao.

30. What a Heuristic Does NOT Do

A heuristic does not automatically:

  • guarantee the correct next node
  • guarantee the optimal final solution by itself
  • replace the search algorithm
  • give the exact remaining cost
  • eliminate the need for problem formulation

Instead, it supplies information that a search strategy can use.

This is why your lecture presents heuristics together with specific informed-search strategies such as Greedy and A*.

31. Common Misconceptions

Misconception 1

“A heuristic is the actual remaining cost.”

Correct

It is an estimate.

Misconception 2

“A larger heuristic is always better.”

Correct

Not automatically. Under the lecture's admissibility/dominance discussion, a larger estimate is meaningful only under the relevant validity conditions.

Misconception 3

“Admissible means exact.”

Correct

No.

Admissible means:

\[ h(n)\le h^*(n) \]

It can be much smaller than the truth.

Misconception 4

“Heuristic means random guess.”

Correct

A good heuristic is problem-informed. It is based on useful structure or knowledge about the problem.

Misconception 5

“Heuristic search means we no longer need search.”

Correct

The heuristic guides the search; it does not replace the search process.

33. Mathematical Summary

Heuristic

\[ \boxed{h(n)=\text{estimated remaining cost from }n\text{ to a goal}} \]

True optimal remaining cost

\[ \boxed{h^*(n)=\text{actual cheapest remaining cost from }n\text{ to a goal}} \]

Admissibility

\[ \boxed{0\le h(n)\le h^*(n)} \]

Goal

\[ \boxed{h(G)=0} \]

Greedy uses

\[ \boxed{h(n)} \]

A* uses

\[ \boxed{f(n)=g(n)+h(n)} \]

The last two belong formally to the next chapters, but they should be mentally connected to the current idea.
