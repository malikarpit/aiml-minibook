MAIN-U2-C29 — Ridge vs Lasso Regularization

Core question: Both Ridge and Lasso add a penalty to the regression objective. Why do they behave differently, and how can you reason about the difference without memorizing a table blindly?

This chapter turns two separate topics—Ridge and Lasso—into one coherent mathematical picture.

1. Why This Comparison Matters

You now have two regularized regression objectives.

Ridge

\[ J_R(w)=\|Xw-y\|_2^2+\lambda\|w\|_2^2 \]

Lasso

\[ J_L(w)=\|Xw-y\|_2^2+\lambda\|w\|_1 \]

At first glance the difference looks tiny:

Ridge → square the coefficients
Lasso → take absolute values

But this small mathematical change produces major behavioural differences.

The central chain is:

Penalty choice
      ↓
Geometric shape
      ↓
Mathematical smoothness
      ↓
Coefficient behaviour
      ↓
Sparsity / feature selection behaviour

Once this chain is understood, the comparison becomes much easier to remember.

3. The One Idea Behind Both Methods

Both methods solve the same kind of problem:

\[ \boxed{ \text{choose parameters that fit the data while discouraging excessive coefficient magnitude} } \]

General form:

\[ \boxed{ J(w)=L_{data}(w)+\lambda\Omega(w) } \]

The difference is the choice of complexity measure \(\Omega(w)\).

Ridge

\[ \Omega(w)=\|w\|_2^2 \]

Lasso

\[ \Omega(w)=\|w\|_1 \]

So:

Ridge and Lasso are not two unrelated algorithms. They are two different regularization choices built around the same general principle.

4. One-Minute Intuition

English

Suppose many different coefficient vectors can fit the training data reasonably well.

Regularization says:

“Among solutions with useful data fit, prefer one that is controlled in a particular mathematical way.”

Ridge prefers a small sum of squared coefficients.

Lasso prefers a small sum of absolute coefficients.

That difference influences whether coefficients merely become small or whether some can become exactly zero.

Hinglish Teacher Explanation

Dono models bol rahe hain:

“Sirf training data ko fit mat karo. Weights ko bhi control karo.”

Difference hai control ka style.

Ridge:
weight ko continuously chhota karo.

Lasso:
weight ko chhota karo, aur zarurat par exactly 0 tak le jao.

Yahi core intuition hai.

5. The Master Comparison Formula

Memorize the pair:

\[ \boxed{ J_R(w)=\text{Loss}+\lambda\sum_jw_j^2 } \]
\[ \boxed{ J_L(w)=\text{Loss}+\lambda\sum_j|w_j| } \]

Trigger words

Question wording Think first of
squared coefficients Ridge / L2
absolute coefficients Lasso / L1
shrink coefficients Ridge or Lasso
exact zeros Lasso characteristic
sparse model Lasso characteristic
feature selection Lasso characteristic
circular/spherical geometry Ridge
diamond/corners Lasso
smooth penalty Ridge
non-differentiable at zero Lasso

6. The Norms — Do Not Mix Them Up

For

\[ w=(w_1,w_2,\ldots,w_d), \]

L1 norm

\[ \boxed{ \|w\|_1=\sum_{j=1}^{d}|w_j| } \]

L2 norm

\[ \boxed{ \|w\|_2=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{d}w_j^2} } \]

Ridge commonly uses the squared L2 norm:

\[ \|w\|_2^2= \sum_jw_j^2. \]

Numerical example

For

\[ w=(3,-4), \]

we have:

\[ \|w\|_1=|3|+|-4|=7, \]

and

\[ \|w\|_2^2=3^2+(-4)^2=25. \]

Do not answer “L2 = 25” unless the question specifically asks for the squared L2 norm. The norm itself is:

\[ \|w\|_2=5. \]

7. Geometry — Circle vs Diamond

In two dimensions, imagine all parameter vectors allowed by a fixed complexity budget.

Ridge / L2

A constraint such as

\[ w_1^2+w_2^2\le t \]

gives a circle.

Lasso / L1

A constraint such as

\[ |w_1|+|w_2|\le t \]

gives a diamond.

Conceptually:

Ridge                              Lasso

         ______                     /\
      .-'      '-.                 /  \
    .'            '.              /    \
   /       •        \            /  •   \
   \                /             \    /
    '.            .'               \  /
      '-.______.-'                  \/

smooth boundary                    sharp corners

The exact fitted solution depends on the loss contours and the data. The geometric picture provides intuition for why the two penalties select different coefficient patterns.

8. Why Corners Matter

Imagine a set of elliptical contours representing equal values of the prediction loss.

The regularized optimum occurs where a loss contour first touches the feasible/penalized region in the geometric interpretation.

Ridge

The boundary is smooth.

The touching point is less structurally biased toward coordinate axes.

Lasso

The boundary has corners lying on the axes:

\[ w_1=0 \quad\text{or}\quad w_2=0. \]

Therefore an intersection with a corner can naturally produce an exact zero coordinate.

Memory anchor

Corners create opportunities for zeros.

This is one of the most important visual explanations of Lasso sparsity.

9. Smoothness and Differentiability

Ridge

For the penalty:

\[ \sum_jw_j^2, \]

the derivative with respect to \(w_j\) is:

\[ 2w_j. \]

It is smooth everywhere.

Lasso

For:

\[ \sum_j|w_j|, \]

the derivative is:

\[ \operatorname{sign}(w_j) \]

away from zero, but ordinary differentiation fails exactly at

\[ w_j=0. \]

This requires subgradient or other optimization treatment.

Thus:

L2 → smooth penalty
L1 → kink at zero

10. Why Ridge Has a Simple Closed Form

For standard least-squares Ridge:

\[ J_R(w)=\|Xw-y\|_2^2+\lambda w^Tw. \]

Differentiate:

\[ \nabla J_R(w)=2X^T(Xw-y)+2\lambda w. \]

Set equal to zero:

\[ X^TXw-X^Ty+\lambda w=0. \]

So:

\[ (X^TX+\lambda I)w=X^Ty. \]

Hence:

\[ \boxed{ \hat w_R=(X^TX+\lambda I)^{-1}X^Ty } \]

when the inverse form is valid.

The important comparison point is not simply “Ridge has a formula.”

It is:

The L2 penalty is smooth and quadratic, allowing the standard least-squares derivative route to produce this closed-form result.

11. Why Lasso Does Not Have the Same Simple Formula

Lasso uses:

\[ J_L(w)=\|Xw-y\|_2^2+\lambda\sum_j|w_j|. \]

The absolute-value term is non-differentiable at zero.

Therefore the usual sequence:

Differentiate
→ set gradient to zero
→ isolate w
→ write inverse formula

does not transfer directly.

Instead, Lasso is commonly solved with optimization methods designed for its structure, including coordinate-descent-style methods.

Exam trap

If the question says Ridge closed-form solution, use the Ridge formula.

If it says Lasso, do not write the Ridge inverse formula.

12. Coefficient Behaviour — The Central Difference

Suppose an unregularized model has:

\[ (5,-3,1,0.2). \]

Ridge intuition

The coefficients are generally pulled toward zero:

\[ (3.5,-2.2,0.8,0.15) \]

could be a conceptual outcome.

Lasso intuition

Some may be pulled all the way to zero:

\[ (3.2,-1.9,0.6,0) \]

could be a conceptual outcome.

These are illustrative patterns, not guaranteed numerical outputs.

Core line

\[ \boxed{ \text{Ridge: shrink} \qquad \text{Lasso: shrink + possible exact zeros} } \]

13. Feature Selection — What Exactly Is Being Selected?

Suppose Lasso estimates:

\[ \hat w=(2.4,0,-1.1,0,0.6). \]

Features 2 and 4 have zero coefficients.

Therefore the fitted prediction is:

\[ \hat y=2.4x_1-1.1x_3+0.6x_5. \]

Features 2 and 4 do not contribute to this fitted model.

This is often described as embedded feature selection.

Ridge generally keeps coefficients nonzero, so its standard role is coefficient shrinkage rather than exact sparse selection.

14. But Does Lasso Always Select One Feature From a Correlated Pair?

No universal guarantee should be memorized as a theorem of ordinary Lasso without qualifications.

Suppose:

\[ x_1\approx x_2. \]

Both may explain similar variation in \(y\).

Lasso can favour a sparse representation, potentially giving more weight to one and less or zero weight to another.

But correlated predictors make the selected subset sensitive to the data and setup.

So:

Zero coefficient
≠
proof that the variable is inherently irrelevant

For interpretation, distinguish:

  • predictive model behaviour,
  • statistical selection behaviour,
  • and real-world causal meaning.

15. Scaling — A Shared Requirement

Both Ridge and Lasso penalize coefficient magnitudes.

Suppose a feature is multiplied by a constant \(c\):

\[ x_j' = cx_j. \]

To preserve the same prediction contribution, the coefficient can change roughly as:

\[ w_j'\approx\frac{w_j}{c}. \]

Therefore the same underlying predictor can receive a numerically different coefficient merely because its measurement unit changed.

Since regularization acts on the coefficient values, scaling affects the relative penalty experienced by features.

Practical rule

For regularized regression, think carefully about feature scaling before interpreting coefficient magnitude or comparing feature selection.

Your Lecture09.pdf explicitly supplies standardization and min-max scaling foundations.

16. Intercept — Shared Convention

A common objective is:

\[ J(w,b)=L(w,b)+\text{penalty}(w), \]

where the intercept \(b\) is not penalized.

For Ridge:

\[ J_R(w,b)=\|Xw+b\mathbf1-y\|_2^2+\lambda\|w\|_2^2. \]

For Lasso:

\[ J_L(w,b)=\|Xw+b\mathbf1-y\|_2^2+\lambda\|w\|_1. \]

The exact convention can vary by textbook and implementation, so use the specification in your question.

17. The Role of \(\lambda\) — Same Idea, Different Effect Pattern

Both methods use \(\lambda\).

Small \(\lambda\)

Regularization is weak.

The objective behaves more like ordinary least squares.

Larger \(\lambda\)

Regularization is stronger.

Coefficients are pushed toward zero more aggressively.

Very large \(\lambda\)

The model can become too restricted and underfit.

The important difference is what “pushed toward zero” looks like:

Ridge:
continuous shrinkage toward 0

Lasso:
continuous shrinkage + the possibility of exact 0

18. A Numerical Comparison of Penalty Values

Take:

\[ w=(2,-3). \]

Ridge penalty

\[ \|w\|_2^2=2^2+(-3)^2=13. \]

Lasso penalty

\[ \|w\|_1=|2|+|-3|=5. \]

If:

\[ \lambda=0.4, \]

then:

\[ Penalty_R=0.4(13)=5.2 \]

and

\[ Penalty_L=0.4(5)=2.0. \]

Important interpretation

Do not conclude that Ridge is “more powerful” because its numeric penalty is 5.2 rather than 2.0.

The two penalties live on different mathematical scales and can use different tuned values of \(\lambda\).

The numbers are useful only for comparing two candidate objectives under the exact same stated convention.

19. Worked Candidate-Solution Comparison

Suppose the same data-fit loss is obtained by two candidate coefficient vectors:

\[ w_A=(3,-2) \]

and

\[ w_B=(2.4,-1.2). \]

Assume:

\[ L_{data}(w_A)=4.0, \qquad L_{data}(w_B)=4.5. \]

Let:

\[ \lambda=0.5. \]

Under Ridge

For \(w_A\):

\[ \|w_A\|_2^2=9+4=13 \]

so:

\[ J_A=4+0.5(13)=10.5. \]

For \(w_B\):

\[ \|w_B\|_2^2=2.4^2+1.2^2=5.76+1.44=7.20 \]

so:

\[ J_B=4.5+0.5(7.2)=8.1. \]

Thus:

\[ J_B\lt J_A \]

under this Ridge objective.

Under Lasso

For \(w_A\):

\[ \|w_A\|_1=3+2=5 \]

so:

\[ J_A=4+0.5(5)=6.5. \]

For \(w_B\):

\[ \|w_B\|_1=2.4+1.2=3.6 \]

so:

\[ J_B=4.5+0.5(3.6)=6.3. \]

Again:

\[ J_B\lt J_A. \]

The example teaches an essential point:

Regularization can favour a slightly worse data fit if the reduction in the penalty is large enough.

20. A Better Mental Model: Two Kinds of “Simplicity”

Ridge simplicity

“Do not let any coefficient become unnecessarily large.”

This is a magnitude-control mindset.

Lasso simplicity

“Control total coefficient magnitude in a way that can also make some coordinates exactly zero.”

This is a magnitude-control + sparsity mindset.

This does not mean every Lasso model is sparse or every Ridge model is dense in every imaginable setup. The distinction is about the characteristic behaviour encouraged by the penalty.

21. Bias-Variance View

Recall the general framework:

\[ \text{Expected test error} \approx \text{Bias}^2+\text{Variance}+\text{Irreducible Error}. \]

Increasing regularization restricts the fitted model.

Conceptually:

\[ \text{regularization}\uparrow \Rightarrow \text{model freedom}\downarrow. \]

This can reduce sensitivity to the particular training sample but can also increase systematic error if the model becomes too restricted.

Therefore both Ridge and Lasso sit inside the same broader bias-variance story.

The supplied Lecture08.pdf establishes the general trend: lower flexibility tends toward higher bias and lower variance; higher flexibility tends toward lower bias and higher variance.

22. Why Lasso Is Not Automatically “Better” Than Ridge

These methods encode different forms of complexity control.

The appropriate choice depends on the modelling objective and data structure.

For example, a practitioner may value:

  • a dense but stable set of shrunken coefficients,
  • or a sparse representation with some exact zeros.

That is a modelling decision, not a universal “winner” question.

For your exam, focus on the documented structural differences rather than memorizing a blanket claim that one method always outperforms the other.

23. A Practical Decision Framework

When reasoning about a problem, ask these questions in order.

Question 1 — Do I need regularization?

Think about model flexibility, overfitting risk, feature count, multicollinearity, and generalization.

Question 2 — Do I want sparse coefficients?

If exact zeros are useful to the modelling objective, L1/Lasso is the natural penalty to examine.

Question 3 — Do I primarily want smooth coefficient shrinkage?

L2/Ridge provides that behaviour.

Question 4 — Are predictors measured on very different scales?

Scale or otherwise account for units before interpreting the penalty.

Question 5 — How should \(\lambda\) be chosen?

Use a validation strategy or cross-validation rather than choosing it by looking repeatedly at the final test result.

25. Exam Comparison — 2 Marks

Question

State two differences between Ridge and Lasso Regression.

High-quality answer

  1. Ridge uses an L2/squared-coefficient penalty, while Lasso uses an L1/absolute-value penalty.
  2. Ridge generally shrinks coefficients toward zero, while Lasso can set some coefficients exactly to zero, enabling sparse fitted models.

That is enough for a direct 2-mark response.

28. Numerical Problem Type Recognition

A useful exam skill is identifying the question before calculating.

Type 1 — Compute Ridge penalty

Look for:

\[ \sum_jw_j^2. \]

Type 2 — Compute Lasso penalty

Look for:

\[ \sum_j|w_j|. \]

Type 3 — Compute total regularized objective

Always do:

data loss
+
λ × penalty

Type 4 — Derive Ridge solution

Use gradients and the stationary-point equation.

Type 5 — Explain Lasso sparsity

Use:

L1 penalty
→ kink at 0
→ diamond geometry
→ corners on coordinate axes
→ exact zeros can occur

Type 6 — Choose between them conceptually

Ask whether sparse selection or smooth shrinkage is the modelling objective.

29. Common Misconceptions

Misconception 1

“L1 and L2 only differ numerically.”

No. Their geometry and smoothness differ, which changes optimization and coefficient behaviour.

Misconception 2

“Ridge cannot have a coefficient equal to zero.”

It can be zero in special data configurations. The important standard distinction is that exact sparsity is not the characteristic mechanism Ridge is designed to induce.

Misconception 3

“Lasso always gives a sparse model.”

Not necessarily. Sparsity depends on the problem and regularization strength.

Misconception 4

“More zero coefficients means the model is automatically better.”

Sparsity is a structural property, not a universal performance guarantee.

Misconception 5

“A larger penalty always reduces test error.”

No. Regularization strength is a model-selection parameter and can be too small or too large.

Misconception 6

“Correlation means Ridge and Lasso behave identically.”

No. Both shrink coefficients, but their treatment of correlated predictors can differ, especially in sparse selection behaviour.

Misconception 7

“Ridge and Lasso use the same \(\lambda\) scale.”

Not necessarily. The objective conventions and penalty magnitudes differ, so \(\lambda\) values are not directly comparable across methods without considering the scaling.

34. Mini Viva

Imagine the examiner asks rapidly:

Examiner

Why do we regularize?

You

To control model complexity and reduce overfitting risk by penalizing coefficient magnitude in the objective.

Examiner

Ridge penalty?

You

\[ \lambda\sum_jw_j^2. \]

Examiner

Lasso penalty?

You

\[ \lambda\sum_j|w_j|. \]

Examiner

Big behavioural difference?

You

Lasso can produce exact zero coefficients; Ridge is characterized by smooth shrinkage.

Examiner

Why?

You

Because L1 has a non-smooth point at zero and diamond-shaped geometry with corners on coordinate axes.

Examiner

Does large \(\lambda\) always improve the model?

You

No. It means stronger regularization and can produce underfitting if excessive.

That sequence is an excellent oral-revision drill.

35. One-Page Mental Summary

REGULARIZATION
                           │
                 loss + λ × penalty
                           │
              ┌────────────┴────────────┐
              │                         │
            RIDGE                     LASSO
              │                         │
             L2                        L1
              │                         │
          Σ w²                         Σ |w|
              │                         │
        smooth boundary          sharp corners
              │                         │
      coefficient shrinkage      shrinkage + zeros possible
              │                         │
        generally dense           potentially sparse
              │                         │
        simple closed form         no analogous simple
        for standard LS              inverse-form solution

At the centre of the entire picture is one idea:

Regularization changes what counts as an acceptable model by trading data fit against coefficient complexity.
