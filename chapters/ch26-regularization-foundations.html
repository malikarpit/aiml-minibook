1. Why This Chapter Exists

You have already learned an important pattern:

A model that becomes more flexible can fit the training data better, but it may generalize worse.

Lecture 10 takes this idea one step further. It points out that even a model that is called linear can become extremely flexible when we give it a rich feature representation. The lecture then asks how this flexibility can be controlled in a principled way and identifies regularization as the natural direction. [Lecture10 copy.pdf]

This chapter explains that transition carefully.

The goal is not to jump immediately into a penalty formula and ask you to memorize it.

We want to understand:

More expressive representation
          ↓
More flexibility
          ↓
Can fit complicated patterns
          ↓
Can also overfit
          ↓
Need a way to control complexity
          ↓
REGULARIZATION

2. One-Minute Intuition

English

Regularization is a family of techniques that changes the learning objective so that a model is encouraged not only to fit the training data, but also to avoid unnecessarily complex parameter settings.

A useful beginner mental model is:

“Fit the data, but don't become unnecessarily complicated.”

👨‍🏫 Hinglish
> Data ko fit karna hai, lekin model ko itna “wild” nahi banana ki woh training examples ko yaad karke new data par fail ho jaye. Regularization is therefore connected directly to the problem of **overfitting**.

3. Start With a Familiar Situation

Suppose you have a few points describing:

House size → house price

A straight line may capture the broad trend.

Now imagine that you create many additional features:

\[ x, x^2, x^3, \ldots, x^{18} \]

The model may still be linear in its parameters:

\[ f(x)=\sum_i w_i\phi_i(x) \]

but the feature representation is now extremely rich.

Lecture 10 gives exactly this kind of example, showing that a linear model can become highly flexible when many features are supplied. [Lecture10 copy.pdf]

4. The Important Distinction: Linear in What?

This causes a common beginner confusion.

Consider:

\[ f(x)=w_0+w_1x+w_2x^2 \]

This is nonlinear as a function of the input \(x\), because of the \(x^2\) term.

But it is still linear in the parameters \(w_0,w_1,w_2\).

That distinction matters because many ML algorithms are expressed as linear combinations of features:

\[ f(x)=\mathbf w^T\boldsymbol\phi(x) \]

where \(\boldsymbol\phi(x)\) is a feature representation.

👨‍🏫 Hinglish
> “Model x ke respect mein curved ho sakta hai, lekin weights ke respect mein still linear ho sakta hai.”

5. Why Rich Features Increase Flexibility

Imagine giving an artist only a pencil.

They can draw, but their options are limited.

Now give them:

  • pencils of many hardnesses,
  • pens,
  • brushes,
  • colours,
  • rulers,
  • stencils,
  • a huge collection of shapes.

They can represent much more complicated pictures.

Features play a related role for a model.

Few features
    ↓
Limited representation
    ↓
Lower flexibility

Many / rich features
    ↓
More possible functions
    ↓
Higher flexibility

Higher flexibility is not automatically bad.

It becomes dangerous when the model can adapt to noise or accidental details in the training set.

6. Connection to Hypothesis Classes

Recall the idea of a hypothesis class from C16.

A hypothesis class \(\mathcal H\) is the collection of candidate functions the learning procedure is allowed to choose from.

Changing the feature representation changes what kinds of functions can be represented.

Therefore:

Feature engineering can effectively change the flexibility of the hypothesis class.

This is why Lecture 10 can move from feature expansion to regularization without suddenly introducing an unrelated topic.

7. A Visual Model of Flexibility

MODEL FLEXIBILITY
                    │
      ┌─────────────┼─────────────┐
      ▼             ▼             ▼
   Too low        Useful        Too high
      │             │             │
      ▼             ▼             ▼
Underfitting   Generalization  Overfitting
      │                           │
 High bias                  High variance

This connects directly to Lecture 8's bias-variance treatment and Lecture 10's motivation for regularization.

8. Why Training Error Alone Cannot Decide

Suppose two models are trained on the same data.

Model A

Training error:

\[ 2 \]

Model B

Training error:

\[ 0.1 \]

It is tempting to declare Model B better.

But that ignores generalization.

The second model might be so flexible that it memorizes the training set.

Therefore model selection must consider data not used to fit the final parameters, typically through validation procedures, while a final test set is kept untouched for final evaluation. This matches the methodology explained in your Lecture 8. [Lecture08.pdf]

9. The Regularization Idea

We start from a data-fitting objective.

For least squares:

\[ L_{data}(\mathbf w) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i-\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i)^2 \]

A regularized objective adds another term:

\[ \boxed{ L_{regularized} = L_{data} + \lambda\,\Omega(\mathbf w) } \]

where:

  • \(L_{data}\) measures how well the model fits the data;
  • \(\Omega(\mathbf w)\) measures some notion of parameter complexity;
  • \(\lambda\ge 0\) controls how strongly that complexity is penalized.

This general form is a universal ML formulation that expands beyond the exact material in Lecture 10. Lecture 10 provides the motivation for this move; the detailed Ridge/Lasso forms are taught in the next chapters as the syllabus-required expansion.

10. What Does \(\lambda\) Do?

Think of \(\lambda\) as a strictness dial.

\(\lambda=0\)

No regularization penalty.

Fit data freely

Small \(\lambda\)

Fit data strongly
+ mild complexity control

Large \(\lambda\)

Complexity is penalized strongly
→ parameters are pushed toward simpler configurations
👨‍🏫 Hinglish
> “Lambda decide karta hai ki model ko complexity ke liye kitna fine dena hai.” That is an analogy, not a mathematical definition.

11. Why the Penalty Can Help Generalization

Without regularization, a highly flexible model may find parameter values that reduce training error dramatically.

With regularization, the model is asked to optimize a combined objective.

Conceptually:

TOTAL OBJECTIVE
                           │
              ┌────────────┴────────────┐
              ▼                         ▼
         Data fit                    Complexity
       “errors kam karo”          “weights controlled rakho”

The learned model therefore trades a little data fit for a potentially simpler parameter configuration.

The important idea is not:

“Regularization always lowers every kind of error.”

The correct idea is:

Regularization changes the optimization problem so that fitting the training data is balanced against a complexity preference.

12. The Underfitting–Overfitting Connection

Regularization is not magic.

If the penalty is extremely strong, the model may become too constrained.

Then we can get underfitting.

So there is again a trade-off:

Weak / no regularization
        ↓
More freedom
        ↓
Possible overfitting

Strong regularization
        ↓
Less freedom
        ↓
Possible underfitting

The goal is appropriate control, not “maximum regularization.”

13. Why Not Simply Delete Features?

You might ask:

“If too many features cause trouble, why not remove them?”

Sometimes that works.

But sometimes the extra features contain useful information.

For example:

\[ x, x^2, x^3, \ldots \]

may allow the model to describe a genuinely nonlinear relationship.

Regularization gives us another option:

Keep a rich representation, but discourage unnecessarily extreme parameter choices.

This becomes especially important in high-dimensional and highly flexible models.

14. Two Famous Regularization Families

Your syllabus explicitly requires:

  • Ridge Regression
  • Lasso Regression

They differ primarily in the penalty they add to the objective.

Ridge

Uses an \(L_2\)-type penalty:

\[ \Omega(\mathbf w)=\|\mathbf w\|_2^2 \]

Lasso

Uses an \(L_1\)-type penalty:

\[ \Omega(\mathbf w)=\|\mathbf w\|_1 \]

Therefore:

\[ L_{ridge}=L_{data}+\lambda\|\mathbf w\|_2^2 \]

and:

\[ L_{lasso}=L_{data}+\lambda\|\mathbf w\|_1 \]

These exact penalty forms are the universal expansion we will study in C27/C28; Lecture 10 itself provides the motivation for regularization rather than a complete Ridge/Lasso derivation. [Lecture10 copy.pdf]

15. What Does “Complexity” Mean Here?

A very useful beginner question is:

“How can a vector of numbers have complexity?”

In regularized linear models, the penalty is often used as a proxy for parameter magnitude.

For Ridge:

\[ \|\mathbf w\|_2^2= \sum_j w_j^2 \]

For Lasso:

\[ \|\mathbf w\|_1= \sum_j|w_j| \]

Large weights contribute more penalty.

👨‍🏫 Hinglish
> “Model ke weights bahut extreme honge toh objective usko extra cost dega.” The exact relationship between parameter magnitude and generalization is subtler than saying “small weights always mean a simpler model,” but the penalty gives the optimizer a mathematically explicit preference for smaller parameter values.

16. Geometry Preview

Regularization can also be understood geometrically.

Imagine that the data-fitting objective gives a landscape of possible parameter choices.

Regularization adds a preference for parameter vectors inside a particular region.

A useful conceptual picture is:

Data-fit contours
       ↓
Choose the best point
       │
       + complexity constraint / penalty
       ↓
Choose a point that balances both goals

In later chapters we will see why the geometry of an \(L_2\) penalty and an \(L_1\) penalty leads to different behaviour.

17. Why Regularization Appears After Feature Expansion

This is a key lecture-to-syllabus connection.

Linear Regression
       ↓
Feature expansion
       ↓
Rich representation
       ↓
Much higher flexibility
       ↓
Overfitting risk
       ↓
How do we control it?
       ↓
Regularization
       ↓
Ridge / Lasso

This is essentially the narrative of your Lecture 10.

18. Connection to Neural Networks

Lecture 10 also makes another conceptual connection:

A neural network can be viewed as learning a feature representation, after which a final prediction can be made from that learned representation.

Conceptually:

\[ \boldsymbol\phi_\theta(x) \longrightarrow \mathbf w^T\boldsymbol\phi_\theta(x) \]

The important lesson for now is:

Representation power and model flexibility are related.

Later, regularization will reappear in neural networks through ideas such as weight penalties, dropout and early stopping.

Those later techniques belong to the deeper learning chapters and are not interchangeable with the simple Ridge/Lasso treatment here.

19. A Concrete Analogy: Exam Preparation

Imagine you are preparing for your university exam.

No regularization mindset

“I will memorize every possible wording, every exact question, and every special case.”

This gives you huge flexibility for matching training examples—the previous questions—but may not generalize.

Regularized mindset

“I will learn the general concepts, but I will also constrain myself to a simpler, more disciplined rule set.”

Again, this is only an analogy.

The mathematical reality is about modifying the objective function.

20. A Numerical Mini-Example

Suppose two candidate parameter vectors have the following data-fit losses:

\[ L_{data}(w_A)=4 \]
\[ L_{data}(w_B)=3 \]

But suppose:

\[ \Omega(w_A)=1 \]
\[ \Omega(w_B)=10 \]

and:

\[ \lambda=0.2 \]

Then:

\[ L_{reg}(w_A)=4+0.2(1)=4.2 \]

and:

\[ L_{reg}(w_B)=3+0.2(10)=5 \]

Without regularization, B looks better because 3 < 4.

With this penalty, A becomes preferable because 4.2 < 5.

👨‍🏫 Hinglish
> B ne training data ko better fit kiya, lekin uska complexity penalty bahut zyada tha. Combined objective mein A win karta hai. This example is purely explanatory; the exact choice of penalty determines the actual values in a real model.

21. Common Misconceptions

Misconception 1

“Regularization means removing features.”

Not necessarily. It often keeps the representation but penalizes parameter configurations.

Misconception 2

“Regularization always improves training error.”

No. Adding a penalty can deliberately accept a worse data fit in exchange for complexity control.

Misconception 3

“Larger \(\lambda\) is always better.”

No. Excessive regularization can produce underfitting.

Misconception 4

“Ridge and Lasso are the same.”

No. They use different norms and can behave differently.

Misconception 5

“A linear model cannot overfit.”

Lecture 10 explicitly motivates the opposite: a linear model can be highly flexible when the feature representation is rich. [Lecture10 copy.pdf]

Misconception 6

“Regularization is only for neural networks.”

No. It is central to classical ML too.

22. Comparison: No Regularization vs Ridge vs Lasso

Property No regularization Ridge Lasso
Objective Data fit only Data fit + \(L_2\) penalty Data fit + \(L_1\) penalty
Main control None Penalizes squared magnitude Penalizes absolute magnitude
Typical effect More parameter freedom Shrinks weights toward zero Can drive some weights exactly to zero
Feature selection tendency No Usually not exact zeroing Stronger exact-zero tendency
Main intuition Fit freely Keep weights small Encourage sparsity

The final two rows describe the standard behaviour of the corresponding regularizers and will be derived in the dedicated Ridge/Lasso chapters.

23. Mathematical Lens

You should now understand these pieces before C27:

Ordinary least squares

\[ L_{OLS}(w)=\|Xw-y\|_2^2 \]

General regularized form

\[ L(w)=L_{data}(w)+\lambda\Omega(w) \]

Ridge preview

\[ L_{ridge}(w)=\|Xw-y\|_2^2+\lambda\|w\|_2^2 \]

Lasso preview

\[ L_{lasso}(w)=\|Xw-y\|_2^2+\lambda\|w\|_1 \]

You will learn why these terms have their particular forms in the following chapters.
