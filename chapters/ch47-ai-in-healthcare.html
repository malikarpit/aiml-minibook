1. Why This Chapter Exists

Healthcare is one of the clearest examples of the difference between:

building a predictive model

and

building a safe AI-enabled decision system.

Consider a model that predicts whether a patient may require additional clinical attention.

Mathematically, the task can look familiar:

\[ X\rightarrow f_\theta(X)\rightarrow \hat p\rightarrow \hat Y. \]

But the consequences are different from a low-stakes prediction such as recommending a movie.

A false negative may mean a genuinely important case is not escalated.

A false positive may create unnecessary tests, workload, anxiety or resource use.

Therefore healthcare gives us a concrete environment in which to combine nearly everything learned so far:

DATA
 ↓
PREPROCESSING
 ↓
MODEL
 ↓
PROBABILITY / SCORE
 ↓
THRESHOLD OR DECISION POLICY
 ↓
CLINICAL WORKFLOW
 ↓
HUMAN REVIEW
 ↓
PATIENT OUTCOME
 ↓
MONITORING

3. One-Minute Intuition

Suppose an AI system assigns a risk score:

\[ p=0.82. \]

What does that number mean?

It does not automatically mean:

“The patient definitely has the disease.”

A model output may instead represent a probability-like estimate under a particular model, training distribution and prediction task.

Then someone must decide:

p = 0.82
   ↓
What threshold?
   ↓
What action?
   ↓
Who reviews it?
   ↓
What happens if wrong?

That chain is the heart of healthcare AI.

4. Healthcare AI Is Not One Problem

“AI in healthcare” is not a single algorithm.

Common task families include:

Task family Example AI question
Classification Is this case likely to belong to class 1 or class 0?
Risk prediction What is the estimated probability of a future event?
Medical imaging What patterns appear in an image?
Segmentation Which pixels/regions correspond to a structure of interest?
NLP What information can be extracted from clinical text?
Forecasting What may happen later in a time series?
Retrieval / summarization Which information from a large record is relevant?
Decision support Which cases should receive additional attention?

The underlying ML family can therefore differ.

image      → CNN / deep vision
text       → NLP models
sequences  → RNN/LSTM or other sequence models
structured → regression / trees / neural nets

The application context stays healthcare even when the model family changes.

5. Real-World Analogy — Hospital Triage Queue

Imagine a hospital emergency department.

There are many patients but limited attention and resources at a particular moment.

A triage process attempts to identify which cases need faster attention.

Now replace one part of the process with an ML model.

Mapping

Triage process ML system
Patient information Feature vector \(x\)
Triage assessment Model score
Priority category Predicted class
Escalation rule Decision threshold / policy
Clinician Human reviewer
Actual condition Ground truth / later outcome
Missed urgent case False negative
Unnecessary escalation False positive

Where the analogy breaks

Real clinical processes are complex, involve multiple data sources and may include information unavailable to the model.

The analogy is useful only for understanding resource allocation under uncertainty.

Return to the technical model:

Healthcare AI often combines prediction with a downstream decision policy.

7. Healthcare Data Types

A healthcare system may involve several data structures.

7.1 Tabular data

Examples:

  • age;
  • laboratory measurements;
  • vital signs;
  • coded diagnoses;
  • medication-related variables.

Representation:

\[ X\in\mathbb R^{n\times d} \]

where each row represents an example and each column a feature.

7.2 Images

Medical images can be represented as grids/tensors.

Conceptually:

\[ X\in\mathbb R^{H\times W\times C} \]

or with a batch dimension as well.

This is where CNN concepts from C36 become relevant.

7.3 Text

Clinical notes may contain:

symptoms
history
observations
plans
results

This connects to C43 NLP preprocessing and text classification.

7.4 Time series

Repeated measurements may form:

\[ x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_T. \]

This connects to sequence modelling from C37–C38.

8. The First Question: What Is the Target?

A common beginner mistake is to start with:

“Which AI model should we use?”

Start earlier.

Ask:

What exactly are we trying to predict or decide?

For example:

Patient data
   ↓
What target?
   ├── current condition?
   ├── future event?
   ├── image finding?
   ├── treatment response?
   └── workflow priority?

These are different learning tasks.

The target also determines how the model should be evaluated.

9. Classification Formulation

Suppose a healthcare task is binary classification.

Training data:

\[ D=\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n} \]

with:

\[ y_i\in\{0,1\}. \]

A model estimates:

\[ \hat p_i=P(Y=1\mid X=x_i) \]

or a score interpreted through the model's output semantics.

Then a decision rule may be:

\[ \hat y_i= \begin{cases} 1,&\hat p_i\ge t\\ 0,&\hat p_i\lt t \end{cases} \]

The three quantities must be kept separate:

MODEL
  ↓
score / probability-like output
  ↓
THRESHOLD
  ↓
CLASSIFICATION DECISION

This distinction is critical in healthcare.

10. Why Probability and Decision Are Different

Suppose:

\[ \hat p=0.64. \]

With threshold \(0.50\):

\[ \hat y=1. \]

With threshold \(0.70\):

\[ \hat y=0. \]

Same model.

Same patient input.

Different decision rule.

Therefore:

A model output does not determine the final action unless a decision policy is specified.

11. Worked Example — Threshold Change

Suppose five patients receive predicted scores:

Patient Score
P1 0.91
P2 0.73
P3 0.64
P4 0.42
P5 0.18

Threshold = 0.50

Positive predictions:

P1, P2, P3

Threshold = 0.80

Positive prediction:

P1

What changed?

Not the learned parameters.

The decision policy changed.

This is why healthcare AI must be evaluated at the level of the operational decision, not just the model score.

12. False Positives and False Negatives in Healthcare

The confusion matrix now becomes domain-specific.

Suppose positive means:

“Send this case for additional review.”

Then:

True positive

The system escalates a case that genuinely needs additional review.

False positive

The system escalates a case that does not require the additional review.

True negative

The system does not escalate a case that does not require escalation.

False negative

The system fails to escalate a case that does require additional review.

Notice that the practical meaning depends on the definition of the positive class.

Never discuss “false positive” without first stating what positive means.

13. Error Cost Must Be Defined in Context

Suppose the costs are represented abstractly as:

\[ C_{FP},\qquad C_{FN}. \]

The cost of the decision system depends on:

  • how frequent each event is;
  • the relative cost of false positives and false negatives;
  • downstream actions;
  • available human review;
  • resource constraints.

A simple expected-cost view is:

\[ E[C] \approx C_{FP}P(FP)+C_{FN}P(FN) \]

for a simplified binary setting.

This is a conceptual teaching equation, not a complete clinical utility model.

The key idea is:

The “best” threshold depends on the consequences of errors, not only on a generic accuracy score.

14. Guided Example — Why Accuracy Alone Can Mislead

Suppose a screening dataset has 1,000 cases.

Only 20 are truly positive.

A naive model predicts everyone as negative.

Then:

  • TP = 0
  • FN = 20
  • TN = 980
  • FP = 0

Accuracy:

\[ \frac{0+980}{1000}=98\%. \]

That looks impressive.

But recall is:

\[ \frac{0}{0+20}=0. \]

So the model finds none of the positive cases.

Lesson

In imbalanced healthcare classification, accuracy can look high even when the model completely fails at the clinically relevant positive class.

The specific practical importance depends on the task definition, but the mathematical lesson is general.

15. Evaluation Should Be Multi-Dimensional

A healthcare AI evaluation may include:

discrimination
   ↓
calibration
   ↓
group-wise performance
   ↓
robustness / distribution shift
   ↓
human workflow interaction
   ↓
operational consequences

Relevant quantities may include:

  • sensitivity / recall;
  • specificity;
  • precision;
  • F1;
  • ROC-AUC;
  • calibration-related measures;
  • subgroup error rates;
  • latency and resource use.

No single metric describes every important property.

16. ROC Curve Connection

For a binary classifier, changing the threshold changes:

  • true-positive rate;
  • false-positive rate.

That produces an ROC curve.

Conceptually:

threshold high
   ↓
fewer positive predictions
   ↓
usually lower TPR and lower FPR

threshold lower
   ↓
more positive predictions
   ↓
usually higher TPR and higher FPR

The ROC curve therefore visualizes a family of operating points.

A healthcare team still needs to choose an operating point based on the task and costs.

AUC summarizes ranking ability across thresholds, but it does not by itself choose the clinical decision threshold.

17. Calibration — A Different Question from Classification Accuracy

Suppose a model outputs:

\[ 0.8. \]

Calibration asks a probability-oriented question:

Among cases receiving predictions around 0.8, does the observed frequency of the target event correspond appropriately to that level?

This is different from asking only whether the class label is correct.

So:

Discrimination
→ can the model rank cases?

Classification threshold
→ what action do we take?

Calibration
→ do probability-like outputs correspond to observed frequencies?

Do not interchange these concepts.

18. Imaging Systems — CNN Connection

Suppose the input is a medical image.

A CNN may process it as:

image
 ↓
convolution
 ↓
feature maps
 ↓
activation
 ↓
downsampling / further convolution
 ↓
representation
 ↓
prediction head

From C36, remember:

  • kernels are learned local operators;
  • filters are shared across spatial locations;
  • feature maps describe responses to learned patterns.

In healthcare, the model may be trained to identify image patterns associated with a target label.

But the ethical and clinical questions remain:

  • What data were used?
  • What population was represented?
  • What imaging devices were included?
  • What happens when the acquisition protocol changes?
  • Which errors matter?
  • Who reviews the output?

The CNN does not remove those questions.

19. Clinical Text — NLP Connection

From C43:

raw note
 ↓
tokenization / normalization
 ↓
representation
 ↓
classifier / sequence model
 ↓
prediction

Possible task types include:

  • document classification;
  • information extraction;
  • routing;
  • summarization support;
  • retrieval.

The same caution applies:

Language models can process text without necessarily understanding it in the same way a clinician does.

For the textbook, the important technical idea is that NLP systems inherit the properties of their training data and representation.

20. Sequential Data — RNN/LSTM Connection

Healthcare data can be longitudinal.

A patient may have observations over time:

\[ x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_T. \]

RNNs represent this using a hidden state:

\[ h_t=f(x_t,h_{t-1}). \]

An LSTM adds gated memory mechanisms.

The application mapping is:

patient history
      ↓
sequence model
      ↓
representation of temporal pattern
      ↓
risk / classification output

Again, good sequence modelling does not automatically imply safe deployment.

21. Missing Data

Healthcare data often contain missing values.

A critical question is:

Why is the value missing?

Missingness can arise because:

  • a test was not ordered;
  • a measurement was unavailable;
  • a patient did not attend;
  • a device failed;
  • a clinician chose a different pathway.

Therefore:

missing value
   ≠
random empty cell

The missingness mechanism may itself carry information.

But using it carelessly can also create leakage or spurious correlations.

22. Data Leakage — A Major Healthcare Trap

Suppose a model is intended to predict whether a patient will require an intervention before that intervention occurs.

Now imagine one input feature is generated only after the intervention has begun.

The model can appear extremely accurate because it has access to future information.

prediction time ───────────────►
       |             |
       |             └── future event
       |
       └── allowed information

Using information from after the prediction point creates temporal leakage.

The fix is to define the prediction timestamp explicitly and ensure training features respect it.

23. Dataset Shift Between Hospitals

Suppose the model is trained in Hospital A.

Hospital A
patients + devices + protocols
          ↓
       training

Deployment occurs in Hospital B.

Hospital B
patients + devices + protocols
          ↓
       deployment

Differences may arise in:

  • patient population;
  • measurement devices;
  • coding practices;
  • clinical workflows;
  • image acquisition;
  • missingness patterns.

Therefore:

\[ P_{train}(X,Y)\neq P_{deploy}(X,Y) \]

is a useful conceptual warning.

A model should be evaluated for the environment in which it will actually be used.

24. Worked Case — A Risk-Scoring Tool

Suppose a hospital uses an ML model to route patients into three workflow categories:

LOW
MEDIUM
HIGH

The model receives:

\[ x=(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_d). \]

It computes a score:

\[ s=f_\theta(x). \]

Then the policy is:

\[ \text{LOW if }s\lt t_1 \]
\[ \text{MEDIUM if }t_1\le s\lt t_2 \]
\[ \text{HIGH if }s\ge t_2. \]

Now evaluate the entire system.

Question 1 — Model

Does the score discriminate between relevant cases?

Question 2 — Calibration

Do similar scores correspond to similar observed risk?

Question 3 — Thresholds

What happens around \(t_1\) and \(t_2\)?

Question 4 — Groups

Do error rates differ by relevant population?

Question 5 — Workflow

Can clinicians challenge the routing decision?

Question 6 — Monitoring

What happens when patient mix or data collection changes?

This is the difference between model evaluation and system evaluation.

25. Fairness in Healthcare

From C46, fairness requires an explicit criterion.

In healthcare, group-wise evaluation may examine:

\[ TPR_A, TPR_B \]

or:

\[ FPR_A, FPR_B. \]

But comparing these quantities requires context.

A difference might come from:

  • data representation;
  • measurement differences;
  • label quality;
  • model behaviour;
  • different base rates;
  • different workflows;
  • different thresholds.

Therefore:

A fairness metric identifies a pattern; it does not automatically identify its cause.

This distinction is extremely important in technical analysis.

26. Explainability in Clinical Settings

Suppose a model says:

“High risk.”

A clinician may reasonably want to know:

  • which inputs contributed to the prediction;
  • whether the case resembles training data;
  • how uncertain the prediction is;
  • whether important measurements were missing;
  • whether the model has known failure modes.

There are different forms of explanation.

Global explanation

How does the model generally behave?

Local explanation

Why did this particular input receive this output?

Example-based explanation

Which similar cases or patterns influenced the model?

For high-stakes systems, the useful question is not simply:

“Can we explain it?”

but:

“Is the explanation reliable, appropriate for the user, and useful for the decision?”

27. Human Oversight Design

A human-review system can be structured in several ways.

Pattern A — AI recommends, clinician decides

AI
 ↓
recommendation
 ↓
clinician
 ↓
final decision

Pattern B — AI routes uncertain cases

AI
 ↓
confidence / uncertainty
 ├── confident → automated route
 └── uncertain → human review

Pattern C — AI is advisory only

AI
 ↓
additional information
 ↓
clinician integrates it with other evidence

The correct design depends on the task, consequences and operational setting.

28. Why “AI = Doctor Replacement” Is the Wrong Mental Model for This Chapter

The relevant technical framing is not a binary choice between:

human OR AI

Instead consider:

human + data + model + workflow + oversight

30. Incident Response

A responsible system should define what happens when a model fails.

Possible steps:

  1. detect the incident;
  2. preserve relevant records;
  3. assess the impact;
  4. determine whether the model or workflow contributed;
  5. restrict or suspend use when appropriate;
  6. communicate to responsible stakeholders;
  7. correct and revalidate;
  8. record the change.

The exact operational process is organization-specific.

The AI/ML lesson is:

Accountability requires a process for responding to failure, not merely a statement that errors are possible.

32. Common Mistakes

Trap 1 — “The model predicts disease, therefore it diagnoses disease.”

Correction:

A prediction task must be defined precisely, and the downstream clinical meaning depends on how the system is designed and used.

Trap 2 — “Sensitivity is always the most important metric.”

Correction:

Metric importance is task-dependent. False positives, false negatives, resource constraints and workflow consequences all matter.

Trap 3 — “AUC tells us which threshold to use.”

Correction:

AUC summarizes ranking behaviour across thresholds; selecting an operating threshold is a separate decision problem.

Trap 4 — “A hospital dataset is automatically representative.”

Correction:

Representation depends on who, where, when and how the data were generated.

Trap 5 — “No missing values means the dataset is high quality.”

Correction:

Missingness may have been transformed, imputed or encoded. Data quality is broader than the presence of empty cells.

Trap 6 — “A more complex neural network automatically solves clinical risk.”

Correction:

Architecture affects predictive modelling, but it does not solve target definition, dataset shift, threshold policy, fairness, governance or accountability.

37. Formula Sheet

Binary decision threshold

\[ \hat y= \begin{cases} 1,&\hat p\ge t\\ 0,&\hat p\lt t \end{cases} \]

Recall / sensitivity

\[ Recall=\frac{TP}{TP+FN} \]

Specificity

\[ Specificity=\frac{TN}{TN+FP} \]

Precision

\[ Precision=\frac{TP}{TP+FP} \]

False-positive rate

\[ FPR=\frac{FP}{FP+TN}=1-Specificity \]

Simplified expected-cost idea

\[ E[C]\approx C_{FP}P(FP)+C_{FN}P(FN) \]

Use this as an intuition-building approximation, not as a complete clinical utility model.

Probability

\[ P(Y=1\mid X=x) \]

Confusion-matrix rates

Ratios of counts form sensitivity, specificity, precision and FPR.

Expected cost
