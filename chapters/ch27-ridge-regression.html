1. Why Ridge Regression Exists

Start with the problem we already know.

Ordinary least squares asks:

“Which parameter vector fits the training data best?”

Mathematically:

\[ \min_{\mathbf w}\;\|X\mathbf w-\mathbf y\|_2^2 \]

But in a highly flexible feature space, we may prefer a solution that also keeps the parameter vector controlled.

Ridge regression adds exactly that preference.

\[ \boxed{ \min_{\mathbf w} \left[ \|X\mathbf w-\mathbf y\|_2^2 + \lambda\|\mathbf w\|_2^2 \right] } \]

where \(\lambda\ge 0\).

This is the central formula of the chapter.

2. One-Minute Intuition

Imagine you are adjusting many knobs on a machine.

Without Ridge:

“Turn every knob as aggressively as necessary to match today's measurements.”

With Ridge:

“Match the measurements, but avoid unnecessarily extreme knob settings.”

👨‍🏫 Hinglish
> “Sirf training data ko fit nahi karna; weights ko bhi unnecessarily bada nahi hone dena.” This is not a rule saying that every small weight is automatically correct. It is a **penalty-based preference** built into the objective.

3. The Two Competing Goals

Ridge combines two terms.

Goal A — Fit the data

\[ \|Xw-y\|_2^2 \]

Small value means predictions are close to observed targets in squared-error terms.

Goal B — Control parameter magnitude

\[ \lambda\|w\|_2^2 \]

Large weights create a larger penalty.

Together:

RIDGE OBJECTIVE
                      │
          ┌───────────┴───────────┐
          ▼                       ▼
      DATA FIT                WEIGHT CONTROL
          │                       │
     “fit samples”        “avoid large weights”
          └───────────┬───────────┘
                      ▼
             BALANCED SOLUTION

4. What Is the \(L_2\) Norm?

Before Ridge makes sense, understand the penalty itself.

For a parameter vector:

\[ \mathbf w= \begin{bmatrix} w_1\\ w_2\\ \vdots\\ w_d \end{bmatrix} \]

its squared \(L_2\) norm is:

\[ \boxed{ \|\mathbf w\|_2^2 = \sum_{j=1}^{d}w_j^2 } \]

Example:

\[ \mathbf w=(3,-4) \]

Then:

\[ \|w\|_2^2=3^2+(-4)^2=25 \]

The norm itself is:

\[ \|w\|_2=\sqrt{25}=5 \]

Important

Ridge normally uses the squared \(L_2\) norm:

\[ \|w\|_2^2 \]

not merely \(\|w\|_2\).

5. Why Square the Weights?

The square has useful mathematical properties.

For example:

  • positive and negative weights both contribute positively;
  • larger magnitudes are penalized more strongly;
  • the penalty is smooth/differentiable;
  • the resulting objective remains convex for ordinary least squares with Ridge.

The first three points help build intuition; the convexity point is a mathematical property used later.

6. Ridge vs Ordinary Least Squares

Ordinary Least Squares

\[ J_{OLS}(w)=\|Xw-y\|_2^2 \]

Ridge

\[ \boxed{ J_{Ridge}(w)=\|Xw-y\|_2^2+\lambda\|w\|_2^2 } \]

So Ridge is not a completely new regression model.

A helpful mental model is:

Ridge = least squares + a parameter-size penalty.

7. What Happens When \(\lambda\) Changes?

This is one of the most important exam ideas.

Case 1 — \(\lambda=0\)

\[ J_{Ridge}=J_{OLS} \]

Ridge reduces to ordinary least squares.

Case 2 — small \(\lambda\)

The data-fit term dominates.

The optimizer is encouraged to fit the data while mildly shrinking the coefficients.

Case 3 — large \(\lambda\)

The parameter penalty becomes much more important.

The model sacrifices more data fit to reduce coefficient magnitude.

Very large \(\lambda\)

The learned coefficients are strongly pushed toward zero.

This can make the model too simple and lead to underfitting.

8. The “Shrinkage” Idea

Ridge is commonly described as a shrinkage method.

That means:

The estimated coefficients are pulled toward zero compared with ordinary least squares.

Not necessarily exactly to zero.

This distinction becomes extremely important when comparing Ridge to Lasso.

👨‍🏫 Hinglish
> “Ridge weights ko zero ki direction mein kheenchta hai, lekin usually unhe exactly zero nahi banata.”

9. A Small Numerical Comparison

Suppose a particular parameter vector has:

\[ \|w\|_2^2=25 \]

and the data-fit term is:

\[ \|Xw-y\|_2^2=12 \]

If \(\lambda=0.1\)

\[ J=12+0.1(25) =14.5 \]

If \(\lambda=1\)

\[ J=12+1(25) =37 \]

If \(\lambda=10\)

\[ J=12+10(25) =262 \]

The same parameter vector receives increasingly strong penalties as \(\lambda\) grows.

10. Why Standardization Matters

This is a very important practical connection to your Lecture 9.

Suppose one feature is measured in:

  • rupees;
  • another in years;
  • another in square metres.

The raw numerical scales can be very different.

Ridge penalizes coefficients, so feature scaling affects how parameter magnitudes should be interpreted.

For this reason, standardization is commonly used before regularized linear modelling.

Your Lecture 9 explicitly teaches standardization and min-max scaling and explains why feature scaling matters for models and optimization. [Lecture09.pdf]

Practical mental rule

Scale features before Ridge when using the usual coefficient-penalty formulation, unless there is a deliberate reason not to.

The intercept is commonly handled separately rather than penalized.

11. Why Usually Do We Not Penalize the Intercept?

Suppose:

\[ f(x)=w_0+w_1x_1+\cdots+w_dx_d \]

The intercept \(w_0\) shifts the entire prediction function vertically.

The usual Ridge formulation penalizes the slope/feature coefficients rather than the intercept.

So conceptually:

w0 → baseline / offset
w1...wd → feature effects

Ridge penalty → usually w1...wd

This is a standard modelling convention rather than a universal law.

12. Ridge in Scalar Form

For a simple model:

\[ y_i\approx w_0+w_1x_i \]

a Ridge objective can be written as:

\[ J(w_0,w_1) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(y_i-w_0-w_1x_i\right)^2 + \lambda w_1^2 \]

where the intercept is not penalized under the standard convention.

This form is excellent for intuition because you can literally see the competition:

fit the points and keep \(w_1\) from becoming unnecessarily large.

13. Ridge in Vector/Matrix Form

For multiple features:

\[ J(w)=\|Xw-y\|_2^2+\lambda\|w\|_2^2 \]

Expand the second term:

\[ \|w\|_2^2=w^Tw \]

so:

\[ \boxed{ J(w)=\|Xw-y\|_2^2+\lambda w^Tw } \]

This form will be our starting point for the derivation.

14. Mathematical Derivation of the Ridge Gradient

This section is intentionally slower.

We want:

\[ \nabla_wJ(w) \]

Start with:

\[ J(w)= (Xw-y)^T(Xw-y)+\lambda w^Tw \]

The first term is the usual least-squares objective.

For ordinary least squares, its gradient is:

\[ 2X^T(Xw-y) \]

The gradient of:

\[ w^Tw \]

is:

\[ 2w \]

Therefore:

\[ \boxed{ \nabla_wJ(w) = 2X^T(Xw-y)+2\lambda w } \]

Set the gradient to zero at the optimum:

\[ 2X^T(Xw-y)+2\lambda w=0 \]

Divide by 2:

\[ X^T(Xw-y)+\lambda w=0 \]

Expand:

\[ X^TXw-X^Ty+\lambda w=0 \]

Collect terms:

\[ (X^TX+\lambda I)w=X^Ty \]

Therefore, when the inverse exists:

\[ \boxed{ \hat w_{Ridge} = (X^TX+\lambda I)^{-1}X^Ty } \]

This is the closed-form Ridge solution under this standard formulation.

15. Why Does the Identity Matrix Appear?

At first glance:

\[ X^TX+\lambda I \]

can look like magic.

It is not.

We obtained:

\[ X^TXw+ \lambda w \]

and we want to combine both terms into one matrix multiplying \(w\).

But:

\[ Iw=w \]

so:

\[ \lambda w=\lambda Iw \]

Therefore:

\[ X^TXw+\lambda Iw = (X^TX+\lambda I)w \]
👨‍🏫 Hinglish
> “Identity matrix ka kaam bas yeh hai ki vector ko change kiye bina matrix form mein likhne de.”

16. Why Ridge Helps With Singular or Ill-Conditioned Systems

Ordinary least squares can run into trouble when:

\[ X^TX \]

is singular or nearly singular.

Ridge instead works with:

\[ X^TX+\lambda I \]

for \(\lambda\gt 0\).

Adding the positive diagonal term changes the matrix properties and commonly improves numerical stability.

This is one of the important mathematical reasons Ridge is useful in addition to its statistical regularization role.

17. Connection to Collinearity

Suppose two features are strongly correlated.

Example:

  • house area in square feet;
  • house area in square metres.

They carry nearly duplicate information.

OLS may produce unstable coefficient estimates because many parameter combinations can fit the data similarly well.

Ridge discourages extreme coefficient magnitudes and can make the solution more stable.

Important wording

Ridge does not magically remove correlation from the data.

It regularizes the parameter estimation problem.

18. Geometric Intuition

A powerful way to understand Ridge is as a constrained problem.

Instead of saying:

“Minimize error plus a penalty,”

you can think, under an equivalent constrained formulation, roughly as:

“Minimize data error while keeping \(\|w\|_2^2\) below a chosen bound.”

Geometrically, the allowed parameter region is a sphere in parameter space.

For two parameters, the \(L_2\) constraint appears as a circle.

Parameter space

          w2
           ↑
        .--------.
      .'          '.
     /      ○       \
    |   allowed      |
     \   region     /
      '.          .'
        '--------'
           │
           └────────→ w1

The circle/sphere intuition becomes especially useful when we compare Ridge with Lasso.

19. Ridge Does Not Usually Produce Exact Zeros

This is one of the most important distinctions.

Ridge shrinks coefficients toward zero.

But because the \(L_2\) penalty is smooth, coefficients generally remain nonzero unless special circumstances cause an exact zero.

Lasso behaves differently because the \(L_1\) geometry has corners.

That distinction will become the central story of C28.

20. Ridge and Bias-Variance

Recall:

Less flexible model
→ higher bias
→ lower variance

More flexible model
→ lower bias
→ higher variance

Ridge deliberately introduces a constraint/penalty.

The intended effect is to reduce sensitivity to training-data fluctuations by discouraging extreme parameter configurations.

In exchange, some bias can be introduced.

Conceptually:

\[ \text{Variance}\;\downarrow \qquad \text{Bias}\;\uparrow \]

This is one reason Ridge can improve unseen-data performance even when training error becomes slightly worse.

21. Ridge Does Not “Fix” All Overfitting

Regularization is one tool among many.

Poor generalization can also arise from:

  • poor features;
  • data leakage;
  • distribution shift;
  • noisy labels;
  • insufficient training data;
  • inappropriate model assumptions;
  • poor validation methodology.

So:

Ridge is a mechanism for controlling parameter magnitude; it is not a universal cure for every ML problem.

22. The Effect of \(\lambda\) as a Learning Dial

A useful visual:

λ = 0
│
├── OLS behaviour
│
├── small λ
│
├── moderate λ
│
├── large λ
│
└── very large λ
        ↓
  coefficients strongly constrained
        ↓
  possible underfitting

The actual best value is selected using validation/model-selection procedures, not by peeking at the final test set.

23. Worked Numerical Example — Objective Value

Suppose:

\[ \|Xw-y\|_2^2=20 \]

and:

\[ \|w\|_2^2=9 \]

For:

\[ \lambda=0.5 \]

we obtain:

\[ J(w)=20+0.5(9) \]
\[ \boxed{J(w)=24.5} \]

24. Worked Numerical Example — Comparing Two Parameter Vectors

Suppose:

Model A

\[ L_{data}=10,\quad \|w_A\|_2^2=2 \]

Model B

\[ L_{data}=7,\quad \|w_B\|_2^2=20 \]

Take:

\[ \lambda=0.5 \]

Then:

\[ J_A=10+0.5(2)=11 \]
\[ J_B=7+0.5(20)=17 \]

Although B has the smaller data error, A has the smaller regularized objective.

👨‍🏫 Hinglish
> “B training data ko better fit kar raha tha, lekin weights bahut bade the. Ridge ne us complexity ko expensive bana diya.”

25. Worked Numerical Example — Closed-Form with a Tiny Dataset

Consider a one-feature model written without an intercept for simplicity:

\[ X= \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad y= \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \]

Then:

\[ X^TX=1^2+2^2=5 \]

and:

\[ X^Ty=1(1)+2(2)=5 \]

So Ridge gives:

\[ \hat w= \frac{5}{5+\lambda} \]

For \(\lambda=5\):

\[ \hat w=\frac{5}{10}=0.5 \]

Compare that with OLS:

\[ \hat w_{OLS}=\frac55=1 \]

So Ridge shrinks the coefficient from 1 toward 0.

This is the shrinkage idea in one line.

26. What Happens as \(\lambda\to\infty\)?

From the simplified example:

\[ \hat w=\frac{5}{5+\lambda} \]

As \(\lambda\) becomes very large:

\[ \hat w\to0 \]

This illustrates the general tendency of strong Ridge regularization to force coefficient magnitudes toward zero.

It also explains why excessive regularization can lead to a nearly useless model: the model can lose the ability to represent the data relationship.

27. Ridge vs Polynomial Regression

Suppose you have:

\[ 1,x,x^2,\ldots,x^{18} \]

Polynomial expansion gives the model more flexibility.

Ridge then says:

Keep these expressive features, but discourage large coefficient values.

So these are complementary ideas:

Polynomial expansion
→ increase representation power

Ridge
→ control coefficient magnitude

This is a particularly important connection to your Lecture 10.

28. Common Misconceptions

Misconception 1

“Ridge removes features.”

Not in the usual exact-zero sense. It shrinks coefficients.

Misconception 2

“Ridge chooses the smallest training error.”

No. It chooses the minimum of data fit + penalty.

Misconception 3

“\(\lambda\) should be as large as possible.”

No. Too much regularization can underfit.

Misconception 4

“Ridge and Lasso are interchangeable.”

No. Their penalties and coefficient behaviour differ.

Misconception 5

“Scaling does not matter because the model is still linear.”

Scaling matters for regularization because the penalty acts directly on coefficient magnitudes.

Misconception 6

“Ridge always improves test error.”

No. It can improve generalization in appropriate settings, but the value of \(\lambda\) and the data/model context matter.

29. Mathematical Summary

Ridge objective

\[ \boxed{ J(w)=\|Xw-y\|_2^2+\lambda\|w\|_2^2 } \]

Expanded penalty

\[ \|w\|_2^2=\sum_j w_j^2 \]

Gradient

\[ \boxed{ \nabla_wJ(w)=2X^T(Xw-y)+2\lambda w } \]

Stationary condition

\[ (X^TX+\lambda I)w=X^Ty \]

Closed-form solution

\[ \boxed{ \hat w_{Ridge}=(X^TX+\lambda I)^{-1}X^Ty } \]

when the inverse exists.

Limits

\[ \lambda=0 \rightarrow OLS \]

and increasingly large \(\lambda\) produces stronger shrinkage.
