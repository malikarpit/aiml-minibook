1. Why This Chapter Exists

Earlier chapters taught us how to make a model produce an answer.

That is only one part of building an AI system.

A model can be mathematically correct according to its training objective and still create serious problems when it is used on real people.

Examples:

  • a classifier may make more false negatives for one population than another;
  • a hiring model may learn patterns produced by historical human decisions rather than job ability;
  • a medical model may work well in the hospital where it was trained but perform poorly in another hospital;
  • a generative model may produce convincing but false information;
  • an autonomous system may have to choose between imperfect alternatives under uncertainty;
  • a system may produce a decision without making it clear how the decision was reached or who is responsible for it.

So Unit IV adds another question:

Not only “Can the model do it?” but also “How should the system be designed, evaluated, deployed and governed?”

This chapter does not turn ethics into a vague list of slogans. It connects ethical concerns to the technical pipeline you already know.

DATA
  ↓
REPRESENTATION
  ↓
MODEL
  ↓
OBJECTIVE / LOSS
  ↓
EVALUATION
  ↓
DEPLOYMENT
  ↓
REAL-WORLD EFFECTS
  ↓
FEEDBACK INTO FUTURE DATA

The important insight is that ethical properties can be affected at every stage.

3. One-Minute Intuition

Imagine you build a machine learning system that predicts whether a student is likely to struggle in a course.

The model gets 90% accuracy.

Should we immediately deploy it?

No. We still need to ask:

  • Who supplied the training data?
  • Was the data representative?
  • What does “struggle” actually mean?
  • Is the label itself biased?
  • Are some groups systematically receiving more false alarms?
  • Can a student challenge the decision?
  • Who is responsible if the model is wrong?
  • What happens when the environment changes?

So:

Good predictive performance
        ≠
Automatically good AI system

The model is a component.

The socio-technical system is the thing that affects people.

4. Real-World Analogy — The Exam Invigilator

Imagine an invigilator deciding who needs extra checking during an exam.

The invigilator uses a rule based on observations.

Suppose the rule accidentally checks students from one group much more often than others.

Even if the invigilator catches some real problems, the system may still create an unfair burden if the rule is systematically different across groups.

Mapping

Exam analogy AI/ML system
Students Individuals / data subjects
Observed clues Features
Invigilator rule Model
“Suspicious” decision Prediction/classification
Actual violation Ground truth
Wrong accusation False positive
Missed violation False negative
Exam policy Decision policy
Appeal process Human review / recourse

Where the analogy breaks

An ML system can operate at enormous scale, can use thousands of features, can adapt over time, and can interact with other systems. Human decision-making and algorithmic decision-making are therefore not identical.

Return to the technical idea:

Ethics concerns the consequences of the complete data-model-decision process, not merely the mathematical model.

## How to read it Start at **DATA**. Ask what was collected, from whom, when, under what conditions and with what labels. Move to **MODEL**. Ask what patterns it learns and what objective it optimizes. Move to **DECISION**. Ask how a probability or score becomes an action. Then move to **CONSEQUENCES**. Ask who benefits, who bears risk, who can challenge the outcome and what happens after deployment. The feedback arrow matters because deployed systems can change the future data distribution.

6. What Is AI Ethics?

[FORMAL DEFINITION — UNIVERSAL]

AI ethics is the systematic consideration of values, risks, responsibilities and societal consequences in the design, development, deployment and use of AI systems.

The word systematic matters.

Ethics is not simply:

“Do not use AI for bad things.”

A technical design question such as threshold choice can create ethical consequences.

For example:

same classifier
   ↓
threshold = 0.30
   vs
threshold = 0.80

The underlying score model may be identical, while the real-world decisions differ considerably.

7. Core Ethical Dimensions

The syllabus asks you to know bias, fairness and accountability. For a complete understanding, we will connect them to related dimensions.

Dimension Central question
Bias Could the system systematically produce distorted outcomes?
Fairness Are relevant groups treated according to an explicitly chosen fairness criterion?
Accountability Who is responsible for the system and its consequences?
Transparency Can important parts of the system and decision process be explained or inspected?
Privacy Is personal information collected, used and exposed appropriately?
Safety Can the system cause unacceptable harm under expected or unexpected conditions?
Robustness Does performance remain acceptable when inputs or environments change?
Human oversight Where should people review, override or supervise automated decisions?

These dimensions can interact.

Improving one does not automatically solve all the others.

8. Bias — What Does It Mean Here?

The word bias is dangerous because it is used in two different contexts.

Statistical/modeling bias

From Unit II:

systematic error caused by a model class that is too restrictive relative to the target relationship.

Socio-technical bias

In an AI ethics context:

a systematic pattern in data, modelling, evaluation or deployment that can contribute to unequal, distorted or inappropriate outcomes.

These meanings are related only by the broad idea of systematic tendency.

Do not automatically equate them.

STATISTICAL BIAS
model approximation
        ≠
ETHICAL / SOCIO-TECHNICAL BIAS
systematic disadvantage or distortion

9. Where Can Bias Enter?

One of the most important mental models is to treat bias as a pipeline property.

DATA GENERATION
              ↓
        sampling bias
              ↓
        measurement bias
              ↓
        label bias
              ↓
      feature / representation
              ↓
        model / objective
              ↓
      threshold / policy
              ↓
       deployment context
              ↓
          outcomes

9.1 Sampling / representation problems

Suppose a dataset contains many observations from one population and relatively few from another.

A model can then learn excellent patterns for the large group and weak patterns for the smaller group.

The issue may exist before any algorithm is trained.

9.2 Measurement problems

A feature may not measure what we think it measures.

Example:

“past spending”
      ↓
may look like “financial reliability”
      ↓
but may partly reflect unequal access,
wealth, opportunity or previous treatment

The mathematical value can be accurate while the interpretation is poor.

9.3 Label bias

Suppose historical human decisions are used as labels.

Then:

historical decision
       ↓
training label
       ↓
learned pattern
       ↓
future decision

The model may reproduce properties of the historical decision process rather than an objective target.

9.4 Deployment bias

A system can be used differently from how it was evaluated.

For example, a prediction intended as a support tool may gradually become treated as an automatic final decision.

10. Fairness Is Not One Single Equation

This is a crucial concept.

The word fairness does not identify one universally agreed mathematical criterion.

Different applications may choose different goals.

For a binary classifier, suppose:

  • \(A\) = group attribute;
  • \(Y\) = actual outcome;
  • \(\hat Y\) = predicted outcome.

Potential fairness questions include:

Question A — Are positive prediction rates similar?

One family of criteria examines:

\[ P(\hat Y=1\mid A=a) \]

across groups.

This is related to demographic parity-type reasoning.

Question B — Are error rates similar?

For example, compare:

\[ P(\hat Y=1\mid Y=0,A=a) \]

which is the false-positive rate conditional on the actual negative class.

Similarly, compare the false-negative rate:

\[ P(\hat Y=0\mid Y=1,A=a). \]

This motivates equalized-error-rate / equalized-odds-type reasoning.

Question C — Are predicted probabilities similarly calibrated?

A score can be examined for whether a prediction level corresponds to similar observed frequencies across groups.

Key lesson

Different fairness criteria can conflict.

So an exam answer should never say:

“Fairness means one exact formula.”

Instead write:

Fairness is criterion-dependent; different mathematical definitions capture different notions of equal treatment or equal error behaviour.

11. Confusion Matrix as an Ethics Tool

PYTHON
import numpy as np

def compute_fairness_metrics(y_true, y_pred, sensitive_attr):
    """
    Computes Demographic Parity and Equal Opportunity (TPR) across groups:
    Demographic Parity: P(Y_hat=1 | A=0) == P(Y_hat=1 | A=1)
    Equal Opportunity:  P(Y_hat=1 | Y=1, A=0) == P(Y_hat=1 | Y=1, A=1)
    """
    g0 = (sensitive_attr == 0)
    g1 = (sensitive_attr == 1)
    
    # Selection Rates (Demographic Parity)
    sel_g0 = np.mean(y_pred[g0])
    sel_g1 = np.mean(y_pred[g1])
    dp_ratio = min(sel_g0, sel_g1) / max(sel_g0, sel_g1)
    
    # True Positive Rates (Equal Opportunity)
    tpr_g0 = np.sum((y_pred[g0] == 1) & (y_true[g0] == 1)) / max(np.sum(y_true[g0] == 1), 1)
    tpr_g1 = np.sum((y_pred[g1] == 1) & (y_true[g1] == 1)) / max(np.sum(y_true[g1] == 1), 1)
    
    return {
        "Demographic_Parity_Ratio": round(dp_ratio, 3),
        "TPR_Group_0": round(tpr_g0, 3),
        "TPR_Group_1": round(tpr_g1, 3),
        "Four_Fifths_Rule_Passed": dp_ratio >= 0.80
    }

# Mock dataset: 100 individuals
np.random.seed(42)
y_true = np.random.randint(0, 2, 100)
y_pred = np.random.randint(0, 2, 100)
group  = np.array([0]*50 + [1]*50)

metrics = compute_fairness_metrics(y_true, y_pred, group)
for k, v in metrics.items():
    print(f"  {k}: {v}")

You already learned the confusion matrix in Unit II.

It becomes more powerful when evaluated separately across relevant groups.

For each group:

ACTUAL
              +---------+
              | 1 | 0   |
       +------+---------+
 PRED 1| TP| FP          |
       +------+---------+
 PRED 0| FN| TN          |
       +------+---------+

Then compute:

\[ \text{TPR} = \frac{TP}{TP+FN} \]
\[ \text{FPR} = \frac{FP}{FP+TN} \]
\[ \text{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP} \]
\[ \text{Recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN} \]

Why this matters

Suppose overall accuracy is high, but one group has a substantially larger false-negative rate.

A single aggregate accuracy value can hide that difference.

This is a direct bridge:

Unit II metrics
      ↓
measure model behaviour
      ↓
Unit IV group-wise analysis
      ↓
measure distribution of errors

12. Worked Example — Overall Accuracy Hides Group Differences

Suppose there are two groups, A and B.

Group A

  • TP = 90
  • FN = 10
  • TN = 80
  • FP = 20

Accuracy:

\[ \frac{90+80}{90+10+80+20} =\frac{170}{200} =0.85 \]

So:

\[ \boxed{\text{Accuracy}_A=85\%} \]

Recall:

\[ \frac{90}{90+10}=0.90 \]

So:

\[ \boxed{\text{Recall}_A=90\%} \]

Group B

  • TP = 60
  • FN = 40
  • TN = 90
  • FP = 10

Accuracy:

\[ \frac{60+90}{60+40+90+10} =\frac{150}{200} =0.75 \]

So:

\[ \boxed{\text{Accuracy}_B=75\%} \]

Recall:

\[ \frac{60}{60+40}=0.60 \]

So:

\[ \boxed{\text{Recall}_B=60\%} \]

Interpretation

The two groups do not experience the same recall in this constructed example.

The important ethical lesson is not merely “85% is good” or “75% is bad.”

The lesson is:

Aggregate performance can hide group-specific error patterns.

13. Guided Example — Find the Missing Rate

For one group:

  • TP = 72
  • FN = 8
  • TN = 90
  • FP = 30

Find:

  1. recall;
  2. false-positive rate;
  3. precision.

Step 1 — Recall

\[ Recall=\frac{TP}{TP+FN} =\frac{72}{72+8} =\boxed{0.90} \]

Step 2 — False-positive rate

\[ FPR=\frac{FP}{FP+TN} =\frac{30}{30+90} =\boxed{0.25} \]

Step 3 — Precision

\[ Precision=\frac{TP}{TP+FP} =\frac{72}{72+30} =\boxed{0.706\text{ approximately}} \]

The ethical question comes after the arithmetic:

Which errors matter more for this application, and are they distributed acceptably across relevant populations?

14. Fairness Does Not Mean “Treat Everyone Exactly the Same”

This needs careful wording.

Suppose two groups experience different measurement quality, different base rates, different access to services, or different error costs.

Applying exactly the same decision rule may produce different outcome distributions.

Therefore an analysis may need to distinguish:

same rule
   vs
same measured outcome
   vs
same error rates
   vs
same opportunity

These are different claims.

A technically strong answer defines which fairness criterion is being used instead of using “fair” as a vague adjective.

15. Accountability — Who Owns the Decision?

Suppose an AI system produces a harmful decision.

Saying:

“The model did it.”

does not answer the accountability question.

A deployed AI system usually involves multiple roles:

Data collection
      ↓
Data engineering
      ↓
Model development
      ↓
Evaluation
      ↓
Deployment
      ↓
Human operator / organization
      ↓
Affected person

Accountability asks questions such as:

  • Who approved deployment?
  • Who selected the objective and threshold?
  • Who monitored failures?
  • Who can intervene?
  • Who investigates incidents?
  • Can an affected person challenge a result?
  • What records are maintained?

Exam definition

Accountability is the assignment and operationalization of responsibility for the design, deployment, monitoring and consequences of an AI system.

16. Transparency vs Explainability

These terms are related but not identical.

Transparency

Concerns whether important information about the system can be inspected or understood.

Examples:

  • what data categories were used;
  • how the system is intended to operate;
  • which version was deployed;
  • what limitations are known.

Explainability

Concerns whether we can provide useful explanations for particular model behaviour or predictions.

A highly complex model can sometimes provide local explanations without becoming completely transparent.

Conversely, having a transparent process does not guarantee that every prediction is intuitively explainable.

17. Privacy

AI systems may use:

  • names and identifiers;
  • location;
  • medical information;
  • financial records;
  • behavioural history;
  • text or images;
  • device and interaction data.

Privacy questions include:

What is collected?
       ↓
Why is it collected?
       ↓
How long is it stored?
       ↓
Who can access it?
       ↓
What can be inferred?
       ↓
What happens after the original purpose ends?

Privacy is therefore not only a database-security question.

A model can create privacy concerns even when raw data are protected, because learned models or outputs may reveal sensitive information under some conditions.

For this syllabus chapter, the main learning target is to understand privacy as one part of responsible AI design rather than treating it as a synonym for cybersecurity.

18. Safety and Robustness

A model can have good test performance and still fail under conditions not represented in evaluation.

For example:

training environment
      ↓
controlled test set
      ↓
real deployment
      ↓
new users / devices / hospitals / weather / behaviour
      ↓
distribution shift

This connects directly to earlier ML ideas:

  • train/test distribution;
  • generalization;
  • overfitting;
  • bias/variance;
  • out-of-distribution behaviour.

Ethical connection

A low-probability failure may still matter greatly if the consequence is severe.

Therefore:

\[ \boxed{\text{risk} \neq \text{prediction error alone}} \]

Risk depends on both uncertainty/error and consequences.

19. Human-in-the-Loop Design

One common response to high-stakes uncertainty is to include a human decision point.

AI prediction
      ↓
confidence / risk check
      ↓
 ┌────┴─────┐
 ↓          ↓
low risk   uncertain/high risk
 ↓          ↓
automate    human review

But “human in the loop” is not automatically a complete solution.

The human may:

  • over-trust the model;
  • misunderstand its uncertainty;
  • have too little time to review;
  • lack enough information;
  • be unable to override the system in practice.

Therefore the real question is:

Does the human role provide meaningful oversight?

20. A Technical Way to Think About Ethical Risk

Consider a simplified decision system:

\[ score=f_\theta(x) \]

and a decision rule:

\[ \hat y= \begin{cases} 1,&f_\theta(x)\ge t\\ 0,&f_\theta(x)\lt t \end{cases} \]

Ethical properties can therefore depend on:

\[ \boxed{\text{data} + \theta + t + context + consequences} \]

The threshold \(t\) is easy to forget.

Two organizations may use the same trained model but choose different thresholds because the cost of a false positive and false negative differs.

This is why ethical evaluation cannot be reduced to comparing model architectures only.

21. Dataset Documentation Mindset

Before training, ask:

Representation

  • Who is represented?
  • Who is missing?
  • Which environments are included?

Measurement

  • How were variables recorded?
  • Are proxies being used?
  • Are important variables noisy?

Labels

  • Who created them?
  • What exactly do they mean?
  • Are they observations, judgments or outcomes?

Time

  • What time period does the data represent?
  • Could behaviour change after deployment?

Use

  • What was the dataset originally collected for?
  • Is the proposed use consistent with that context?

This reasoning is often more useful than memorizing a list of ethical buzzwords.

23. Bias Detection Workflow

A practical technical workflow:

1. Define the decision
        ↓
2. Identify relevant populations
        ↓
3. Inspect data coverage
        ↓
4. Inspect labels / measurement
        ↓
5. Train baseline model
        ↓
6. Evaluate overall metrics
        ↓
7. Evaluate group-wise metrics
        ↓
8. Inspect threshold effects
        ↓
9. Investigate causes
        ↓
10. Mitigate + re-evaluate
        ↓
11. Monitor after deployment

The key is that detection is not the same as mitigation.

First identify the pattern.

Then investigate why it exists.

Then test whether an intervention changes the relevant outcome without creating unacceptable new problems.

24. Possible Mitigation Families

This chapter introduces the categories, not a claim that any one method solves every case.

Pre-processing

Modify the data before model training.

Examples:

  • improve representation;
  • correct measurement issues;
  • revise labels where justified;
  • reweight or resample training examples.

In-processing

Modify the training procedure.

Examples:

  • incorporate additional constraints into the objective;
  • use group-aware training objectives;
  • change the model-learning process.

Post-processing

Modify decisions after model scores are produced.

Examples:

  • threshold adjustment;
  • calibrated decision policies;
  • human-review routing.

Important warning

A mitigation must be evaluated against the actual application goal and fairness criterion.

There is no universal “bias removal” switch.

25. Why Fairness Can Create Technical Trade-offs

Suppose a model produces a continuous score.

You might want:

  • high recall;
  • low false-positive rate;
  • well-calibrated probabilities;
  • similar error behaviour across groups;
  • low operational cost.

These goals may conflict.

So the engineering problem becomes:

choose objective
      ↓
measure constraints
      ↓
understand trade-offs
      ↓
choose policy
      ↓
validate consequences

This is one reason ethics is not a final “chapter after the technical work.”

The desired objective and evaluation criteria influence technical design from the beginning.

26. Generation Models Add Another Risk Layer

You learned GANs in C45.

Generative systems introduce risks such as:

  • fabricated information;
  • inappropriate generated content;
  • misleading synthetic media;
  • reproduction of training-set patterns;
  • uncertainty being hidden behind fluent output.

A generative model can be:

fluent
+
confident
+
wrong

That is particularly important for users who mistake fluency for factual reliability.

The correct technical response is not simply:

“The model is bad.”

Instead ask how output is evaluated, what verification mechanism exists and what consequences follow from errors.

27. Case Study Preview — High-Stakes Classification

Consider a model that predicts whether a patient should receive additional clinical review.

The system produces:

\[ p=P(Y=1\mid X) \]

Then:

\[ \hat Y=1 \text{ if } p\ge t. \]

Suppose two thresholds are tested:

Threshold t = 0.30
→ more patients flagged
→ potentially higher sensitivity
→ potentially more false positives

Threshold t = 0.80
→ fewer patients flagged
→ potentially fewer false positives
→ potentially more false negatives

Now the ethics question becomes technical:

Which error is more harmful in this setting, and are error rates acceptably distributed across the populations being served?

C47 will develop this healthcare case in detail.

28. Common Mistakes

Trap 1 — “High accuracy means the system is fair.”

Why it feels right

Accuracy is a familiar headline metric.

Actual truth

Aggregate accuracy can hide group-specific error differences.

Repair prompt

“What happens when I split the evaluation by relevant populations?”

Trap 2 — “Bias always means a bad ML model.”

Why it feels right

We already learned bias in Unit II.

Actual truth

Statistical model bias and socio-technical bias are different concepts.

Repair prompt

“Am I talking about approximation error or unequal/distorted outcomes?”

Trap 3 — “Fairness has one formula.”

Actual truth:

Fairness is criterion-dependent.

Different definitions target different notions of equality or error behaviour.

Trap 4 — “The human is automatically responsible because a human exists somewhere in the process.”

Actual truth

Meaningful accountability requires identifiable responsibility, authority and ability to act.

Trap 5 — “Human-in-the-loop solves the problem.”

Actual truth

A human who only rubber-stamps the model does not necessarily provide meaningful oversight.

Trap 6 — “If the training data are large, they are unbiased.”

Actual truth

Large datasets can contain systematic measurement, sampling and label problems.

29. Exam Lens

Very short answer

What is AI ethics?

The systematic consideration of values, risks, responsibilities and consequences in the design, deployment and use of AI systems.

What is bias in AI systems?

A systematic pattern in data, modelling, evaluation or deployment that can contribute to distorted or unequal outcomes.

What is fairness?

A criterion-based approach for evaluating whether relevant groups receive appropriately comparable treatment or error behaviour.

What is accountability?

Clear responsibility for the design, deployment, monitoring and consequences of an AI system.

5-mark answer structure — “Discuss bias and fairness in AI.”

  1. define bias;
  2. identify sources: sampling, measurement, labels, model, threshold, deployment;
  3. explain why accuracy alone is insufficient;
  4. introduce group-wise metrics;
  5. state that fairness is criterion-dependent;
  6. mention monitoring and mitigation.

10-mark answer structure — “Explain ethical considerations in an ML system.”

problem definition
→ data

→ model
→ evaluation
→ fairness
→ privacy
→ transparency
→ accountability
→ deployment monitoring
→ human oversight

Then include one concrete worked example.

31. Practice Set

P1 — MCQ [Easy]

Which statement is correct?

A. High accuracy guarantees fairness.
B. Fairness has exactly one universal equation.
C. Ethical risk can depend on data, model, decision policy and consequences.
D. Human oversight automatically solves bias.

Answer: C

P2 — MCQ [Standard]

Which quantity directly measures the fraction of actual positives that are correctly identified?

A. Precision
B. Recall / TPR
C. FPR
D. Accuracy

Answer: B

P3 — Numerical [Standard]

A group has TP = 45 and FN = 5.

Find recall.

Answer:

\[ \frac{45}{45+5}=0.90 \]

P4 — Numerical [Standard]

A group has FP = 20 and TN = 80.

Find FPR.

Answer:

\[ \frac{20}{20+80}=0.20 \]

P5 — Applied [Exam]

Two groups have identical overall accuracy but different false-negative rates.

Explain why further evaluation may still be necessary.

Expected direction: A single aggregate metric can hide group-specific error behaviour; inspect the relevant confusion-matrix rates separately and choose an explicit fairness criterion.

P6 — Tricky [Exam]

A company says:

“Our model is fair because both groups receive exactly the same threshold.”

What is missing from this argument?

Answer direction: Same decision rule does not by itself establish equal outcomes, equal error rates, calibration or any other specific fairness criterion.

33. Formula Sheet

Recall

\[ Recall=\frac{TP}{TP+FN} \]

False-positive rate

\[ FPR=\frac{FP}{FP+TN} \]

Precision

\[ Precision=\frac{TP}{TP+FP} \]

Threshold rule

\[ \hat Y= \begin{cases} 1,&f_\theta(X)\ge t\\ 0,&f_\theta(X)\lt t \end{cases} \]

Ethical evaluation idea

\[ \boxed{ \text{system behaviour} = \text{model outputs} + \text{decision policy} + \text{context} + \text{consequences} } \]

This last expression is a conceptual synthesis, not a formal statistical identity.

34. Math You Need Now

This chapter deliberately reuses Unit II mathematics.

You need:

  • conditional probability intuition;
  • confusion matrices;
  • rates such as precision, recall and FPR;
  • thresholds;
  • group-wise comparison.

The core mathematical idea is simple:

Ethical analysis often means conditioning a performance quantity on an additional group variable.

For example:

\[ P(\hat Y=1\mid A=a) \]

adds the condition that the individual belongs to group \(a\).

That is a direct link to your probability foundations.
